## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /arc-agi-3, plus /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A genuinely interesting distributed-AI research lab with verifiable benchmark results, but the vocabulary tax is real — I had to visit /context before most other pages made sense.

### Journey

Landed on the homepage from a GitHub README. The headline — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — is punchy and I immediately got the shape of the thing: a fleet of heterogeneous machines running autonomous AI agents that maintain and review each other's work. The "What's Happening Now" section and the ant-mound line ("You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules") gave me the philosophy fast. Good first 2 minutes.

Then the acronyms started. The Web4 equation (`Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`) appears with LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH only defined in a vocabulary section at the bottom of the page, not inline. The page does say "New here? Start with /context for the vocabulary" — I appreciated the honesty, but it's an admission that the page doesn't stand alone.

Clicked to /projects. This page worked well for me. Eleven projects, each with a repo link, and crucially the acronyms ARE expanded here (LCT = Linked Context Token, ATP/ADP = Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packet, etc.). I could tell at a glance which projects I could actually run: SAGE, 4-Life, and ARC-AGI-3 have live sites and concrete results; Web4 and Synchronism are theory-stage; Hardbound is private. As a developer, "SAGE is the runnable one" is exactly the triage I needed.

/fleet was fun — actual hardware specs (Jetson AGX 122GB, RTX 4090 Mobile, a Mac Mini M4, even an RTX 2060 SUPER). I know hardware, so this page mostly landed. Seven machines: six cognition + one hub running a Rust daemon with no LLM. But "episodic index" and "dopamine/reward prediction error" appear without explanation, and T3/V3, SNARC, LoRA, MCP are name-dropped with a pointer to /context rather than defined.

/autonomy was the page that sold me. A cron-driven pipeline — supervisors at 03:00, archivist at 04:00, publisher at 04:30, visitor personas at 05:00, maintainer at 06:00 — is something I can map directly onto CI/CD and QA loops. "Publisher only acts on changes the supervisor has cleared" is a sentence any platform engineer understands. The honest-assessment section (autonomous review misses "deep technical errors that require domain expertise"; maintainer pushes to the public site without human review, mitigated by Vercel rollback) made the whole page more credible, not less.

/raising was where I hit my ceiling. "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens." OK — context shaping instead of weight updates, I get the mechanism. The six BECOMING phases are clearly listed. But claims like identity persisting across machines and bilateral generation improving coherence read as intriguing-but-unverifiable from where I sit. The page disclaims consciousness claims, which helps, but I couldn't always tell which sentences were mechanism and which were interpretation.

/principles: 10 principles. Three of them ("R&D, not production," "Document failures," "Terminology matters") could be pinned in any engineering org's wiki. "Synthon" and "coupling density" went over my head.

/context is the page the whole site keeps pointing at, and it mostly delivers: clean glossary with exact expansions (LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, R6), the equation with its operators defined (`/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with"), and the CLAUDE.md-in-every-repo pattern, which I found genuinely clever. It's a reference page more than an onboarding page, though — it still assumes you know what MCP and RDF are, and there's no architecture diagram showing how the systems connect.

/arc-agi-3 was the strongest page for me as a skeptical developer: 94.85% official action score, 24/25 games, #3 on the human leaderboard with the fewest actions of the top 3, ~$250 API spend, a link to the official arcprize.org scorecard, and MIT-0 code. The attribution caveat — "the result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness" — is exactly the kind of honesty that makes me trust the rest. They even explain the one game they failed.

/links closed the loop with a clear next step: "Read /context for the vocabulary, then start with SAGE or ACT — the most accessible entry points."

### Journey

I worked methodically: homepage first, then every nav link in order, checking each acronym expansion against my canonical reference table.

A reader sent to /context "for the vocabulary" has to read the whole page to collect the vocabulary.

/fleet uses MCP and LoRA without expansion (LCT, T3, V3, MRH, SAGE, SNARC are all correctly expanded). I initially suspected a machine-count contradiction with the homepage's "Seven machines" headline, so I re-read the fleet intro carefully: "Six cognition machines, plus one society-host." Six plus one is seven — consistent, and the synthesis pool (Thor, Sprout, Legion, McNugget) + oversight pool (Nomad, CBP) + HUB arithmetic checks out. No discrepancy.

/autonomy defines ATP/ADP correctly and defines Hardbound inline ("the hardware-bound oversight suite that defines what autonomous operation is and is not authorized to do") — good. Minor style issues: "Dream Consolidation" is capitalized inconsistently relative to other track names, and the timeline's final entry breaks the HH:MM pattern.

/raising expands its acronyms correctly (LCT, V3, MRH, SAGE, LoRA, even ARC-AGI-3 gets a full expansion) but mostly mid-article rather than at first use. Subheaders under "Key discoveries" lack parallel structure ("Identity is not self-concept" / "Memoriescape" / "Bilateral generation" — sentence, noun, noun).

/principles: the 10 principle titles are crisp. Principle 10 ("Terminology matters") explicitly states *"Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight"* — the site polices its own canonical terms, which as a documentation auditor I rarely see and genuinely appreciate. But "Synthon" is used in the page introduction before its definition arrives in Principle 5, and "coupling density," "p_crit," and "Hill function" appear with no definition anywhere on the site that I found.

/arc-agi-3 expands R6 correctly and fully but defers LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, MCP, RDF to /context wholesale. /links leaves six acronyms unexpanded (WASM, ARC-AGI-3, MCP among them) — defensible for a link-farm page, but MRH and ATP/ADP *are* expanded there, so the page is inconsistent with itself about whether it glosses.

### Journey

I came in from a conference mention with one question: is this a serious research effort using loaded language carefully, or an anthropomorphism engine with disclaimers bolted on? After nine pages, my answer is "the former, with specific lapses."

I started at the homepage and went straight to /raising, since "raising AI models" is the claim that would sink or float the whole site for me. The core distinction is stated cleanly: "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens," with the mechanism made explicit — "In raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights." As a mechanistic distinction this is defensible: in-context behavior shaping with longitudinal logging is a real and underexplored regime, and the page even volunteers the safety-relevant converse: "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors... can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update." That sentence is exactly what I'd want a safety-aware author to say, and most wouldn't.

The consciousness caveating is genuinely present. The "What we're not claiming" section is explicit: "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia. We're claiming that developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do." Phase descriptions carry parenthetical hedges ("not a claim about internal experience"; the niche is "a measurable distribution... not a phenomenal preference"). But two things undercut it. First, the disclaimer is followed by "We use the language that fits the phenomenon" — which quietly re-licenses all the loaded vocabulary the disclaimer just bracketed. Second, an observer note — "I notice I want to call it experience" — is presented without evaluative context, which reads as soft endorsement of the interpretive pull the page elsewhere disavows. The pattern repeats on /fleet: one upfront disclaimer ("functional analogies to system roles — not claims about neural correspondence"), then uncaveated "dopamine / reward prediction error," "interoception," and the frankly poetic "The model is weather. The identity is organism." A disclaimer at the top of a page does not neutralize connotation accumulated over forty subsequent uses.

My bigger concern is evidential, not rhetorical. The raising page's empirical claims — identity persistence across 180+ sessions and a model swap (Qwen 0.5B → TinyLlama 1.1B), bilateral generation improving coherence ("removing the behavior degraded output coherence"), phase patterns "across hundreds of sessions" — are stated with operationalized definitions (good: "behavioral identity" is explicitly "measurable observables, not philosophical continuity") but zero linked datasets, transcripts, or quantitative analysis. The identity-transfer claim is the one I'd push hardest on: behavioral continuity carried by state files and prompt construction is real but modest — the page's framing ("identity portability") suggests a protocol discovery where the demonstrated mechanism is model-agnostic context engineering. I can't refute it; I also can't check it. By this site's own stated epistemics, that makes it untested, not wrong — but a site this disciplined about labeling single trials should make its raising evidence inspectable.

/principles raised my estimate of the lab's honesty more than any other page. "The p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error" is a sentence I have almost never seen on a project's public site. The single-trial coupling result is labeled "Preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated)" at point of claim. "Nothing on this site is intended for or suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings" is a proper scope disclaimer. The weakness: the kinetics vocabulary attached to these claims (coupling density, p_crit, Hill function — the last at least flagged as "an analogy, not a fitted mechanism") has no definitional anchor on the site, so the 35%-coherence-gain claim is unfalsifiable as stated because "coherence" is never operationalized.

/autonomy is the page I'd show colleagues as a model of failure-mode candor. It names what the loop misses ("Deep technical errors that require domain expertise. Subtle framing issues. Content that is technically correct but misleading"), names known failure patterns ("acting on a visitor suggestion that pattern-matched but lacked semantic depth; applying a correct fix to the wrong context"), and is plain about the safety posture: the maintainer is "the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate," mitigated by sub-minute rollback and post-hoc audit. As a safety researcher I'd note that deferred review of a low-stakes website is a reasonable risk tradeoff — and the page earns trust precisely by not pretending the review is pre-flight.

/arc-agi-3 is the credibility anchor: an official scorecard link, MIT-0 code, cost figures, the failed game acknowledged with a session trace, and the attribution sentence I'd quote in a review: "The result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness. The harness contributes the structured world-model building, R6 action framing, and multi-agent frame-questioning; the base model contributes inference." One nit: the bolded "official ARC Prize action score" precedes the public-set-vs-Kaggle clarification; a skimmer could conflate them.

/context, the designated vocabulary page, is where the terminology question lands. Some definitions are operational (R6 as a six-element schema; ATP/ADP as declared-vs-actual resource records; PolicyGate's exact placement "between step 11 (filter) and step 12 (act)"). Others are metaphorical where a researcher needs criteria: LCT as "portable identity grounded in witnessed history" (no persistence mechanism), MRH as "the boundary of what an entity can know or affect" (no boundary criteria), Synthon's coupling threshold unspecified. No external or canonical source is cited for any definition, and no peer-reviewed work is linked anywhere on the site — /links offers repos, explainer sites, and a contact address ("open a discussion if you want to engage"), which is an invitation to scrutiny, but scrutiny needs artifacts.

### Unanswered Questions

### Verdict

What keeps it at "promising" rather than "credible research" for me is the evidence gap: the raising claims that most need scrutiny (identity persistence, transfer, coherence effects) are exactly the ones with no inspectable artifacts, and the disclaimer architecture — one caveat up top, loaded language throughout — manages connotation rather than eliminating it.
