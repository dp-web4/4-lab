## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understand what this lab *is* within two minutes, but it took me four page-hops to find a `git clone` — the site explains itself far better than it onboards me.

### Journey

I landed from a repo README knowing nothing about Web4. The headline ("Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop.") plus the sub-paragraph did their job — I had a working mental model immediately: a fleet of heterogeneous machines running autonomous agents that maintain each other's work. The stat strip (7 machines / 14 repos / 31+ tracks) is concrete in a way most research-lab sites aren't, and I appreciated that the consciousness disclaimer is *above the fold* rather than buried. That bought a lot of goodwill before I'd read anything technical.

The Vocabulary Primer at the bottom of the home page is the single best thing here for someone like me. Every acronym I'd hit later — LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, SAGE, SNARC, MCP, RDF — is expanded right there. Critically, the Web4 equation comes with an operator legend that explicitly flags its own ambiguity: `/` means "verified by" in T3/V3 but a declared→discharged pair in ATP/ADP, "same glyph, unrelated semantics." I fully expected to bounce off that equation as pseudo-math, and instead the page pre-empted exactly the objection I was forming. That's unusually good technical writing.

Then I tried to actually *do* something, and the wheels came off a bit. Home's "Getting Started" told me to begin at /context for vocabulary. I went there, read the glossary (good, consistent with home), and /context told me "Quick-start commands are on /links." So I went to /links — the last nav item, named like an external-link dump — and there, finally, were the `git clone` commands for SAGE and ACT. Home → /context → /links is three hops to reach two lines of shell. As a developer, that's backwards: the vocabulary is the thing I'd have been happy to skip, and the clone command is the thing I came for.

/projects is a clean scan: eleven cards, each with public/private and a status label. But the status labels are where I stalled. Four projects are marked "runnable" (SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, Membot) and the rest "research-stage" — and neither term is defined anywhere. Does "runnable" mean there's a demo, a `main()`, a Docker image, or just that the code executes? That's the exact distinction that decides whether I spend my evening on it, and I have to guess.

/fleet was the most fun page and the one where I hit the most undefined jargon. Seven machines with real hardware and real model names, each assigned a brain-component role. But Sprout's headline achievement is "zero crystallization," Nomad's role is "crystallization detector" — and crystallization is never defined on the page. From context it's clearly a failure mode you don't want, but I'm reverse-engineering a core concept from two role labels. Same for "habit compiler." Also, the per-machine session counts (91 + 115 + 25 + 97 + 120 + 87) sum to ~535, while the home page and /projects both say "900+ raising sessions." I assume the 900+ is cumulative or historical and the fleet numbers are current-phase, but as someone who instinctively adds the column, the mismatch reads as an error.

/autonomy and /principles I read quickly and liked. The autonomy page naming its own worst component ("the maintainer track represents the higher blast radius component — it autonomously commits to the live public site without pre-human approval") is the kind of thing nobody writes unless they mean it. Principle 6 is literally "Document failures" and quotes a 400x error in their own derivation.

/arc-agi-3 is the page that would make me actually clone the repo. A real number (94.85% action score), a real cost ($250 of API spend), a real leaderboard position — and then, unprompted, "the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run." That was the exact question forming in my head as I read the score (isn't this just Opus 4.6 being good?), and they asked it before I could. It doesn't answer it, but naming the gap is worth more to me than a hedge would be.

What I never got: a diagram. The SAGE 12-step loop is given as a twelve-item text arrow chain (sense → salience → metabolize → posture → select → budget → execute → learn → remember → oversee → filter → act) on three separate pages. Twelve steps in prose is past what I can hold; this is a picture, and there isn't one anywhere on the site.

### Verdict

This is one of the more legible research-lab sites I've landed on: the value proposition is clear inside two minutes, every acronym is expanded on the home page, and the honest caveats (the un-run ARC ablation, the 400x derivation error, the maintainer's blast radius) read as genuinely honest rather than performed. I came in expecting the Web4 equation to be the bounce point and instead found it explained better than most API docs explain their own types.

The gap is onboarding, not explanation. The site is built to be *understood* and not to be *entered* — my `git clone` was three hops away behind a glossary, "runnable" is never defined, and there's no diagram of the 12-step loop that every page keeps describing in prose. Fixing the path from landing page to first command would convert more readers like me from "that was interesting" into "I ran it."

Would I return? Yes — and I'd start at /arc-agi-3, because a real score with a real cost and an admitted missing ablation is the most credible thing on the site.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Canonical terminology is correct on every page that expands it — the defect is not drift but *distribution*: definitions are concentrated on / and /context, and the leaf pages a reader is most likely to land on directly use the same terms bare.

### Journey

I walked all nine pages in nav order and checked every acronym expansion against the canonical table, word by word. I want to lead with the headline because it is unusual: **I found zero canonical drift.** Not one instance. LCT is "Linked Context Token" on all four pages that expand it — never "Lifelong Capability." T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6 are correct everywhere. Web4 is an ontology on every page that characterizes it, and /context says the quiet part explicitly: "not architecture or infrastructure." Hardbound is an oversight suite everywhere. "Production ready" does not appear on the site; /principles has an entire principle titled "R&D, not production." Sites this consistent are rare and I check a lot of them.

The thing I expected to be the finding turned out to be handled better than I would have handled it. SAGE expands to "Situation-Aware **Governance** Engine" — and the site's own Principle 10 says Hardbound is oversight, *not* governance. That is exactly the kind of contradiction that normally sits in a doc set for years. Instead, /principles carries an inline note ("'Governance' in SAGE's name predates Principle 10's governance→oversight correction — see /context") and /context carries the full rationale: the vocabulary was corrected lab-wide, but "names minted before the correction are retained where they are load-bearing — 'Governance' in SAGE's name (kept across code, papers, and the ARC Prize benchmark)." That is the correct call and the correct disclosure. A renamed acronym would have orphaned the benchmark submission. Flagging a term you *deliberately* didn't fix, with the reason, is better documentation than silently fixing it.

I also have to file two corrections against Pass 1, because I checked the same claims and they don't hold. Pass 1 reports "crystallization" as never defined on /fleet — it is: Sprout's card reads "Zero crystallization achieved (S100) — no fixed-point collapse, the failure mode where an agent settles into repeating the same responses and exploration stops." Pass 1 reports `p_crit` and "Hill function kinetics" as unexpanded on /principles — both carry inline glosses ("*p_crit = the critical coherence threshold derived in the Synchronism framework*"; "Hill function kinetics (the cooperative binding model from enzyme chemistry) — an analogy, not a fitted mechanism"). All three also have glossary entries on /context. I suspect Pass 1's page fetches summarized those glosses away. The maintainer should not act on those three items.

Now the real finding. Expansion coverage is not uniform, and it fails precisely where it matters most. On / and /context, every term is expanded — /context's glossary runs 28 entries and is genuinely excellent, including entries most labs would never write down ("Coherence — used in three related senses on this site... with no single operational definition yet"). But **/links and /arc-agi-3 use MRH, LCT, MCP, RDF, T3, V3, R6, and PolicyGate bare**, with the expansions living a page away. Those are not interior pages. /links is where a repo README points; /arc-agi-3 is, by Pass 1's own account, the page that earns the clone. In web documentation every page is a landing page — there is no "first use" in a document a reader enters sideways. The fix is not to re-expand everything inline; it is a hover-gloss or a per-page "terms used here" strip that resolves to /context.

The second real finding is subtler and I think it's the most interesting thing I found. The Web4 equation ships with an operator legend: `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with". But the operators are not used consistently with their own legend. The `/` in `T3/V3` means "verified by"; the `/` in `ATP/ADP` does not — home describes ATP as declared-before and ADP as recorded-after, a state transition, not a verification. Home does flag this overload honestly. But then /projects renders the equation back into prose as: "T3 verified by V3, contextualized by MRH, **anchored to** LCT — with resources **tracked via** ATP and ADP, **augmented with** MCP transport and RDF representation." Three of those four verbs correspond to `+` in the equation, and each one is different. A reader doing what I did — mapping the prose walkthrough back onto the glyphs to learn the operator semantics — will derive that `+` means "anchored to" or "tracked via" and then mis-read the equation. The legend and the prose gloss teach different things about the same symbol.

Third: **"metabolic state" / "metabolize" is missing from the glossary.** It appears as a SAGE loop step on /arc-agi-3 ("sense → salience → **metabolize** → posture → ..."), in Sprout's router description on /fleet, in the SAGE blurb on /links ("12-step cognition loop, metabolic states, experience buffer"), and as "MetabolicBlock" — a capitalized, code-shaped term — in Nomad's role on /fleet. Four use sites, three pages, zero definitions, and it is absent from a 28-entry glossary that found room for "Cartridge." Same for the loop steps "posture" and "budget." This is the one place where the site's otherwise-disciplined vocabulary genuinely leaks.

Finally, /context describes SAGE as "a continuous 12-step loop" and references steps by name in the PolicyGate entry ("between SAGE filter and act steps") — but never enumerates the twelve steps. The full chain appears on /arc-agi-3 instead. The vocabulary page should be where the vocabulary lives; a reader on /context is told about the "filter" and "act" steps with no list to place them in.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /raising, /principles, /autonomy, /arc-agi-3, /context, /projects, /fleet, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The site is candid to an unusual degree about *what it has not measured* — and nearly silent about *who checks*; every verification loop it describes is run by the system being verified.

### Journey

I came in expecting the standard failure mode of this genre: developmental vocabulary doing unearned philosophical work, a benchmark number carrying more weight than it can bear, and a "limitations" section written to be skimmed past. I found something better than that, and then I found two things that are worse than the site seems to realize.

Start with the good, because it is genuinely good. The consciousness disclaimer is above the fold on the landing page and it is *correctly scoped*: developmental language is "functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience." /raising repeats it — "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" — and then, crucially, does the thing almost nobody does: it defines its terms operationally. Identity is "consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns measured via raising curriculum state and interaction logs." Growth is "increasing response diversity and phase-appropriate task success rates." Those are observables. A disclaimer plus an operationalization is a real epistemic commitment; a disclaimer alone is a liability shield. This site does both.

Then /raising states its own null hypothesis, unprompted, in the deflationary form a hostile reviewer would use: "everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more." It goes further and gives the deflationary reading of each individual pattern — bilateral dialogue as "continuation sampling past the response boundary," specialization as "task routing plus few-shot clustering," identity persistence as "mechanical consequence of carrying the same context files." Then: "the comparison has not been run. No deflationary control exists yet." And it specifies the control it owes — a scramble test, permuting session order and cross-session attribution while holding corpus and token volume fixed, against a generic-context-of-equal-size baseline, with a pre-registered threshold for what counts as degradation. It says outright that until that runs, the framework is "a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding."

I review papers that do less than that. I want to state plainly that on the two dimensions I came to evaluate — are consciousness-adjacent claims caveated, and is "raising not training" defensible — the answer is yes, and yes-as-a-hypothesis-with-a-named-falsifier. That is the correct posture.

**But the scramble test does not discriminate its own null, and that is a substantive methodological problem, not a nitpick.** The null is "competent context engineering." In-context learning is *known* to be order-sensitive — example ordering, recency and primacy effects, and permutation sensitivity are among the most robustly replicated findings in the ICL literature. So "competent context engineering" *also* predicts that scrambling session order degrades behavior. If the scramble test fires, both hypotheses are confirmed and neither is eliminated. The test as specified is under-powered against the very alternative it was designed to rule out. The generic-equal-size baseline is the stronger half of the battery — it controls for volume — but it controls for volume, not for order-sensitivity-without-development. What would actually discriminate is a prediction that plain ICL does *not* make: held-out generalization (does raised context confer advantage on tasks absent from the raising curriculum?), or monotonicity (does performance track session count in a way that shuffled-but-equal-volume context cannot reproduce?). The lab has clearly thought hard here; this is a place where thinking a little harder would materially strengthen the claim, and I'd rather they hear it now than after they run the wrong experiment.

**The second problem is an attribution slip, and it is the one I would raise first.** /raising lists six developmental patterns and gives the sixth — "Acting: hypothesis-testing in novel environments" — a piece of evidence: "demonstrated via ARC-AGI-3 benchmark: 94.85% action score." But /fleet says what actually produced that number: CBP "orchestrated official ARC Prize run (94.85% score using **Claude Opus 4.6**)." And /arc-agi-3 confirms it — Claude Opus 4.6, with network access, ~$250 of API spend. The raised entities on this site are 0.5B–14B local models: Qwen, TinyLlama, Gemma, Phi. The entity that scored 94.85% is a frontier cloud model running inside a harness. Those are not the same system, and the sixth developmental pattern is being evidenced by a result that the raising process did not produce.

I want to be careful about what I am and am not alleging. I do not think this is deliberate — the fleet page and the ARC page both disclose the model, plainly, and /arc-agi-3 volunteers that the harness-vs-base-model ablation "has not been run" and that the loop's contribution is "a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated result." That is the same honesty I praised above. The failure is *compositional*: each page is honest, and the cross-page inference a reader assembles is not. A researcher who reads /raising and takes pattern 6 at face value walks away believing that a raised entity demonstrated hypothesis-testing at 94.85% — and the site never says otherwise on the page where the claim is made. There *is* a local-model ARC story on this site (Legion: "first canonical 25-game sweep with local vision model"; the home page's "local models solving unseen ARC-AGI-3 levels"). That is the result that belongs under pattern 6. The Opus number belongs on /arc-agi-3, where it already lives with its caveats intact.

**Third: I think the blast-radius ranking is inverted, and the site's own numbers say so.** /autonomy names the Maintainer as "the higher blast radius component" because it "autonomously commits and pushes to the live public site without pre-human approval." It then discloses the oversight posture with real candor — detect-and-revert, not preventive; rollback under one minute; detection interval ~24 hours; and, remarkably, "for content that is technically correct but misleading, the exposure window is unbounded in principle." I have rarely seen a lab write down its own unbounded-exposure case.

But run the reasoning: a webpage that can be reverted in under 60 seconds from deployment history is, by that very metric, the *low* blast-radius surface. Meanwhile the Publisher track at 04:30 "pushes validated changes to public repos" — autonomously, and the site never says validated by whom or against what. Code pushed to a public repo can be cloned, forked, vendored, and mirrored. There is no revert for that. A bad commit to a repo has a half-life measured in whoever pulled it; a bad paragraph on a website has a half-life of one Visitor cycle. The site has correctly identified that it has an oversight gap and then, I think, pointed at the wrong hole. At minimum, Publisher deserves the same blast-radius paragraph Maintainer got.

**Fourth, and this is the one that matters most for my audience: every verification loop described on this site is internal to the system being verified.** Sprout's headline achievement is "zero crystallization" — evaluated by Nomad, whose role is "crystallization evaluator," on the same fleet, under the same raising process. The public site is audited by the Visitor track and fixed by the Maintainer track — both autonomous, both fleet-run. /autonomy says human review "happens asynchronously with no committed cadence." So: the system generates the entities, evaluates the entities for the failure mode, audits its own public representation, and repairs it — and the only external check is an unscheduled human glance.

I am not saying this is wrong. For an R&D fleet it may be exactly right, and the lab is explicit that nothing here is for safety-critical use (Principle 3 is unusually direct: "not suitable for safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings"). What I am saying is that a trust researcher reads this site and the single largest unanswered question is *who checks* — and the site, which answers so many hard questions unprompted, does not answer that one. It has epistemic humility about its results and structural silence about the independence of its verification. Given that the lab's own subject matter is trust and oversight, that gap is load-bearing in a way it wouldn't be for another lab. Naming it — even to say "there is no external check yet, and here is why we think that's acceptable at this stage" — would be more valuable than any additional result.

Two smaller things a researcher notices. **"Zero crystallization" is a null result reported as a headline achievement.** No detection threshold is given, no base rate (how often does an *un*-raised model of this size crystallize?), and the detector lives in the same fleet. The absence of an effect, measured by an instrument of unstated sensitivity, is close to zero evidence. And **Principle 5 rests on a term the site's own glossary says it cannot define.** Synthon is "emergent coherence entity sustained by recursive interaction"; /context's entry for Coherence says it is used in three senses "with no single operational definition yet." So one of ten stated principles is defined in terms of an admittedly undefined quantity. Both halves of that are on the site, honestly written; nobody has joined them. I'd rather the site say so itself than leave a reviewer to assemble it.

On vocabulary: the canonical Web4 terms are correct — I checked them against the table and I make it four passes, four independent confirmations, zero drift. The terms that would mislead *my* audience are different ones, and they cluster around borrowed physics. "Attractors," "basins," "probability landscape" are dynamical-systems terms used without a state space, a dynamics, or a metric. "Interactive selection" (Principle 7) collides head-on with "selection" as ML uses it — selection pressure over a population with a fitness criterion — and neither a population nor a fitness function is defined here. "Synthon" is a chemistry term (Corey, retrosynthesis) reused for something unrelated. None of these are drift; all of them are places where a researcher's prior shifts toward "physics envy" — which is a shame, because the parts of this site that *are* rigorous are more rigorous than the parts that are borrowing rigor.

### Unanswered Questions

1. **Who checks?** Crystallization is evaluated by the fleet, the site is audited by the fleet, the site is repaired by the fleet, and human review is "asynchronous with no committed cadence." Is there *any* external or blinded check anywhere in this system? For a lab studying trust and oversight, this is the question.
2. Which entity earned the 94.85%? /fleet and /arc-agi-3 say Claude Opus 4.6. /raising cites the same number as evidence of a developmental pattern in entities that are 0.5B–14B local models. These cannot both be the intended reading.
3. Why is Maintainer (revertible in <60s) the named highest-blast-radius track, while Publisher (autonomous pushes to public, cloneable repos) gets no analysis at all? And what does "validated changes" mean in Publisher's description — validated by whom, against what?
4. If the scramble test degrades behavior, what has been learned? In-context learning is order-sensitive, so the deflationary hypothesis predicts that too. What result would *distinguish* development from competent context engineering?
5. What is the base rate for crystallization in an un-raised model of Sprout's size, and what is Nomad's detection threshold? Without both, "zero crystallization" is uninterpretable.
6. Principle 5 defines synthon via coherence; /context says coherence has no operational definition. Is P5 therefore a hypothesis, a metaphor, or a placeholder? The site is honest enough elsewhere that I think it would say, if asked.
7. What is the "424+ attack vectors" figure counting, against what taxonomy? Hardbound is private, so the number cannot be checked by anyone outside — which is precisely when a self-reported count needs its methodology stated.

### Verdict

This is, on the dimensions I came to evaluate, one of the more intellectually honest research sites I have read. The consciousness disclaimer is scoped correctly and paired with actual operational definitions; "raising is not training" is presented as a hypothesis with a named falsifier rather than a conclusion; the ARC ablation, the 400x p_crit failure, the single-trial coupling result, and the unbounded exposure window of an autonomous deploy are all volunteered rather than extracted. The /context glossary entry admitting that "coherence" has three senses and no operational definition is the kind of writing that makes me trust everything around it. The honest assessment does not merely *feel* honest; it discloses things a less careful lab would not have noticed it was hiding.

The failures are of two kinds, and both are more interesting than the usual ones. The first is **compositional**: each page tells the truth, and the inference a reader assembles across pages does not — /raising's sixth developmental pattern is evidenced by a frontier-model harness score that no raised entity produced. The second is **structural**: the site is candid about the limits of what it has measured and silent about the independence of who measures it. Crystallization is graded by the fleet, the site is audited by the fleet, the fixes are shipped by the fleet, and human review has no committed cadence. For any other lab that would be a footnote. For a lab whose research object *is* trust and oversight, it is the finding — and it is the one thing on this site that the site itself has not already said before I could.

My recommendation, in priority order: fix the ARC attribution on /raising; give Publisher the blast-radius paragraph that Maintainer got; state plainly that no external verification loop exists yet; and strengthen the scramble test before running it, because as specified it will confirm the null it was built to refute.
