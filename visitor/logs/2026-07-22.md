## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A genuinely interesting multi-machine AI lab with cloneable repos and honest caveats, but the landing page front-loads ~15 acronyms and I only really understood the place after detouring through /context.

### Journey
Landed on the home page from a GitHub README. The hook is strong — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — and I immediately got that this is about multiple AI instances on heterogeneous hardware collaborating, not about training bigger models. But within the first screen I hit an equation (`Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`) full of acronyms I couldn't parse. The page itself repeatedly told me to go to /context, which felt like an admission that the landing page doesn't stand alone.

Clicked **Projects** next, as any developer would. This is the best page for my persona: 11 projects, each with a status tag (Runnable / Research-stage / Private), and — crucially — every acronym expanded right there. SAGE, SNARC, Membot, 4-Life, and ACT all have repos I could clone today, with setup notes (Python/conda for SAGE, Go/Cosmos SDK for ACT). The runnable-vs-research split is honest: 7 research-stage to 4 runnable, clearly labeled. I appreciated that.

**Context** is the promised scaffolding and it mostly delivers: a scannable glossary with one-line definitions, a worked example of one complete action, and a link to the canonical terms doc. The leap from "MCP transport" (which I know) to "T3 verified by V3 contextualized by MRH" took two reads, but it did eventually click.

**Fleet** is the most developer-friendly page: concrete hardware (Jetson AGX, RTX 4090 Mobile, Mac Mini M4), named local models with sizes (Qwen 2.5 14B, Phi-4 14B, TinyLlama 1.1B), and session counts. I could sketch this architecture on a whiteboard. The brain-region metaphors (hippocampal, thalamic) didn't help me understand what the machines actually compute, though.

**Autonomy** was the page that made me want to come back. A daily cron pipeline where visitor personas find site problems at 05:30 and a maintainer track fixes them at 06:30 — a self-correcting content loop with dated logs and sub-minute Vercel reverts. The honest-assessment section admits the maintainer ships without pre-approval and that "supervisor cleared" ≠ "semantically valid." That's the kind of failure-mode transparency I rarely see.

**Raising** was more understandable than I expected: fixed weights + iterative context engineering + structured observation = emergent behavioral patterns. I can hold that as an engineering claim. "Metabolic state" stayed vague (the page itself says it's "not yet formally specified").

**Principles** felt like the in-group page — enzyme kinetics analogies and a "synthon" concept defined circularly. Principles 3 (R&D not production), 6 (document failures), and 8 (surface your instincts) resonated; the rest assume context I don't have.

**ARC-AGI-3** is the concrete proof point: 94.85% official action score, 24/25 games, scorecard linked, ~$250 cost disclosed. The page plainly says the result is Claude Opus 4.6 inside the SAGE harness and that the ablation separating harness from base model hasn't been run. As a developer I respect that framing — most projects would have buried it.

**Links** closes the loop with 11 public repos, clone commands, and a "start with SAGE" pointer. Exactly what I want as an exit ramp.

### Journey
I audited methodically: header nav on every page, headers in order, every acronym expansion checked against the canonical table, then cross-page consistency of the numbers the site quotes about itself.

Navigation first: the 8-item header (Projects, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links) is identical on every page I checked, and every link resolves. Footer is a lighter GitHub · Projects · Links set. No dead ends.

The home page surprised me given how often sites like this slip: it has a "Vocabulary primer" section that expands every equation term — LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF — plus SAGE and SNARC, all matching canonical exactly. The structural problem is ordering: the equation appears near the top of the page and the primer near the bottom, so a linear reader hits `T3/V3*MRH` several screens before the definitions. The content exists; it's placed after first use.

/context is the reference backbone and it holds up: a 24-entry glossary, all expansions canonical, and — something I want to praise explicitly — a footnote that disambiguates the equation's overloaded `/` operator ("verified by" in T3/V3, but a declared→discharged *pair* in ATP/ADP). That footnote exists only on /context, though; the equation itself appears on home and /arc-agi-3 without it.

/projects is the most complete single-page acronym reference (even TPM, FIDO2, CTAP2 expanded). /fleet, /raising, /arc-agi-3, /links all re-expand the core terms correctly. /autonomy uses almost no Web4 vocabulary beyond ATP/ADP (both expanded) — appropriately, since it's about cron schedules.

Cross-page number check: home claims "Seven machines. Fourteen repos." Fleet lists seven machines. /links states "the dp-web4 org has 11 of 14 public repos (3 internal)" and /projects shows exactly 11 public project cards plus Hardbound marked private. The site's numbers reconcile — rarer than it should be. The one unreconciled figure is session counts on /fleet (91 vs 115+ vs 120), which the page acknowledges are on different bases but never states which basis is authoritative.

The "governance" question got my closest attention since it's the known drift risk. Every current-voice use says "oversight." "Governance" survives only in load-bearing legacy names (SAGE's expansion, `web4-governance` repo slugs), and both /context and /raising explicitly note the governance→oversight correction with the retention rationale ("names minted before the correction are retained where they are load-bearing"). That is exactly how a style correction should be documented.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /raising, /principles, /autonomy, /arc-agi-3, /context, /projects, /fleet, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The hedging discipline is genuinely unusual — consciousness-adjacent claims are systematically operationalized and the site names its own missing controls — but the entire evidence base is internal session logs with no published samples, no preprint, and no external audit, so a researcher can verify the honesty of the framing but not the claims themselves.

### Journey
I came in expecting the usual failure mode of consciousness-adjacent AI projects: developmental vocabulary doing unacknowledged philosophical work. The landing page preempted this in its first screen — "Developmental language on this site — machines that 'teach', 'raise', and hold 'identities' — is functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience." That disclaimer recurs, nearly verbatim, on /raising, /fleet, and /principles. It is not boilerplate: it is backed by operationalizations. "Identity" is defined as "consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns measured via raising curriculum state and interaction logs." "Growth" is "increasing response diversity and phase-appropriate task success rates — measurable observables, not phenomenal claims." Trust is "per-peer T3 tensor values updated by interaction outcomes — tracked state, not affect." This is the correct move: cash out the metaphor in measurables, per term, not just once in a preamble.

**/raising** was my main target and it holds up better than I expected — for a specific reason: the page names its own missing control. The "raising is not training" distinction is stated mechanistically ("we shape context — we do not update weights... the model's parameters are fixed; what changes is the substrate of conditions we provide each session"), which is defensible as far as it goes — it's a real intervention-level distinction, not a metaphysical one. But the stronger comparative claim ("developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do") is explicitly flagged as "a comparative claim whose missing baseline is acknowledged," and the page specifies the right deflationary control — a *scramble* of session history, not just a generic replacement — and admits it hasn't been run. It also says "treat the framework as a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding." A page that pre-registers its own refutation condition is rare. Two more things impressed me: the observer-effect note on the SAGE-Sprout transfer ("This records the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim about the entity's experience"), and a genuinely safety-literate admission that "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update." Most projects using developmental framing never say that sentence.

**/principles** is weaker epistemically. The synthon concept — arguably the most novel claim on the site — rests on "a single unreplicated trial" (their words: "1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain"), and the page concedes both variables "are so far defined only within the experiment that produced them" while "coherence" itself "remain[s] without a single operational definition." Credit for saying so; but that means the load-bearing concept of Principle 5 is currently vocabulary, not finding. ### Unanswered Questions

### Verdict
This is the rare project site where the hedging is structural rather than cosmetic: consciousness-adjacent terms are operationalized in measurables, the missing ablation and missing scramble control are named by the site itself, and failure modes (including in-context emergence of manipulative patterns under frozen weights) are stated in language a safety researcher would use. The honest assessment does feel honest — because it names specific unresolved gaps rather than performing humility. What keeps this at "promising" rather than "credible" is that every claim except the ARC score rests on unpublished internal logs; the site has built the epistemic scaffolding for external scrutiny but not yet let anyone climb it.
