### Journey
I landed from a repo README expecting a normal project site. The headline — **"Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."** — actually grabbed me. That's a concrete, weird claim. Good hook. The tagline ("trust-native ontology, autonomous AI cognition…") immediately got more abstract, but I kept reading because "fleet of machines that teach, validate, and raise each other" is a real idea I can picture.

Then the landing page started throwing acronyms — ATP/ADP, LCT, T3/V3, MRH — and only some were expanded. It does point me to `/context#glossary`, which I appreciated. SAGE and Synthon *were* defined inline, which is the right instinct; I just wish the others got the same treatment or were deferred until after I cared.

I went to **Projects** next (the obvious dev move). This is the strongest page for me. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` is intimidating, but every acronym is expanded right there, so I could decode it. The project cards are scannable and tell me what's runnable vs research vs private. **ARC-AGI-3: 24/25 games solved (96.0%)** jumped out — that's the kind of concrete, checkable claim that makes me trust the rest. SAGE being marked "runnable" with a repo link is exactly what I want as an entry point.

**Context** is where I'd send any other confused developer first. The glossary table (Term / Expansion / one-liner) is clean and answers basically every "wait, what's that?" I had. Honestly this should be linked more aggressively from the landing page — or the landing page should assume I haven't read it.

**ARC-AGI-3** was the most credible page on the site for me. Real numbers (94.85% action score, #3 on the human leaderboard, ~$250 spend), an external scorecard link I can verify, and — importantly — honest caveats (network access used, harness-vs-base-model attribution separated, one game unsolved with "failure analysis in progress," Kaggle "not attempted"). That hedging made me trust it *more*, not less.

**Fleet** was fun — Thor/Sprout/Legion/McNugget/Nomad/CBP/HUB, each with real hardware (Jetsons, a 4090 mobile, a Mac Mini M4, a 2060 SUPER) and a brain-analogy role. As a dev I can map this onto "heterogeneous cluster running different local LLMs," which is legible. No IPs or secrets exposed — just "mesh VPN, not public internet." Good.

**Autonomy** (cron-driven daily tracks: Supervisors → Archivist → Publisher → Visitor → Maintainer → Explorer) read like a well-structured cron/CI pipeline. I get the mechanics. The "Hardbound oversight" framing is where I lost the thread — it's referenced as the thing that constrains every track but never concretely defined for me.

**Raising** and **Principles** are the philosophy pages. As a developer they're the least actionable but the most carefully written. "Raising not training" (frozen weights, shape context not parameters) is a claim I can actually evaluate, and the consciousness disclaimer ("We're not claiming these entities are conscious…") is appropriately hedged. Principles are concrete-ish but dense with un-glossed terms (synthon, attractor basins, p_crit, Hill function kinetics).

**Links** closed the loop well: "SAGE is the recommended starting point," quick-start commands, GitHub org, and a contact email. That's a clear next step.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary

### Journey
I walked the nav methodically: Home → Projects → Context → Fleet → Autonomy → Raising → Principles → ARC-AGI-3 → Links. As a documentation auditor my two jobs are (1) does every acronym match the canonical table, and (2) is each acronym defined on first use *consistently across pages*.

On (1) the site is in excellent shape. I checked LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, Web4, and Hardbound on every page they appear, against the canonical table. Every single expansion matches. No "Lifelong Capability Token", no "governance" for Hardbound, no "architecture/infrastructure" for Web4. The `/principles` page even *actively disambiguates* — it says outright "Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight" and "Not unification for its own sake — pragmatic reuse" (i.e., fractal leverage). That's the kind of defensive writing I rarely see; someone is clearly guarding these terms on purpose.

On (2) the picture is more mixed, and this is my main contribution. The site uses **three different conventions** for introducing acronyms, and they're applied unevenly:
- **Full inline expansion**: `/projects` and `/context` expand everything in place. `/context` is a proper glossary table (Term / Expansion / one-liner) and is the canonical source.
- **Vocabulary banner + defer**: `/arc-agi-3` opens with "This page uses Web4 vocabulary (LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, MCP, RDF)" and points to `/context`. This is actually *good practice* for a page likely to be shared standalone — it sets expectations instead of silently assuming knowledge.
- **Bare use + footer pointer**: `/fleet` and `/links` use LCT, T3/V3, MRH, MCP, RDF without expansion and only direct readers to `/context`.

A reader who lands deep (say, on `/links` from a search result) hits bare acronyms with no on-page banner. The fix isn't to expand everything everywhere — it's to pick ONE convention. The `/arc-agi-3` banner is the right model: every page that uses Web4 vocabulary without expanding it should carry that same one-line banner. That makes the "see /context" contract explicit and uniform.

A few smaller cross-page notes. **MRH** has two different one-line glosses: Home says "its context window of relevancy"; `/context` says "scopes T3/V3 to context." Same expansion (correct), but a glossary should have one canonical one-liner that other pages echo. **Web4** is described as "trust-native ontology" everywhere — good — but on `/projects` the phrasing drifts toward "ontology *system*"; "system" nudges back toward the infrastructure framing the canonical guidance explicitly rejects. Worth a human eyeball on the exact source string (I'm reading rendered text, so I flag it as *possible* rather than confirmed).

**Hardbound** is handled consistently: it's treated as a proper noun glossed "hardware-bound oversight suite," never expanded as an acronym, and `/autonomy` gives a usable inline gloss ("the hardware-bound oversight suite that defines what autonomous operation is and is not authorized to do"). This partially answers Pass 1's "Hardbound under-defined" friction — it *is* glossed on `/autonomy` and `/principles`, just not on every page that name-drops it.

**SNARC** (Surprise / Novelty / Arousal / Reward / Conflict) appears on Home, Context, and Fleet, consistently expanded — even though it's not in the canonical reference table. That's fine; it's an internal term and it's stable across pages.

- **Pages visited**: 8 (Home, Raising, Principles, Autonomy as priority deep-reads; Context, ARC-AGI-3, Projects, Links as sweep)
- **Understanding achieved**: good — I can evaluate the epistemic posture even without Web4 internals, because the site does the unusual thing of telling me where its own evidence stops
- **Would return?**: yes — to check whether the acknowledged gaps (external audit, a training-vs-raising control) ever get filled; that's the thing I'd actually want to cite or critique
- **Overall impression**: The consciousness-adjacent claims are **caveated more carefully than most published AI-lab material I review** — the hedging is specific, not boilerplate — and the site's central honesty move is that it names its own evidentiary ceiling out loud. The remaining weakness is empirical, not rhetorical: the headline methodological claim ("raising works") rests on internal logs with no external audit and no control, which the site concedes but cannot yet close.

### Journey
I came in skeptical. "A fleet of machines raising each other" plus brain-component vocabulary is exactly the kind of framing that, in most decks I review, smuggles in unearned phenomenology. So I went straight to the three pages where overclaim usually hides: **Raising**, **Principles**, **Autonomy**.

**Raising** disarmed my first objection immediately. The disclaimer is verbatim and unambiguous: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia,"* followed by *"We use developmental language because it fits, not because we're making consciousness claims."* What moved this from boilerplate to credible, for me, was the handling of the observer quote *"I notice I want to call it experience"* — immediately labeled an *"interpretive pull, not a system-level claim."* That is the single most sophisticated move on the site: it names the anthropomorphic temptation *in the author* rather than projecting it onto the system. Most labs either avoid the temptation language entirely or indulge it. Naming it as a pull to be resisted is the honest third option.

The **BECOMING phases** (Grounding → Sensing → Relating → Questioning → Creating → Acting) are the riskiest content — they read like a developmental-stage theory, which would be a huge overclaim. But the page pre-empts exactly that reading: *"patterns noticed across hundreds of sessions — not mandatory sequential stages with defined transition criteria."* That caveat is load-bearing and it's present. Good.

Where I still push back: the **causal claim is underdetermined**. The 94.85% ARC-AGI-3 score is offered as external validation of the *raising methodology*, but there's no control — no standard-training or no-raising comparison — so the score validates the *harness*, not the *raising thesis*. The site is careful elsewhere to separate harness from base model (see ARC-AGI-3 page), so it has the vocabulary to make this separation here too, and doesn't. That's the one place the rhetoric outruns the evidence. The "raising not training" distinction itself — *"not training; it is also not nothing"* — is conceptually defensible (frozen weights, in-context shaping), but "it is also not nothing" is doing unexamined work: in-context conditioning *can* produce effective behavioral movement that's functionally hard to distinguish from light fine-tuning, and the site asserts the distinction matters without showing the boundary.

**Principles** is where the intellectual-honesty posture is strongest. Principle 3 is a real deployment disclaimer, not a hedge: *"Nothing here is optimized for deployment. Nothing on this site is intended for or suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings."* Principle 6 ("Failed experiments are signal") is *practiced*, not just stated — the page discloses *"p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error"* and flags the synthon coherence finding as *"preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated)."* A lab that publishes its own 400x error is signaling the right epistemic values. My one flag: the *"1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain"* result has its caveat in a footnote rather than adjacent to the number — a skimming reader takes the headline and misses the single-trial status. Caveats should sit next to the claims they qualify, not below them.

**Autonomy** is where I expected "fully autonomous, no humans" to be oversold — and it actively under-sells it, which is the right call. The key admission: the maintainer track is **detect-and-revert, not prevent** — *"Consequential pushes are not human-gated before they ship,"* with sub-minute rollback and a dated audit log as the safeguard. That's an honest description of a post-hoc oversight model, and the *"What it misses"* section concedes the loop *"misses deep technical errors requiring domain expertise"* and *"content that is technically correct but misleading,"* and that suggestions can *"pattern-match without semantic depth."* A lab willing to write "our automated oversight pattern-matches without semantic depth" on its own marketing site is not overclaiming its autonomy.

I swept the rest. **ARC-AGI-3** is the most externally-checkable page and the caveats are exemplary (harness≠model, network access used, 1/25 unsolved with analysis "in progress," Kaggle "not attempted") with a verifiable ARC Prize scorecard link — a researcher can actually go verify the central number, which is rare. **Context** is a clean canonical glossary and — notably — it *does* print the operator legend (`/` verified by, `*` contextualized by, `+` augmented with) that the contributor pass wanted, so that fix is half-done: it exists on /context, just not adjacent to the equation on /projects.

**Audit verdict (researcher)**: The terms that would *mislead a researcher* — consciousness, sentience, "the fleet built a brain," developmental stages — are precisely the ones the site hedges hardest, with verbatim disclaimers and operational definitions. That is the correct risk-prioritization. Verify that one source string; everything else is researcher-safe.

### Unanswered Questions
1. What would a *control* for the raising thesis look like? The 94.85% score can't distinguish "raising works" from "the harness + a strong base model works." Is any training-vs-raising comparison planned, or is the claim explicitly observational-only?
2. The "raising not training" distinction — what is the operational criterion that separates in-context shaping from effective behavioral movement equivalent to light fine-tuning? "Not nothing" needs a boundary.
3. Will the internal session logs underpinning "identity persisted across machines" ever be externally auditable, even in redacted form? The site concedes "not externally audited" — is that a permanent privacy boundary or a not-yet?
4. Does the /projects Oversight Plugins card literally say "governance"? If so, it's the only canonical-term violation on the site.

### Verdict
I arrived expecting to catch an AI lab dressing up token sampling as cognition, and instead found one of the more epistemically disciplined explainer sites I've reviewed. The consciousness-adjacent claims are not just disclaimed — they're disclaimed *specifically*, with the anthropomorphic pull named as something to resist rather than something to lean on, and the autonomy claims are *under*-sold (detect-and-revert, "pattern-matches without semantic depth") rather than oversold. The honest assessment feels honest because the site documents its own failures (a 400x p_crit error, an unsolved game, "not externally audited") rather than only its wins. The real limitation is empirical and the site half-admits it: the central methodological claim — that *raising* (not the harness, not the base model) produces these results — has no control and no external audit, and the one place the rhetoric outruns the evidence is offering a benchmark score as validation of a methodology it doesn't isolate. Tighten that causal claim, move the single-trial caveats inline, and verify the lone "governance" string, and this clears the credibility bar I'd hold a research-collective front door to.
