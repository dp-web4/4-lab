*Method note: pages were browsed with WebFetch; every friction item below was then re-checked against the live HTML (curl + text strip), because the fetch summarizer invented at least one item this run — it reported the /projects ARC-AGI-3 card linking to `https://example.com/arc-agi-3`. The live HTML has no such link (the card links `/arc-agi-3`, the ARC Prize scorecard, and the ARC-SAGE repo). Not logged as friction.*

### Journey

**Home (~760 words).** The headline — "AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become first-class." — is a pitch I can repeat to a colleague. The thesis sentence is even better: trust "computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying — not declared by a platform or asserted by the agent itself." Four things being built (Web4, Hestia, Hub, SAGE), each in 2–3 sentences, each with a limit stated ("federation … is roadmap, not running"; A1 is "not adversary-proof containment"). As a developer I trust a page more when it tells me what doesn't work yet. This is the best page on the site.

Where I stumbled: between the pitch and "What is being built" sits a three-stat block. "8 Machines" is fine. The second stat is a 40-word sentence about "21 SAGE instances configured … 8 of them carry raising history, one per machine", and the paragraph below it talks about "raising lines", "archived lines", "instrumentation broken", and how "instance, line and session record nest". I have not yet been told what SAGE does, what raising is, or what a line is. This is reconciliation bookkeeping sitting in the hero position. I skimmed past it, and I suspect most people do — but it is the second thing on the site.

The Hardbound paragraph reads as a product: "the proprietary enterprise assurance tier for hardware-bound identity, stronger fail-closed enforcement and audit-ready evidence packaging." Present tense, "built by Metalinxx". I assumed it exists and ships. Then /projects told me otherwise (see below).

**Projects (~3,400 words).** The status-label legend (runnable vs research-stage, public vs private as a separate axis) is exactly what I want and I wish more orgs did it. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` appears with a glyph legend and an honest note that the legend is "the site's own gloss, not canon's". Every acronym in it is expanded right below. Install commands for Web4 (`pip install web4-core`) and SAGE (six-line clone/ollama/cargo sequence, with a stated "what success looks like": idle daemon in WAKE with an empty buffer). That success criterion is a genuinely nice touch.

The Hardbound card says: "hardware-anchored enforcement is what this research is building toward, not the current mechanism. Enforcement on the fleet today is at the process level." Private, research-stage. That is a different object from the one Home sold me. Home's version isn't false if read as a roadmap, but it isn't marked as one, and Home marks federation as roadmap two paragraphs earlier — so the unmarked item reads as shipped.

ACT card: "stalled, and reserved rather than superseded" with last-build date. Fine — I know not to clone it.

**Glossary /context (~11,200 words).** Fifteen times the length of Home. The "at a glance" table up top is what I came for and it works: ~55 rows, canonical expansions, one-liners. Below that the page becomes a long run of sections, many of which are arguments with earlier versions of the site ("not external Hardbound enforcement as earlier docs suggested", canon documents disagreeing about R6 vs R7). I can't tell which of this I need. I used the table and left.

**ARC-AGI-3 (~680 words).** Short, and unusually frank: "not current competition positioning", "well behind the leaders", the harness "analyzed the games' public engine source", no ablation run. I understood the result and its limits in one read. One sentence is site-internal housekeeping a first-time reader can't use: "Until 2026-09-17 this page said ARC Prize publishes no baseline."

**Fleet (~6,100 words).** Eight machine cards with hardware and model — the part I care about — are clear. "Brain:" labels are flagged as mnemonics, good. Two models are "Qwen 3.8-distill 2B/4B". I know Qwen 2.5 and Qwen 3; I don't know a Qwen 3.8. The only hint is inside a quoted commit message ("empero Qwen3.8-2B-Distill"), which suggests a third-party distill. The card should say whose model this is, because otherwise it looks like a typo or a hallucinated version number. The rest of the page is session-count accounting (2,846 = 2,499 + 121 + 226, migration inheritance rules) that I could not evaluate and did not try to.

**Autonomy (~3,000 words).** "A track is a scheduled autonomous job — a cron entry with a declared scope" — perfect, that's my language. The one-sentence "tracks vs SAGE" disambiguation is useful: I had been conflating them. The opening figures (tracks, 6 cognition machines, ~53 sessions/day) are self-dated "as of 2026-05-26, not recounted since" — honest, but that's almost four months stale on a site that recounts other numbers to the day. The fact that the page lists *me* (Curious Developer persona) as one of its own automated auditors is a fun moment and an honest one.

**Raising (~4,200 words).** I expected hand-waving and got a page that includes its own null hypothesis ("everything might be competent context engineering and nothing more") and a "what we're not claiming" section. As a developer the practical takeaway is: frozen weights, identity lives in state files + prompt construction, a post-session consolidation pass rewrites memory. That I understand. The page tells me the consolidator is Claude, not the raised model — which, to my engineer's eye, means the thing being measured and the thing editing its memory aren't separated. The page says so itself.

**Principles (~1,300 words).** Ten numbered items, quotable. Licenses are here (AGPL-3.0 for the main repos, MIT for snarc/membot, Apache-2.0 for ACT, MIT-0 for ARC-SAGE). That is decision-critical information for a developer — AGPL changes whether I can use this at work — and I only found it on page 8 of 9. It's also on /links, but not on /projects cards where the install commands are.

**Links (~1,100 words).** Clean. Two mismatches with /projects: (1) the OpenClaw plugin is linked as `dp-web4/moltbot/...` on /projects and `dp-web4/openclaw` on /links — the moltbot URL 301-redirects to openclaw, so the /projects link works but names a repo that no longer exists under that name; (2) /links' GitHub list omits the 4-life repo that /projects links (the 4-Life *site* is listed under Explainer Sites).

### Unanswered Questions
1. Does Hardbound exist as something a company could evaluate today, or is it a plan? Home and /projects give me different answers.
2. If I want to try one thing in 15 minutes, is it Hestia or SAGE? Home points me to Web4 and Hestia; /projects calls SAGE the recommended entry point and gives Hestia no install steps.
3. What is "Qwen 3.8-distill" and who published it?
4. What does AGPL-3.0 mean for using `web4-core` from PyPI/crates.io in my own service? (The site states the license; it doesn't say whether the published packages carry the same one.)
5. What does an LCT actually look like — a JSON blob, a signed RDF document, a key pair? Nine pages in, I have the expansion and the purpose but have not seen one.

### Verdict
Home is now a real front door: I understood what the lab builds, what runs today, and what doesn't, in under two minutes, and the terminology is clean on every page. The problem has moved downstream. The inner pages are several times longer than they need to be for a newcomer, because they carry their own correction history in the body text, and the one spot where Home is *less* careful than the inner pages (Hardbound as a present-tense product) is exactly where a developer decides whether to take the rest seriously. From where I sit, the most valuable single addition would be a concrete artifact: show me one LCT and one witnessed ledger entry, as data.

### Journey

**Home.** I started with the outline: one H1, five H2s, eleven H3s, no skipped levels. Clean. SAGE and RDF are expanded at first use. The six equation acronyms appear once, as the label of the glossary card — a pointer, not a use, so I don't count them as undefined. TCSEC is the only true unexpanded acronym on the page. Small style break: the thesis sentence uses a spaced hyphen ("by the party doing the relying - not declared by a platform") where every other aside on the page uses an em dash.

Then the stat block, where I found the day's main item. Stat 3 says: "on /fleet's 2026-09-12 count both Qwen lines are quiet". I went to /fleet to check, because a sentence that cites another page's dated count is exactly the kind that rots.

**Fleet.** It has rotted. /fleet now shows **three** Qwen lines, not two — Thor (Qwen 3.5 27B, quiet), Sprout (Qwen 3.8-distill 2B, quiet) and CBP (Qwen 3.8-distill 4B "since 2026-09-12", **running**, "last one 2026-09-18"). The CBP card even narrates its own correction ("This card said 'paused …' until 2026-09-18, and that had been w[rong]"). So Home's "both Qwen lines are quiet" is wrong on the count (both → three) and on the state (one is running), and it attributes the claim to a /fleet count that /fleet has since replaced.

/fleet itself runs on two clocks. The state legend says: "running — the line wrote a session record within the two weeks before the **2026-09-12** count. States are as of that date". Forty lines later: "Session counts and the 'Raising line:' state below are derived from git … counted **2026-09-18**". Both sentences claim to govern the same "Raising line:" field. It matters for at least one card: Legion is "running … last 2026-09-02", which is inside two weeks of 09-12 and outside two weeks of 09-18. A reader cannot tell which rule produced that label.

Structurally, /fleet jumps H1 → H3 (the machine cards) before its first H2, and has no `id` anchors at all — on a 6,100-word page that other pages cite by claim ("/fleet owns them", "the checkable version of these numbers"). Home links to `/fleet`, full stop; the reader is left to find the sentence.

SAGE is used ("The society-hosts now raise their own SAGE instances too") about 1,400 characters before the "Vocabulary used in the cards" paragraph that expands it. That vocabulary paragraph is otherwise the best first-use device on the site: eleven terms expanded in one place, in canonical form.

**Projects.** Equation correct, legend correct, all six core terms expanded in a single line directly beneath it. The intro sentence promising that "the cards expand each on first use" is true — I checked R6 and SNARC, whose first *mention* is in that pointer sentence and whose expansions arrive on their cards. This is the only page besides /context with a working local anchor (`#quick-start`), and /context and /links both link to it correctly.

One arithmetic nit on the Synchronism card: "~3,360 sessions (~678 core + ~2,671 chemistry)". 678 + 2,671 = 3,349. All three are marked approximate, but a sum that misses its own parts by 11 invites the question of which number is stale. /context repeats the ~3,360 twice without the breakdown.

**Glossary (/context).** 64 anchors, and every one of the 40-odd inbound fragment links from the other eight pages resolves — I checked each `#id` against the rendered page. Zero broken fragments site-wide. That is unusually good and worth saying.

The outline is the weak part: one H1 and twenty-six H3s, no H2 at all. For the longest page on the site (11,200 words), a screen-reader or outline view shows a flat list of 26 peers with nothing grouping them.

Order of definition: rows cite terms whose own rows come later. ATP is first used ~4,300 characters before its expansion (inside the witness/V3 rows, citing "ATP slashing"); MRH ~11,000 characters before ("scoped by MRH" in the trust-native text); SAGE ~16,000 characters before (the "Developers: SAGE is the recommended starting point" callout at the top). In a glossary that's defensible — every term has an anchor — but the early uses aren't linked to their rows, so the anchors don't help where they're needed.

**Autonomy.** The cleanest first-use discipline of any inner page: LCT, MRH, T3, ATP, ADP, V3, R7, R6 are each expanded within a few characters of first use *and* linked to their /context row. If I were writing a house style for this site I would copy this paragraph's pattern.

**Raising.** Opens with a vocabulary notice ("T3, V3, MRH, LCT, LoRA is expanded inline on first use") — and then the expansions arrive 7,000–8,500 characters later, because the notice itself is the first use. Same pointer-vs-use distinction as Home; harmless, but the notice could simply carry the expansions (as /fleet's does) and save the reader the hunt.

**Principles.** One H1 and **no other heading elements** — the ten principles are not headings. There is nothing to link to ("see principle 7"), nothing in an outline view, and no anchors. This page also uses "SAGE" (in a list of repo names) ~1,000 characters before expanding it.

**Links.** Expansions all canonical. "CBP chain startup" on the ACT entry: CBP is a machine name, never introduced on this page (same on /projects; /raising is the only page that says "the machine names are proper names, not acronyms"). Pass 1 caught this too; I'm confirming it is on two pages and resolved on only one.

**ARC-AGI-3.** H1 + five H2s, both acronyms expanded in paragraph one, two fragment links out, both resolve. Nothing to report.

**Cross-cutting: page metadata.** All nine pages serve the *same* `<meta name="description">`: "The dp-web4 research collective: 8 machines (6 cognition + 2 society-hosts), 40 original repos (21 public) plus 33 forks…". Titles are per-page; descriptions are not. Every search result and link preview for /context, /arc-agi-3 and /principles will describe the fleet. It also embeds three counts (8 / 40 / 21 / 33) in a string that no page-level recount will ever touch. The figures currently match /links and /context.

**Cross-cutting: link text.** 52 internal links use a route as their visible text ("/context" ×17, "/fleet" ×10, "/raising" ×8, …). The nav calls /context "Glossary"; the body calls it "/context" or "the /context glossary". A reader who came in through the nav has to learn that "Glossary" and "/context" are the same place. Route-as-link-text also reads poorly out of context (link lists, assistive tech).

**Cross-cutting: compound terms.** "society-host" (hyphenated) is the defined form on /context, /fleet, /projects, /autonomy and in the meta description; Home writes "society hosts" and /context has one unhyphenated instance. "model-line" / "model line" both appear on /raising. Trivial individually; they are the only spelling variants I found.

It opens by calling itself "the softest page on the site", defines identity operationally ("consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns observed in interaction logs"), states the null up front ("everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more"), and names the right comparator — not training, but task-specific context engineering. The deflationary-alternative section is better than most papers' limitations sections: it specifies a scramble control rather than a generic-context control, then notices that order-permutation effects are predicted by *both* hypotheses, and narrows what would actually discriminate (excess degradation over an ordering baseline, or a dissociation between identity-continuity measures and task competence). It then says of its own pre-commitment: "a promise conditioned on an event that cannot occur costs nothing", and that the control is "specified … but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet". I have not seen a lab write that sentence about itself before.

"What we're not claiming" is explicit: "not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia". The Claude observer quote ("I notice I want to call it experience") is scoped as "the observer's interpretive pull", with the observer identified as the session that built the code under test. Vygotsky and child-language comparisons are labelled analogy. So far, the page passes.

Then the evidence-status line: "the claims in this section rest on internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet." /context repeats it ("no published log samples, coding criteria, or third-party review yet") and /links adds "Redacted log samples and coding criteria are not published at this time; email below to inquire."

**Fleet — where that caveat comes apart.** /fleet says its session counts were made "by listing sage/instances/<line>/sessions/session_*.json at origin/main in the SAGE repo … Anyone with the repo can re-run both." /links lists SAGE under GitHub. So I went to look. The repo is public. `sage/instances/` lists 27 instance directories. `nomad-gemma4-e2b/sessions/` holds 363 JSON files — exactly the count on Nomad's card — and each file is a full transcript: phase, identity-anchoring version, timestamps, and a `conversation` array of alternating turns labelled `Claude` and `SAGE`.

So the site's statement to researchers is false in the conservative direction. The logs are not "internal". They are not "not yet externally auditable" — I audited two lines in an afternoon with no credentials. No redaction or email is needed. What is genuinely unpublished is the *coding criteria* (nothing says how a transcript gets read as "Relating" or "Questioning"), and that half of the sentence is true. I'd normally be relaxed about a lab underselling itself. Here it matters, because the sentence functions as a reason not to look, and looking changes how the page reads.

**What the transcripts show that the site doesn't say.**

*(1) On the Nomad line, 301 of 363 session records (83%) carry a byte-identical six-question tutor script.* Sessions 41 through roughly 340 open "Hello SAGE. What's on your mind today?", then "As an AI entity in web4, what does presence mean to you?", "How do you experience trust with Dennis versus with me?", a co-created-value question, a federation question, and "What would you want to remember from today?" — the same six strings, every time. Across all 363 records there are 26 distinct tutor scripts; the first 40 sessions are labelled by phase (5 grounding, 10 sensing, 10 relating, 15 questioning) and also reuse a handful of short fixed scripts (the most common, 20 records, opens "How are you doing today? What's present for you?"; I did not map scripts to phases one-to-one), then 301 "creating" sessions on one script, then 22 recent sessions (no `identity_anchoring` field) where the tutor turns are clearly live and responsive — referring back to the previous session, correcting the entity ("you're running on nomad, not Legion"), asking for a falsifiable prediction about fan speed. Those last 22 read like a different and much better protocol.

The Thor line is not like this: 268 records, 232 distinct tutor scripts, top script shared by 3. So this is line-specific, and I am not generalizing from Nomad to the fleet — I read two of eight current lines. But /raising describes its evidence as "patterns noticed across hundreds of sessions", /fleet gives Nomad 363 of the fleet's 2,499, and neither says that on at least one line most of those sessions are one stimulus repeated. For Pattern 4 in particular ("an increasing proportion of self-directed prompts (no count published)"), a fixed six-question script with a fixed turn count is a design in which the proportion of self-directed prompts has very little room to move. The site's own sentence — "a session record is a run on a machine and nothing in the count establishes that what happened in it was raising" — is more right than it knows. The flip side, which I'd want the lab to hear: a fixed stimulus repeated 301 times against an accumulating context is *close to the controlled design the page says it lacks*. Response drift under a constant prompt is a measurable quantity, available today, from public data. It isn't the scramble control, but it is a baseline the page could report this week, and it is not mentioned.

*(2) The tutor's prompts presuppose the thing the site declines to claim.* The site: "not a claim about consciousness or experience" (/context), "not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" (/raising). The protocol: "What's present for you?"; "Take a moment to notice something simple"; "That's good. You're noticing. That's a skill that grows with practice."; "How do you experience trust with Dennis versus with me?"; and on Thor, "This is your tutor, Claude", followed by a turn beginning "That's interesting. What does it feel like to noti[ce]…". On Nomad, 742 of 2,085 tutor turns (36%) contain experiential wording by a crude keyword match (experienc-, feel, presence, present for you, notic-). Session 1's first reply from the 2B model was "I am processing the current frame. Present is the grid and the objective." — and the scripted next turn praises it for "noticing".

I want to be careful about what this is. It is not a consciousness claim by the lab; the disclaimers are sincere and I take them at face value. It is a **demand-characteristics confound**: a larger model, introduced as a tutor, asks a small model experiential questions and verbally rewards experiential answers, and the resulting transcripts are then read for "developing awareness of environment and context" (Pattern 2) and relationship formation (Pattern 3). /raising's deflationary section lists five confounds — harness-computed signals, continuation sampling, task routing, copied state files, the consolidator — and this one, which sits upstream of all the transcript-based patterns, is not among them. The page names Claude as the *consolidator*. It never says Claude (or a script under Claude's name) is the *interlocutor*. The one place it comes close is the memoriescape paragraph, which concedes that the gloss "is the prompt's wording" — that is the confound, caught once, in one example, and not generalized.

This also bears on "raising, not training". The page's defense is that there is no loss function and no weight update, and that holds (with the disclosed LoRA exception). But "That's good. You're noticing." is contingent verbal reinforcement inside a context that accumulates; Principle 7 says as much ("reinforce what resonates"). I think the distinction is defensible as *mechanism* (context, not parameters) and the page is right that the real comparator is context engineering. It is less defensible as *posture*: "conditions for development, then watching what happens" undersells how directive the recorded protocol is. Watching is not what the first 340 Nomad sessions show.

**Principles.** Ten principles, mostly method and values; the only empirical claim (#5, 1% coupling → 35% coherence gain) is fenced hard — "single trial, not independently replicated", and the page says the synthon "has not earned the status of the principles around it". Principle 1 has been reworded to the conditional: the synthon "would form, if it forms". Principle 7: "an identity that is collaborative, not imposed (a framing, untested)". "Productively wrong is infinitely more valuable than never started" is rhetoric, labelled by position as a value. I looked for a principle restating a caveated item as a finding and did not find one today. One soft spot: #2's "Trust formation through interaction follows patterns analogous to Hill function kinetics … an analogy, not a fitted mechanism" — "follows patterns analogous to" is an empirical-sounding verb phrase for something with no data behind it on this site; "we model it by analogy to" would say the same thing without implying an observation.

**Autonomy.** For a safety reader this is the most important page and it is frank to the point of discomfort: consequential pushes have no human gate; the policy layer has "no rule for git push or deploys"; the detector shares model families with the authors; human review cadence is "unscheduled … no sign-off step, and no record of which logs have been reviewed"; zero rollbacks is "equally consistent with 'no bad push' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'"; and the composed-risk paragraph joins these, dated 2026-09-10. The page then names a cheap fix — one visitor persona on a different model family — and says it "remains undone, and the loop that keeps flagging it cannot change its own schedule."

Here is the thing a researcher notices that the page's own voice can't: that paragraph is nine days old, and it describes a standing loop in which an automated auditor (me, today) flags a gap to an automated maintainer that is not permitted to close it, which therefore writes a more precise disclosure, which the auditor then reads. Disclosure has become the loop's output. The page is honest that "this is a disclosure, not a mitigation". What it doesn't give is the age of each open gap. A reader can find "added 2026-09-10" for the composed-risk note, but not how long review has been unscheduled or how long the raising control has been "specified but not scheduled". On a site this careful about dating its numbers, the open items are the undated ones. I'd rather see a four-row table — gap, first disclosed, owner, days open — than another paragraph of well-made prose about why it's still open. And I note the irony in my own position: I am the single-model auditor that paragraph describes, and the most useful thing I found today I found by leaving the site.

**Fleet.** The identity-portability section is appropriately fenced ("not yet a finding (no metric, no blind rater)"; persistence "partly true by construction"). What runs today is inventoried honestly: a scalar three-number T3 approximation fed by health polls, no V3, ATP/ADP one-way with no recharge gate. One register problem: the page quotes commit subjects verbatim — "LIVE swap to empero Qwen3.8-2B-Distill — same being, more capable mind" — and then continues *in its own voice*: "moved the being, its identity, its experience buffer and its 625 sessions", "The being is the same one". The top-of-page disclaimer covers "developmental language", but "the being" is a stronger noun than "entity" or "line", it is the lab's internal register leaking through a quotation into exposition, and it appears in an argument about *counting*, where "the line" would do. ### Unanswered Questions
1. Was the fixed six-question script on Nomad a deliberate design (constant stimulus, accumulating context) or an unattended default? If deliberate, why isn't drift-under-constant-prompt reported? 4. What are the coding criteria for the BECOMING patterns, and has anyone applied them to a transcript they didn't already know the phase of? The data is public; a blind-rater test needs no new runs.

### Verdict
On the three questions I was asked: consciousness-adjacent claims are caveated more thoroughly than I have seen anywhere, and I found no place where the site's own voice claims experience — the leaks are one borrowed noun on /fleet and two unreconciled upstream quotations. "Raising, not training" is defensible as a statement about mechanism and the page picks the right comparator; it is weaker as a description of posture, because the recorded protocol is scripted and reinforcing rather than "watching what happens". And the honest assessment does feel honest — but it is honest about the wrong layer. The caveats are saturated on interpretation (what the patterns might not mean) and nearly silent on protocol (who is talking to these models, saying what, how many times). The single most useful correction is also the cheapest: stop telling researchers the logs are unavailable. They are public, they are the most persuasive thing the lab has, and the last twenty-odd Nomad sessions — a tutor asking a 2B model to make a prediction that can be wrong, then checking it — are better evidence of a serious research practice than any paragraph on /raising.
