## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, projects, context, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, arc-agi-3, links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A genuinely interesting multi-machine AI research lab that fronts its honesty, but the acronym wall means I had to detour to /context before most pages made sense.

### Journey
Landed on the home page from a GitHub README. The hero — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — is a great hook; I immediately got that this is a distributed research operation, not a product. The "termite mound" line ("You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules.") landed well for me as a developer who's seen emergent behavior in distributed systems. But the Web4 equation (`MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`) hit me in the first minute with six acronyms I couldn't parse. The home page has a vocabulary primer, which helped, but LCT/MRH still felt like insider shorthand.

Went to /projects next, as any developer would. The runnable vs. research-stage split is exactly what I want — I can tell immediately that SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, and Membot are things I could clone today. There's a quick-start code block, which is rare and appreciated. But the project cards use the same undefined acronyms and just point me at "/context glossary."

So I went to /context — and honestly, this page saved the whole visit. Every acronym in a scannable table with expansions and one-line definitions, plus a worked example (a maintainer commit traced through the trust machinery). If I'd found this page first, everything else would have read twice as fast.

/fleet was the most fun page for a hardware-curious dev: seven machines from a Jetson Nano to an RTX 4090, each running a different local model mapped to a brain-ish role. The claim that behavioral patterns survived a model swap (Qwen 0.5B → TinyLlama) — "identity lives in state files and prompt construction, not model weights" — is the single most interesting technical claim on the site.

/autonomy reads like CI/CD for research: 31+ cron tracks, a SQLite registry, a Visitor→Maintainer feedback loop (which I now realize includes visits like mine — nice recursion). The frank admission that the Maintainer ships to the public site without a human gate stood out.

/raising and /principles were the densest. Raising made sense mechanically (frozen weights, shaped context, staged capability rollout) and the "deflationary alternative" section — where they argue against their own framing — earned trust. /principles felt like internal docs; I skimmed it. /arc-agi-3 has real numbers (94.85% action score, #3 on the human leaderboard, ~$250 spend) and states up front that the no-harness ablation hasn't been run, which is the kind of caveat most benchmark pages hide.

/links closed the loop well: "SAGE is the recommended starting point," with setup commands and license info. That's the actionable exit a developer wants.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, projects, context, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, arc-agi-3, links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good

- **Overall impression**: Terminology discipline is the best I've audited on a research site — zero canonical drift across nine pages — but first-use expansion compliance is uneven page to page.

### Journey
I worked through the site methodically: home first, then every nav link in order, cross-checking each acronym expansion against the canonical table as I went, and re-fetching two pages to verify exact wording where a summary suggested possible drift.

The home page fronts the Web4 equation (`MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`) and then defines every component in a vocabulary primer lower on the page — the definitions exist, but the reading order is inverted (symbol before referent). Navigation is a consistent 8-link header (projects, context, arc-agi-3, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, links); every page I visited was reachable, and I found no orphaned pages or dead nav links.

The /context glossary is the terminological anchor and it is exemplary: every canonical term expanded exactly right, the equation's operators glossed ("/" = verified by, "*" = contextualized by, "+" = augmented with), and — unusually — a section where the page audits *itself*, admitting "coherence" has three senses with no operational definition, session counting uses three unreconciled bases, and the T3/V3 implementation is scalar triplets rather than the full canonical RDF sub-graph. As a documentation professional I rarely see a glossary that documents its own gaps. That's the right practice.

Two suspected drift instances dissolved under verification. First, a summary of /fleet suggested Web4 was described as "protocol infrastructure" — a verbatim re-check showed the word "infrastructure" does not appear on the page at all. Second, /projects appeared to call Web4 a "specification" — the exact sentence is "The repo itself is a specification — documents and schemas, no runnable demo," which correctly describes the *repository*, not the ontology. Both are clean.

The consistency wins are systematic: Web4 is "trust-native ontology" on every page that characterizes it, with /context and /raising explicitly stating "not architecture or infrastructure" and /principles reinforcing it as Principle 10. Hardbound is "oversight" everywhere — "hardware-bound oversight suite" on four pages, "Hardbound is oversight" in Principle 10 — never "governance." The word "unification" appears only in negative framing ("reuse, not unification"; "not unification for its own sake"), which is exactly the canonical usage. "Production ready" appears nowhere on the site.

The weakness is uneven first-use discipline. /raising and /autonomy expand every domain acronym they use on first use — the model pages. But /principles uses LCT without expansion while expanding T3, V3, RDF, and LoRA on the same page; /fleet uses RPE (reward prediction error) bare; /arc-agi-3 leaves PolicyGate, MCP, and RDF unexpanded while expanding eight other terms. This isn't drift — it's inconsistent application of a style rule the site clearly has.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, raising, principles, autonomy, arc-agi-3, context, fleet, projects, links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The most systematically self-hedging research site I've evaluated — caveats are integrated into claims rather than appended — but nearly everything rests on internal logs no outsider can audit, and several core terms are operationally hollow.

### Journey
I came in from a conference mention with one question: is this a lab doing careful work with unusual vocabulary, or vocabulary doing the work of a lab? I started at the home page, went straight to /raising, then /principles and /autonomy, and swept the rest.

The home page surprised me in the first screen: "Developmental language on this site — machines that 'teach', 'raise', and hold 'identities' — is functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience." That disclaimer is *proactive* — placed before the reader has encountered the language it disclaims. The identity-persistence claim (SAGE-Sprout across 180+ sessions and a model swap) comes with its own parenthetical operationalization: "measurable observables, not philosophical continuity." This is the hedging discipline I usually have to ask for in review.

/raising is where consciousness-adjacent claims would concentrate, and it's the strongest page from my seat. Every developmental term gets a mechanistic gloss: "learns" is cashed out as "these facts come to appear reliably in session behavior"; bilateral generation as "token sampling that continues past expected response boundary — not a claim about internal experience"; "relationships" as "per-peer T3 tensor values updated by interaction outcomes — tracked state, not affect." An observer's pull toward experience language ("I notice I want to call it experience") is attributed as an observer note, not a system claim — the right epistemic bookkeeping. Best of all, the page presents a genuine deflationary alternative: everything observed "might be competent context engineering and nothing more," followed by the admission "No deflationary control exists yet" and a specification of what a valid control would be (a scrambled-session-order comparison, not generic replacement). Specifying your own falsifier before anyone asks is rare. The closing instruction — "treat the framework as a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding" — is exactly the right epistemic status.

Is "raising not training" defensible? Mechanically, yes: weights are frozen, only context changes, and the page says so plainly ("In raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights"). The distinction is real at the level of mechanism. Where it gets softer is the surrounding developmental framing — Vygotsky analogies, phase names like "Sensing/Relating/Questioning" — which invites readings the operational definitions don't support. The page acknowledges the analogy is superficial, but the labels will still do interpretive work in readers' heads that the disclaimers can't fully claw back. Defensible framing, with a live anthropomorphization tax.

/principles is more mixed. Principles 3, 6, and 10 are methodological positions I'd endorse. But 8–10 shift from empirical to normative without marking the transition — "productively wrong is infinitely more valuable" is a value statement presented alongside evidence-shaped claims, and "words shape how you think" is asserted, not argued. The synthon principle leans on "coherence," which the site's own glossary admits has three senses and no operational definition. To the site's credit, the single supporting experiment is flagged as "single trial, not independently replicated" and its coherence number as "the experiment's internal metric, not any of the glossary's three senses."

/autonomy is where my safety hat came on. The candor is genuinely unusual: "the oversight posture is detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent"; the maintainer is "the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate"; repo pushes are "not fully revertible" and that gap is "unaddressed, not solved"; "This is a description of practice, not a verifiable mechanism — the distinction matters." A lab hiding something does not write those sentences. But naming a gap is not mitigating it. The substantive picture is: 31+ autonomous tracks, ~53 sessions/day, automated bad-push detection on a ~24-hour loop that only catches some failure classes, human review of audit logs "asynchronous, with no committed cadence," and the load-bearing oversight layer (Hardbound) both private and still in development. Semantic errors in deployed public content can persist indefinitely by the site's own account. There's also no incident history — for a lab this honest about design gaps, the absence of a "here's what has actually gone wrong" section is conspicuous; either nothing has (worth saying) or something has (worth documenting, per their own Principle 6).

/arc-agi-3 handles its headline number correctly: "One caveat belongs up front: the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run." Cost disclosed (~$250), scorecard externally linked, harness attribution to Claude Opus 4.6 explicit. This is the only externally verifiable claim on the site — and /links confirms it: no papers, no preprints, "redacted log samples and coding criteria are not published at this time." The 900+ raising sessions, the identity-persistence observation, the coupling experiment — all rest on internal logs nobody outside can inspect. The site is honest *about* this, which I credit, but honesty about unverifiability is not verifiability. As presented, almost nothing here is citable beyond the ARC scorecard.

Does the honest assessment feel honest? Yes — and structurally, not performatively. The tell is that the caveats are load-bearing and specific (which control is missing, which gap is unaddressed, which counting bases disagree) rather than generic humility. Performative hedging says "more work is needed"; this site says "the scramble control has not been run and here is what it would look like." Those are different species.

### Unanswered Questions
1. Has the scramble control for /raising ever been scheduled, or is "no deflationary control exists yet" a standing state? Specifying the falsifier earns credit only if someone eventually runs it.
2. What is the actual incident history across 31+ autonomous tracks? Detect-and-revert implies things have been detected and reverted — where's that log's summary?
3. Is there any path to external audit — redacted logs, a third-party reviewer, a preprint with methods — or is the evidentiary model "trust our internal records" indefinitely?
4. Who is the human in the loop, concretely? The site describes one contact email and no committed review cadence; is oversight of this fleet one person's spare attention?
5. When Hardbound ships, will its enforcement model be published even if its code isn't?

### Verdict
This site does something I almost never see: it hedges proactively, specifies its own missing controls, and states which of its claims are working vocabulary rather than findings — the deflationary-alternative section on /raising alone puts it above most lab pages I review. But its epistemic discipline outruns its evidentiary base: except for one ARC scorecard, every claim bottoms out in internal logs nobody outside can read, and its safety posture (detect-and-revert, uncommitted review cadence, private oversight layer) is described more honestly than it is mitigated. I'd cite this site as an exemplar of caveat practice; I couldn't yet cite it for any of its actual results.
