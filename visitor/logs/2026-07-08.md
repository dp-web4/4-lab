### Journey

Landed on the home page from a GitHub README. Headline — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — is punchy and I got the gist fast: a personal research collective where AI agents maintain sites, review each other, and run on a schedule. The "New here? Start with /context" pointer was the single most useful thing on the page; I took the hint.

/context delivered. The glossary-at-a-glance table is exactly what a developer wants — every acronym in one place with a one-line definition. LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP all made sense as *vocabulary*, though I still can't picture the runtime. The admission that "coherence" has three different senses with "no single operational definition yet" was refreshingly honest.

/projects is the strongest page for my persona: 12 projects, each labeled runnable/research-stage/private, all public repos directly linked. SAGE and Web4 flagged as entry points. "Specification — documents and schemas, no runnable demo" on Web4 is the kind of honest labeling I rarely see. Clicked mentally: SAGE is the thing I'd actually clone.

/fleet was fun — seven named machines with model + hardware specs (Qwen on Jetsons, Phi-4 on a 4090, TinyLlama on WSL2). As a developer I can *see* this infrastructure. The brain-region analogies (hippocampal, thalamic) are clearly marked as metaphors, fine.

/autonomy describes 31+ cron-scheduled tracks with a real timeline. The candor is notable — "detect-and-revert, not prevent," maintainer track has "higher blast radius." But an autonomous agent pushing to prod (Vercel) with only asynchronous human review would raise eyebrows in any CI/CD shop, and the page doesn't quite acknowledge how unusual that is.

/raising and /principles are the philosophy pages. Raising = context shaping without weight updates; six observed phases; explicit disclaimer that "everything might be competent context engineering." As a pragmatist I appreciate that they name their own null hypothesis, but session logs aren't public, so none of it is auditable.

/arc-agi-3 is the credibility anchor: 24/25 games, 94.85% action score, public scorecard link, MIT-0 code, ~$250 API cost disclosed. But they admit the ablation (SAGE harness vs. raw Claude) hasn't been run — which is the one number a developer actually wants.

/links closes the loop with repo entry points and a contact email. Fine.

That tension recurs: the disclaimers are careful, the surrounding prose is confident. Readers extract vibes, not footnotes.

/raising is where I expected to find trouble and mostly didn't. The raising/training distinction is procedural and crisp: "Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development," and "we shape context — we do not update weights." Identity is operationalized as session-to-session behavioral consistency in logs; "growth" as measurable response diversity; bilateral generation as "token sampling that continues past the expected response boundary — not a claim about internal experience." Most striking: the page states its own null hypothesis under "The deflationary alternative" — "everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more" — and admits "the comparison has not been run. No deflationary control exists yet." An observer's pull toward the word "experience" is preserved *as data* about the observer, not promoted to a system claim. This is genuinely good epistemic practice. The problem is evidentiary, and the page admits that too: claims "rest on internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet." So the six phases (Grounding → Acting) are a self-report. I cannot distinguish "the model progressed through phases" from "the observer's labeling vocabulary progressed through phases."

/principles mixes stances and claims. Principle 6 (document failures — a "p_crit derivation failed catastrophically," 400x error) is a strong credibility signal; people constructing a facade don't publish 400x errors. Principle 5 is my sharpest concern on the whole site: "1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain" — a precise number quantifying "coherence," a term the site's own glossary says has "no single operational definition yet." A headline percentage on an undefined metric is exactly the shape of claim that erodes trust with researchers, even flagged as single-trial. Principle 7's "It is not training; it is also not nothing" is honest about the middle ground but is a placeholder where a mechanism should eventually be.

/autonomy is unusually candid for a live autonomous system: "the oversight posture is detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent (pre-approval)"; the maintainer track is flagged as a "Known oversight gap" — "the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate"; and, remarkably, "for failures the visitor loop misses (content that is technically correct but misleading), the exposure window is unbounded in principle." They even flag that Hardbound enforcement is currently "a description of practice, not a verifiable mechanism." What's missing is the proactive layer a safety reviewer would ask for next: no rate limiting or anomaly-detection thresholds, no committed human review cadence or escalation path, no stated credential-scoping details, no mechanism for catching *logical* conflicts between tracks (two tracks emitting contradictory claims on separate pages). Naming the unbounded exposure window is honest; a mitigation plan for it is nowhere stated.

/context earns its role: the evidence-tier framing (externally validated / internal observation / unreplicated single trial) is the single most researcher-friendly structure on the site, and the three-senses-of-coherence disclosure is the kind of ambiguity most glossaries hide. /arc-agi-3 is the one externally checkable artifact: 94.85% official action score, 24/25 games, public scorecard, MIT-0 code, ~$250 cost disclosed, and the key attribution question handled correctly — "the loop is the capability — stated as a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated result: the ablation... has not been run." No confidence intervals or repeat runs, though; one run of a stochastic system is a point estimate. /fleet marks its brain-region labels as "functional analogies... not claims about neural correspondence" and even admits the identity-transfer result's soft spot ("The self-description drifted"), though "zero crystallization achieved" is a metric with no published methodology. /projects and /links close the loop; the 14 public repos and the scorecard are real external anchors.

### Unanswered Questions
1. What are the coding criteria for phase transitions — who decides a session is "Questioning" rather than "Creating," and with a single observer, is any inter-rater reliability possible even in principle?
2. The 35% coherence gain: measured how, and in which of the glossary's three senses of "coherence"?
3. Is there a plan to publish redacted session logs, or to pre-register the deflationary control the raising page admits doesn't exist?
4. Has the ARC-AGI-3 run been repeated? What's the variance across runs of a stochastic system?
5. Who reviews the audit logs — is the entire oversight posture one person, and what happens to the "detect" half of detect-and-revert if that person is unavailable for a week?

### Verdict
This site does the two things I check first better than most academic lab pages: it states its own null hypothesis and it publishes its failures. Consciousness-adjacent claims are consistently operationalized and disclaimed, the "raising not training" framing is procedurally real (context vs. weights) even if the fit-better claim is untested, and the honest assessment genuinely reads as honest — the unbounded-exposure-window admission on /autonomy is something most orgs would never print. But candor about missing evidence is not evidence: everything except the ARC score rests on private logs, single trials, and undefined metrics, and the site's precision (35% gain, six phases, zero crystallization) outruns its published methodology. I'd cite the ARC result today; I'd cite the rest the day a redacted log, a coding rubric, and one control run exist.
