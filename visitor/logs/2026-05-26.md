My job isn't "did they get the term right" — they overwhelmingly did — it's "did they say it the *same way every time*," because inconsistent expansions are how a reader starts to wonder whether two pages mean two different things.

**The good news first**, because it's the headline: I found **no canonical-term drift**. LCT is "Linked Context Token" on every page that expands it — never "Lifelong Capability Token." T3 and V3 are correct everywhere. MRH is "Markov Relevancy Horizon" everywhere. ATP/ADP are "Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packet" everywhere. **Now the writer's nitpicks**, which are about consistency, not correctness:

1. **ARC-AGI-3's expansion is not stable across pages.** Context expands it as "Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence, **generation 3**." But the *dedicated* /arc-agi-3 page, /projects, and /raising all expand it as "Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence" — dropping "generation 3." The "-3" in the name *is* the generation, so the page named after it should be the one that gets the expansion complete. A reader who reads Context then ARC will notice the suffix vanish.

2. **"31" vs "31+" tracks.** Home says "**31+** Autonomous Tracks"; /autonomy says "**31** autonomous tracks across 6 machines, ~53 sessions per day." Pick one. (Separately — and Pass 1 flagged this too — /autonomy claims 31 but its timeline only *names* ~8–9 tracks. That's a numerator/denominator clarity gap, not a terminology gap, but it compounds the count ambiguity.)

3. **`p_crit` and "attractors" are never defined.** `p_crit` appears on /principles with no expansion; "attractors" appears on both /principles and /raising ("Behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns") with no gloss. These aren't Web4 canonical terms, so they're not "drift" — but for any reader they're undefined symbols dropped into prose that assumes familiarity. `p_crit` especially reads like notation that lost its legend.

4. **Definition-on-first-use is page-local, not global.** This is the structural pattern Pass 1 hit from the developer angle, and I confirm it from the terminology angle: each page is internally consistent, but there's no shared first-use discipline. /links uses LCT, T3, V3, MCP, RDF, ATP/ADP all bare; /principles uses Hardbound and dp-web4 bare; /autonomy uses Hardbound bare. That's defensible on a links page, less so on Principles.

**Two places where I could NOT reproduce Pass 1's findings** — flagging these honestly rather than rubber-stamping the prior pass:
- Pass 1 marked /projects HIGH-severity for using "five acronyms with no inline definition." As served to me, the /projects Web4 card defines **all** of them inline: *"T3 (Talent / Training / Temperament) verified by V3 (Valuation / Veracity / Validity), contextualized by Markov Relevancy Horizon (MRH), anchored to Linked Context Token (LCT) — with resources tracked via Allocation Transfer Packet (ATP) and Allocation Discharge Packet (ADP), augmented with MCP (Model Context Protocol) transport and RDF (Resource Description Framework) representation."* That's every acronym expanded on first use. Either the page was updated between passes, or Pass 1's fetch summarizer paraphrased the card; I'm reporting the served text.
- Pass 1 flagged a headline-number "mismatch" between /projects and /arc-agi-3. As served to me the two pages are **consistent in value**: both carry "94.85% official ARC Prize action score" and "96.0% (24/25 games)" with "~$250" and "Claude Opus 4.6." The residual issue is editorial, not a contradiction: two true metrics (action score vs game-solve rate) sit near each other and a skimming reader can conflate them — but both pages label them the same way.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (Home, Raising, Principles, Autonomy — read closely — then Context, Projects, Fleet, ARC-AGI-3, Links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good — I came to evaluate the *epistemics*, not learn Web4, and I got what I needed
- **Would return?**: yes — this is the rare research-collective site I'd cite as an example of honest hedging, with reservations noted below
- **Overall impression**: The intellectual honesty here is genuinely above the field median — explicit phenomenology disclaimers, a showcased 400× failure, an unusually candid R&D-not-deployment caveat — but the *prose* leans on developmental and dynamical-systems vocabulary that does more persuasive work than the caveats fully neutralize, and the autonomy claims assert an oversight mechanism they never demonstrate.

### Journey
I came in the way a skeptical reviewer does: I trust caveats more than claims, and I read the disclaimers *first* to see whether the body text earns them. The home page set the tone well — before any developmental language appears, there's a standing disclaimer: *"Developmental and lifecycle terms below (identity, behavioral continuity) are functional descriptions of observed system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims."* That's the right move and the right placement.

**Raising** is where I spent the most time, because "raising not training" is the claim most likely to overreach. The framing — *"Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens."* — is **defensible**, and more defensible than the word "raising" first suggests. It maps onto a real methodological distinction: in-context behavioral shaping and selection versus gradient descent on weights. That's not a consciousness claim, it's a description of *where* the intervention happens (the context, not the loss). I can accept that.

What gives me pause is the gap between the disclaimers and the verbs. The page disclaims hard and explicitly — *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia,"* and the admirably self-aware *"We use developmental language because it fits, not because we're making consciousness claims,"* plus *"treat them as descriptive scaffolding, not measured stages"* for the six phases (Grounding, Sensing, Relating, Questioning, Creating, Acting). But the phase *body text* still reads with full agency: *"The entity begins generating its own questions rather than only responding to prompts,"* *"finds its own niche in the ecosystem,"* *"begins to distinguish between its own state and external inputs."* A careful reader holds the top-of-page disclaimer in working memory and discounts these. A skimming reader does not — and the disclaimer is doing continuous load-bearing work to undo language the author chose to keep. This is *caveated*, so it's not dishonest; but it's the spot where a press writer or a critic could lift a sentence out of context and the site would have handed them the quote.

The single strongest honesty signal on the site is also on Raising: the load-bearing empirical claim — that identity persisted across model swaps — is **operationalized**, not asserted. It's defined as *"consistent session-to-session interaction patterns, accumulated experience, and raising curriculum — measurable observables, not philosophical continuity."* That's exactly what I want: the claim is reduced to observables I could in principle check. If every developmental sentence on the page were anchored that tightly, I'd have no reservation at all.

**Principles** confirmed the pattern. Principle #6 ("Document failures") states flatly: *"The p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400× error,"* and resists the temptation to spin it — *"Trust thresholds are empirical, not derivable from first principles. That's a finding, not a failure."* Researchers almost never publish their 400× misses; showcasing one is a costly, credible signal. ### Unanswered Questions

### Verdict
As an outside trust/safety researcher, I'd hold this up as a *better-than-average* example of honest hedging in a speculative research collective: the phenomenology disclaimers are explicit and well-placed, the one load-bearing empirical claim (identity persistence) is reduced to observables, a catastrophic 400× failure is showcased rather than buried, and the R&D-not-deployment caveat is stated in exactly the terms a safety reviewer wants.
