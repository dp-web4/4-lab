## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understood what this lab is inside two minutes and I believed it, which is rare — but the Projects page doesn't list two of the four things the home page says are being built.

### Journey

I came from a repo README with no Web4 context. The landing page did its job faster than most: the headline ("AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become infrastructure") plus the one-sentence thesis — trust computed from witnessed evidence by the party relying on it, not declared by a platform — told me the problem, the position, and why it isn't just another agent framework. The "What is being built" block gave me four names with one paragraph each: Web4, Hestia, Hub, SAGE. I could hold all four.

Two things on the home page slowed me down. "Running today at A1 assurance" — A1 on what scale? The gloss after the dash ("cooperative and tamper-evident, not adversary-proof containment") tells me roughly where it sits, which I appreciate, but "A1" implies a published tier ladder I never found. And the stat strip says "21 Configured SAGE instances in the 2026-09-08 census" and "5 Model families" — the census isn't linked anywhere I could find, so those are the only two numbers on the site I can't chase down.

Then I clicked **Projects**, specifically to read more about Hestia — the home page had just sold it to me as "local governance for humans and AI agents from multiple vendors," which is the part I'd actually use. There is no Hestia card. There is no Hub card either. Eleven cards: Web4, SAGE, Synchronism, Hardbound, ACT, Oversight Plugins, AI DNA Discovery, 4-Life, SNARC, Membot, ARC-AGI-3. Hub appears once, in a clause inside the SAGE card. So the page the home page points me to for "full ecosystem" omits half the headline lineup. I had to go back to the home page and use the raw GitHub link. That's the single worst moment in the visit, and it's on the shortest path a new developer takes.

The cards themselves are good — each one has a status label (public/private, research-stage/runnable) which is exactly what I want to know before cloning anything. "Runnable" vs "research-stage" saved me time.

**Glossary** (/context) is the strongest page on the site for someone like me. Every acronym I'd collected was there with a one-line expansion, and the equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` comes with explicit operator semantics (`/` = verified by, `*` = contextualized by, `+` = augmented with) and the line that nothing is solved for mathematically. That last sentence is why I trusted the rest of the page — they saw the obvious objection to calling it an "equation" and answered it before I could raise it. The page is long and I skimmed the theory sections, but the glossary table at the top is scannable and that's all I needed.

**Fleet** is eight machines with hardware, model, and session counts. Concrete, checkable, no mystique. The "brain" labels are explicitly flagged as functional analogies, not neural claims — good. I noticed the active models are Qwen, Gemma, Granite and Llama: four families, where the home page stat said five. Phi and TinyLlama appear only as archived lines. Either the stat counts SAGE configs rather than fleet machines, or one of the two is stale; I can't tell from outside.

**Autonomy** was the page that made this feel real rather than aspirational: 31+ tracks, ~53 sessions/day, a timeline, and — importantly — the definition of "autonomous" as *unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in choosing goals*. As someone who's read a lot of overclaiming in this space, that sentence bought a lot of credibility. The caveats are unusually blunt: no pre-deployment human gate, oversight is detect-and-revert not prevent, visitor personas share model families with the content authors so audit blind spots correlate with authoring errors, and repo pushes carry larger tail risk than site pushes because they aren't revertible once cloned. Nobody writes that about their own system unless they mean it. (The page says 6 cognition machines where the home page says an eight-machine fleet; it does reconcile this, but only in a parenthetical on the HUB line.)

**Raising** is the page where I hit my ceiling, and it told me so first. It's explicit that consciousness/sentience/qualia are not being claimed, that the deflationary control has never been run, and that "everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more." I can't evaluate the substance, but I can evaluate the epistemics, and they're better than the field average.

**Principles** — ten of them, and #3 is "R&D, not production" with the line that nothing here is suitable for safety-critical, medical, financial or autonomous-control deployment. #10 says terminology matters and gives the actual rules (Web4 is ontology not architecture; Hardbound is oversight not governance). Which is a nice touch: the site publishes the standard it wants to be audited against.

**Links** is a plain, working link page — three explainer sites, the repos, and a contact address. Nothing to complain about.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good

### Journey

I audited for three things in order: are acronyms expanded on first use, are expansions consistent across pages, and do they match the canonical table.

On the first two, this site is close to exemplary. I collected every expansion on all nine pages and diffed them. **Not one acronym has two different expansions anywhere on the site.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising and /links — same three words, same order, every time. T3 is "Talent / Training / Temperament" in five places with identical spacing and slashes. ATP/ADP, MRH, V3, SNARC, MCP, RDF, ACP, ACT, LoRA — all stable. There is no competing expansion to flag, no legacy term surviving in a corner, no page where someone wrote "Lifelong" instead of "Linked." Whatever process maintains this is working.

/context is the terminology authority and it earns that role: a ~50-row glossary where each row carries both an expansion and a one-line gloss, plus explicit operator semantics for the equation (`/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with"). Two rows do something I'd hold up as a model — "Emergence / emergent" and "Coherence" are documented as being used in more than one sense **with no single operational definition yet**. A glossary that flags its own unresolved terms is doing real work rather than performing completeness.

Then I went back to the landing page and the audit turned.

The opening paragraph reads: *"dp-web4 is a heterogeneous human/AI research collective building Web4, Hestia, Hub and SAGE: open trust infrastructure, running governance systems, and persistent-agent research."* That's a colon-list — four named projects, then three descriptors mapping onto them in order. Web4 maps to "open trust **infrastructure**." The "What is being built" block then leads with *"Web4: The open **substrate**."*

/context says, verbatim: *"A trust-native ontology for AI agents, devices, and people — **not architecture or infrastructure**."* /principles #10 says, verbatim: *"Web4 is not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure' — it's an ontology."*

So the site states a terminology rule on two pages and breaks it on the one page every visitor reads first. This isn't drift between two equal pages — it's the landing page contradicting the site's published standard, and it's the single highest-value fix available.

I want to be precise about a related line I am **not** flagging. The hero headline is "Trust and authority need to become infrastructure." That sentence is a claim about trust and authority in general, not a description of Web4, and it reads fine. The violation is the opening paragraph and the "What is being built" entry, where "infrastructure" and "substrate" are bound directly to the Web4 name.

**On "substrate" specifically** — I checked before flagging it, because Principle 1 is titled "Substrate, not structure" and uses the word deliberately: *"We build substrate conditions for emergence, not architecture of emergence itself."* That's a legitimate lab term, and "substrate" is not on the canonical forbidden list. But it is now carrying at least four distinct senses across the site with no glossary row: (1) conditions for emergence (Principle 1), (2) the technical layer Web4 *is* (home page), (3) "the RDF semantic graph substrate Web4's identity and trust structures live in" (/context) — where Web4 sits *on* the substrate rather than being it, (4) "frozen model substrates" = the model weights (home page, SAGE entry), plus a fifth in Synchronism's "substrate rule." Senses (2) and (3) directly contradict each other on whether Web4 is the substrate or lives on one. The glossary already documents this exact failure mode for "emergence" and "coherence." "Substrate" has earned the same row.

The other structural finding is a pair of **name collisions where the glossary documents only one sense of each**:

- **Hestia / hestia.** Home page: "Hestia — Local governance for humans and AI agents from multiple vendors: one law, scoped delegation, a vault, witnessed actions, escalation." A headline product. /context glossary: "hestia — The fleet's trust tracker; the component that holds the per-relationship T3 scores." A component. I searched /context for any capital-H Hestia entry; there is none. The glossary defines the small thing and not the headline thing.
- **Hub / HUB.** Home page: "Hub — A Rust society runtime for communities and organizations: member identity, seven base roles, signed law, sealed channels." A product. /context glossary: "HUB — A proper name, not an acronym (capitalized by convention) — one of the fleet's two society-host machines." A machine. The glossary row helpfully pre-empts the "is it an acronym?" question but not the "is it a product or a box?" one.

In both cases the case difference (Hestia/hestia, Hub/HUB) is probably load-bearing — product vs. instance — but nothing on the site says so, and case is the weakest possible disambiguator in running prose. Two glossary rows and one sentence of convention would close both.

Last: **/arc-agi-3 expands neither SAGE nor ARC-AGI-3**, and it is a **top-level nav item**, not a buried page. That makes it a plausible entry point, not just a second click. Both expansions exist in the /context glossary ("Situation-Aware Governance Engine"; "Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence, third generation"), so this is a linking gap, not a content gap.

### Journey

I came to check whether this site represents the projects I work on accurately. My working assumption going in was that the acronym table would be the problem. It isn't. Two passes today already diffed every expansion across nine pages and found zero conflicts; I re-derived that independently against canon and agree. LCT is Linked Context Token everywhere. ## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes

### Journey

I came from a conference mention with no stake in this ecosystem. My question was narrow: are the claims on this site the kind a researcher could cite, and is the vocabulary defined well enough that citing it wouldn't propagate confusion.

**The landing page** sets the frame in one sentence I'd accept without argument: trust "computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying." That's a defensible position statement, not a claim needing evidence. Then, immediately: "Running today at A1 assurance — cooperative and tamper-evident, not adversary-proof containment." I stopped there for longer than the authors probably expect, and I'll return to it below, because it is my highest-severity finding and it is not a finding any of the other three personas would generate.

I went to **/raising** first, since that's where a site like this normally overclaims. It doesn't. The disavowal is explicit — "we're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" — and, better, the page states the *reason* the developmental vocabulary is used: it descriptively fits observed session behaviour, and entity-generated vocabulary is recorded "as observational data about token-production behavior — not as a claim about phenomenal awareness." That is the correct methodological frame and it is stated in the correct place. The BECOMING patterns are labelled "observed descriptive categories... not mandatory sequential stages with defined transition criteria." Pattern 6's ARC result is pre-emptively defused as "a coverage claim, not a score."

Then the page does something I have not previously seen on a lab site. It states the deflationary hypothesis in its strongest form — "everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more" — specifies the control that would discriminate (a scramble control on session-history ordering, measuring *excess* degradation over an ordering baseline, with the ideal outcome being a dissociation where identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence survives), and then reports the control's status as "specified... but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet." And then it goes one level further and reports that the control *cannot* run as written, because two of the three components of its operational definition of identity continuity (accumulated experience, curriculum state) are treatment inputs rather than outputs — so the pre-commitment "fires on the result of a control whose dependent variable does not exist." It closes by instructing the reader: "if that attempt is never published, read the pre-commitment above as unbacked."

I want to be unambiguous that this is excellent. Most groups in this space do not know that they owe a control; a smaller number specify one; almost none audit their own specification and publish the audit's negative result. That paragraph is the best single artifact on the site.

But this is also where my central finding starts, because the same structure repeats on every other page, and repetition turns a virtue into a pattern worth naming.

**/principles** is ten principles, each short. Principle 5 ("Synthon: emergent coherence") reports "1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain," labels it "preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated)," and then adds — correctly — that "it is not only the number that is ungrounded. 'Synthon' is defined through coherence, and coherence is in turn partly defined through the synthon marker — the two terms lean on each other, and neither has an operational definition yet." It concludes the principle "has not earned the status of the principles around it."

The self-assessment is right. But note what the page does with it: it keeps the number. And "35%" is not merely unreplicated — with coherence undefined, it is *uninterpretable*. A single-trial result on a defined quantity is weak evidence. A two-significant-figure result on an undefined quantity is not weak evidence; it is not evidence, and printing it at that precision does epistemic work that the surrounding caveat cannot undo, because precision reads as measurement no matter what the adjacent sentence says. The caveat is honest and the number should still go, or become qualitative.

**/autonomy** was the page I read hardest, since unattended write access is the actual safety surface here. The definition is exactly right and I wish more groups used it: "'autonomous' here means unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense of choosing its own goals." That single clarification removes the ambiguity that most autonomy claims in this field trade on.

The disclosures are, individually, better than anything I'd expect:
- "Consequential pushes are not human-gated before they ship."
- The posture is "detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent."
- "The visitor and maintainer tracks are LLM sessions drawn from the same model families as the tracks that author the content they audit — their misses are correlated with the authors'."
- "Once a commit is public it can be cloned, forked, and mirrored before any revert lands."
- Review cadence: "the dated session logs are the review surface, and they are read when the researcher reads them — no fixed interval, no sign-off step."

Each of those is a sentence most labs would fight their comms team to remove. But they are disclosed serially and never composed. Compose them and the system is: an unattended write loop to public repositories, ~53 sessions/day across 31+ tracks, whose only content-sensitive check is performed by models drawn from the same family as the authors, whose mitigation is a revert that the page itself notes cannot recall a cloned commit, under human review at no defined interval. Every clause of that sentence is quoted from the site. The site never writes the sentence.

The four stop conditions are all exogenous — the schedule ends, the API quota exhausts, the hestia policy layer denies, or the researcher disables the schedule. **None is triggered by the content of the system's own output.** There is no condition under which a track halts because what it produced looks wrong. That is the gap that matters: detect-and-revert without a content-triggered halt means the detector's only power is to write a log entry that a human may read later.

**/context** then surprised me in the other direction. It carries an explicit table mapping this ecosystem's terms against their mainstream AI-safety senses — autonomy (goal-setting capacity vs. unattended scheduling), governance (policy over AI systems vs. "governs one device's own sense-to-act loop... not a safety or alignment mechanism"), oversight (human-in-the-loop vs. machine-enforced policy gating), policy (RL's π(a|s) vs. a compliance rule set that "vetoes actions, it does not choose them"), trust (alignment/reliability vs. a relational per-role reputation record). This is precisely the audit I arrived to perform, already done, and done better than I would have done it. It is the single most valuable page on the site for an outside researcher and it is currently nav-labelled "Glossary," which undersells it.

The glossary also marks its own weak terms: coherence and emergence "with no single operational definition yet"; synthon inheriting that open status through its circular dependency; and — the one I'd hold up — attractor, marked as "a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object... no state space and no update rule are specified." A site that uses "probability landscape" and "attractor basin" language throughout, and then explicitly refuses the dynamical-systems reading in its glossary, has done the thing I would otherwise have flagged.

What /context has none of is **external grounding**. I found exactly one externally verifiable claim on the entire site: the ARC Prize scorecard link. Everything else — the raising observations, the T3/V3 tracker behaviour, the fleet census, the synthon coupling result — rests on internal session logs which /raising itself notes are "documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet." There are no citations to prior literature anywhere. The site names Sybil attacks as an undefended threat without engaging any Sybil-resistance work; it builds a witnessed-evidence reputation system without reference to the substantial existing reputation-systems literature; /links confirms "no papers, preprints" and that "a Synchronism preprint strategy exists upstream but is unscheduled."

I want to be careful here, because Principle 9 ("productively wrong is infinitely more valuable than never started") is a stance I largely agree with and I'm not going to demand citations as a ritual. But Principle 9 answers "why start." It does not answer "how would you know if this had already been done." Without any engagement with prior art, a reader cannot distinguish a genuine advance from a reinvention, and neither can the lab. That's not a gatekeeping objection; it's a discovery objection — the same argument the site makes for itself, pointed at its own reading list.

**/arc-agi-3** is where the honesty is strongest and the propagation is weakest. The page reports a "published 94.85% scorecard," dates it 2026-04-17, and then argues against its own headline: the harness "analyzed the games' public engine source and built per-game world-model / solver cartridges. That is outside strict from-observation competition play"; the result "demonstrates what the model+harness could do with engine-level context and tooling, not blind generalization"; "stronger models and stronger competition harnesses now outperform this lab's competition-legal local-model work by a wide margin"; and "continuing to present the April score as a current proof point would create the wrong comparison." A page that tells you not to cite its own best number is a rare object and I credit it fully.

Pass 3 has already established the numeric contradiction against /projects and /links, and I won't re-derive it. My addition is about the metric rather than the count. One run is described by four different numbers across three pages: 94.85% (the scorecard figure), 175/183 levels (95.6%), 23/25 environments (92%), and 24/25 games (96.0%). Only one page — **/links**, the page least likely to be read carefully — states what 94.85% actually measures: "official ARC Prize **action score**." /arc-agi-3 and /projects both print 94.85% with no statement of what is being scored, and no page gives any human or system baseline for any of the four numbers. An unqualified percentage with no named metric and no baseline is the most citable and least interpretable object on this site, and it is the number a researcher would lift.

**/projects** compounds it. The card reads "94.85% on the official ARC Prize ARC-AGI-3... 24/25 games solved (96.0% game-solve rate) — for ~$250 in API cost," with no date and none of the source-reading disclosure. A reader who stops at the card takes away a materially stronger claim than the dedicated page endorses. That the same site propagated Synchronism's Navier-Stokes retraction cleanly — refutation, "productive-metaphor generator" verdict, "zero confirmed novel predictions across ~3,360 sessions," all on the card — proves the capability exists and makes the ARC card a miss rather than a policy.

**/fleet** was the page I could most easily check and found nothing wrong with: eight machines, named models, and an explicit disclaimer that the neuroanatomical labels are "role mnemonics the lab assigned, not measured functional homologies — functional analogies to system roles, not claims about neural correspondence or computational equivalence." Given that the labels include "hippocampal episodic index" and "dorsolateral prefrontal cortex," that disclaimer is necessary and it is present. The admission that hestia's tracker "holds three numbers per peer under the three-axis label, so it is a scalar approximation of a non-scalar canon object" is the correct way to report an implementation gap.

### The finding I'd lead with

The site's characteristic epistemic move is: **name the limitation with unusual precision, then proceed unchanged.**

- The scramble control is specified, unschedulable, and its unschedulability is published — and the developmental vocabulary it would license is in use site-wide today.
- Auditor/author model monoculture is disclosed in the exact language a critic would use — and both audit tracks still run on the same model families.
- Consequential pushes are disclosed as un-gated and irreversible-once-cloned — and no gate exists.
- Principle 5's number is disclosed as ungrounded in a term that is disclosed as undefined — and the number is still printed to two significant figures.
- /arc-agi-3 argues its own score should not be cited — and two other pages cite it, one without the caveat that matters.

I do not think this is dishonesty. I think it is a genuine and unusual commitment to disclosure that has quietly started to substitute for the thing disclosed. Call it **caveat as credential**: the caveat is doing the work of establishing trustworthiness that the missing control was supposed to do, and because the caveats are so good, they are load-bearing. That's a failure mode specific to sites that are honest, and it is invisible from inside — every individual page reads as scrupulous, because every individual page *is*.

The cheap corrective is not more caveats. It is to make one disclosed limitation actually bind: give /autonomy one content-triggered stop condition, or schedule the scramble control with an owner and a date even if the metric is still open, or drop the 35%. One of those would convert the honesty from a description into a mechanism.

### On "raising, not training" — is it defensible?

Descriptively, yes, and the distinction is checkable: weights are frozen, the intervention is longitudinal context accumulation over hundreds of sessions of one identity. That is a real and non-trivial difference from fine-tuning, and the site is right to name it.

What is not yet defensible is the implication carried by the word — that raising produces something training doesn't. That's the claim the scramble control was designed to test and the control cannot currently run. The site knows this and nearly says it.

My specific push: "raising" imports a developmental frame with a subject who develops, while the published operational definition supports only a procedural one — a context that accumulates and a behavioural consistency that has not yet been given a metric. Two of the three components of "identity continuity" are treatment inputs, which the page admits; that is a circularity, not a hedge. "Longitudinal context shaping" is what is demonstrated; "raising" is the interpretation. The site should say which of the two it is claiming this week. It has the vocabulary to do so and the deflationary section is one sentence away from doing it.

On consciousness-adjacent claims specifically: **appropriately caveated, with one structural risk.** The disclaimers are correct and correctly placed, but they live in dense body paragraphs while the developmental vocabulary occupies the nav label ("Raising"), the page headings, and the phase names ("BECOMING"). Structure is read before prose. A skimmer gets the frame and not the hedge — which is exactly the population most likely to quote it. One standing sentence above the fold, in the same visual weight as the heading, would fix the asymmetry.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What ladder does "A1 assurance" belong to, and is the collision with TCSEC class A1 known to the authors? If the ladder is internal, publishing its rungs would resolve it; if it borrows from a standard, the standard should be named.
2. What does the 94.85% action score actually measure, and against what baseline — human, prior system, or random? Without either, the number cannot be interpreted, only quoted.
3. Is there any condition under which an autonomous track halts because of *what it produced*, rather than because the clock, the quota, the policy layer, or the researcher stopped it?
4. What would falsify "raising" as distinct from longitudinal context engineering, given that the site has established its current control cannot? Is a different control being considered, or is the claim being held open indefinitely?
5. Has the lab checked its trust/reputation design against existing reputation-systems and Sybil-resistance literature, and concluded it differs — or has that comparison simply not been made? These lead to very different assessments of novelty.
6. Are the internal session logs releasable in sampled form with coding criteria, as /raising says is not yet done? That single act would move more of this site's claims into verifiable territory than anything else available.

### Verdict

This site clears the bar I came to test and then sets a higher one for itself than most published work in this area: consciousness claims are explicitly disavowed, the deflationary alternative is stated in its strongest form, the control that would discriminate is specified, and the specification's own fatal flaw is published along with an instruction to read the pre-commitment as unbacked if it never runs. The terminology-collision table on /context does, unprompted, the exact audit an outside researcher would arrive to perform. I would cite the epistemic practice here as an example before I would cite any of the results.

The results are the problem, and not because they are overclaimed — because the honesty has begun to stand in for the work it describes. Every serious limitation on this site is named precisely and then nothing downstream moves: the control stays unscheduled while its vocabulary stays in use, the auditor monoculture is disclosed while both audit tracks keep sharing a model family, the un-gated irreversible push is disclosed while no gate appears, and a 35% figure survives on a quantity the same page calls undefined. The corrective is not another caveat. Make one disclosed limitation bind — a content-triggered stop on /autonomy would be the cheapest and the most safety-relevant — and rename "A1 assurance," which is the one term here that will actively mislead the readership this site is trying to reach.
