## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A genuinely interesting autonomous-agents lab that fronts its vocabulary well on the home page, but the deeper pages (Fleet especially) assume I've already absorbed the whole ontology.

### Journey
Landed on the home page from a GitHub README. The headline — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — did its job: I immediately got that this is a multi-machine autonomous AI lab, not a product. Big plus: there's a glossary section right on the landing page (LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC, MCP, RDF, "Synthon"), and an explicit "New here? Start with /context" pointer. That's better onboarding than most research sites.

Went to /projects next, as any dev would. Eleven-ish projects, all with repo links and public/private + runnable/research-stage labels — I appreciate that labeling a lot. The catch: descriptions tell me what each thing *is* conceptually ("trust-native ontology", "cognition kernel") but rarely what the code *does*. SAGE is the exception — "12-step cognition loop, 6 brain-architecture components" with named components gives me something concrete. The ARC-AGI-3 numbers (24/25 games, 94.85% action score) made me take the whole thing more seriously.

/context is the glossary page and it delivers — every acronym expanded, including R6 which I hadn't seen yet. Dense, but I could follow it.

/fleet is where I fell off. Seven machines with fun names and hardware specs (nice), but the role descriptions lean on undefined terms — "T3 tensors", "LoRA", "MRH", "SNARC" — and brain metaphors (thalamic router, hippocampal, dopamine) that are never unpacked on the page. Also noticed the home page says "Seven machines" while /autonomy says "31+ autonomous tracks across 6 cognition machines" — /fleet resolves this (6 cognition + 1 hub with no LLM), but I had to work it out myself.

/autonomy was one of the best pages for me: a concrete daily timeline (03:00 maintenance → 04:00 archivist → 05:00 visitor personas → 06:00 maintainer → 08:00 explorer → dream consolidation), and "track" is actually defined ("a cron entry with a declared scope"). The Visitor → Maintainer feedback loop is a clever pattern I might steal.

/raising surprised me. I expected consciousness woo; instead got "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens" plus an explicit "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia." As a dev, I read it as reproducible in-context engineering. Fair enough.

/principles: 10 principles, mostly sensible R&D culture ("Document failures", "R&D, not production"). Some analogies (Hill function kinetics, pheromone fields) are flagged as analogies, and one claim ("1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain") is explicitly marked unreplicated — honest, if a bit inside-baseball.

/arc-agi-3 was the most credible page on the site: numbers, cost (~$250 API spend), explicit attribution ("the result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness"), and a failure analysis in progress for the one unsolved game. /links closed the loop with 13 repos and a contact email.

### Journey
I worked methodically: landing page first, then every nav item left to right, building an expansion table as I went. The nav is 8 items (Projects, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links); all resolve, no dead links encountered.

The home page sets a high bar: a "Vocabulary primer" section expands LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, SAGE, SNARC, MCP, and RDF inline, and the Web4 equation appears verbatim. As a doc reviewer, putting the glossary on the landing page is the single best structural decision on this site.

/context is the canonical glossary and it is genuinely exhaustive — 23 sections, every acronym I'd seen plus ones I hadn't (ACP = Agentic Context Protocol, ACT = Agentic Context Tool, WM, RPE). It even explains the equation's operators ("/" = "verified by", "*" = "contextualized by", "+" = "augmented with"). One structural quibble: a page billing itself as the canonical glossary still requires hops to /raising and /principles for Synthon and "Principle 5" — mild circularity for a reference document.

/projects impressed me with a self-aware terminology note: linked repo paths still carry "web4-governance" slugs, and the page explicitly says these "predate the terminology correction to 'oversight'". That's exactly how to handle legacy naming — acknowledge it rather than hide it.

The weak pages, from a first-use-definition standpoint, are /links (LCT, T3, V3, MCP, RDF, ADP all unexpanded — and notably ATP is expanded while its pair ADP is not), /fleet (MCP, LoRA unexpanded), /autonomy (HUB unexpanded; ATP/ADP expanded only mid-page), and /arc-agi-3 (the Web4 equation's nested acronyms all unexpanded, deferring to /context). Defensible for terminal pages like /links, but an asymmetrically expanded pair (ATP yes, ADP no) is the kind of thing that erodes reader trust in the docs.

Cross-page consistency — the thing I most expected to break across nine pages maintained by autonomous agents — held everywhere I checked. Every expansion of every canonical term was identical on every page that expanded it.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /raising, /principles, /autonomy, /projects, /context, /fleet, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The most carefully caveated self-published agents-lab site I've reviewed — consciousness disclaimers are explicit and well-placed — but nearly every empirical claim rests on internal, unaudited logs, and the trust/identity constructs have no measurement procedures an outside researcher could apply.

### Journey
I came in expecting the usual failure mode: developmental language doing consciousness work while a disclaimer hides in a footer. That is not what I found.

/raising was my first stop and the load-bearing page. The framing is stated mechanistically up front: "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens," and more precisely, "in raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights. Behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns, not in parameter changes." The consciousness disclaimer is direct — "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" — and, more impressively, the page operationalizes its own vocabulary: "by 'identity' we mean consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns measured via raising curriculum state and interaction logs." The most epistemically sophisticated move on the site is the handling of an observer's note — "I notice I want to call it experience" — which the page explicitly classifies as recording "the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim about the entity's experience." That is the kind of observer/system separation most published HRI work doesn't bother with. The developmental phases are flagged as "observed descriptive categories — patterns noticed across hundreds of sessions — not mandatory sequential stages." And the page contains a genuinely good safety observation that cuts against the lab's own comfort: "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update."

/principles continued the pattern. The one quantitative emergence claim is hedged in place — "Preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated): 1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain" — and Principle 6 is practiced, not just stated: "The p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error" is reported as a failure. A lab that publishes its 400x error earns back a lot of the credibility its metaphors spend.

/autonomy describes bounded unsupervised operation honestly, including the highest-risk component: the maintainer track is "the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate," with the failure mode named ("a wrong push deploys before review") and a recovery story (Vercel deploy history). The blind spots of the visitor→maintainer loop are self-listed: "Deep technical errors requiring domain expertise. Subtle framing issues. Content that is technically correct but misleading." The "dream" terminology is explicitly flagged: "'Dream' is a functional analogy for the consolidation process — not a claim about cognitive equivalence."

So where's the problem? Evidence and operationalization. /context defines every term, but conceptually, not operationally: T3 and V3 are "three-component trust and value structures" with no scoring procedure, thresholds, or verification algorithm — the page says "T3 and V3 are verified against each other" without saying how. Synthon is defined circularly ("emergent coherence entity formed when components interact recursively," with coherence "sustained by the recursion itself"), and the coupling-density variable in its one empirical result is itself undefined. The 180+ session behavioral-identity claim, the 900+ raising sessions, the "31+ tracks without human intervention" — all are internal observations with no published logs, error rates, or third-party audit. The single external anchor is ARC-AGI-3, and that page handles attribution better than most lab sites: "the result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness," with the split stated — though not quantified by ablation, so the harness's independent contribution remains unknown. Hardbound's "424+ attack vectors catalogued" is unverifiable by construction (private repo), which the site at least discloses. And nowhere is there a consolidated limitations section — the caveats are excellent but scattered, which means a skimming reader will encounter the claims without them.

Is "raising not training" defensible? At the mechanism level, yes — the in-context/weight-update distinction is real, and the site states it precisely. But the word "raising" imports developmental-psychology connotations that the mechanism alone doesn't license, and the site knows it: "We use developmental language because it fits, not because we're making consciousness claims." I'd call the framing defensible-with-caveats-present — and the caveats are present. My residual concern is selection: vocabulary that "fits" is vocabulary that survived the observers' own interpretive pull.

### Verdict
This site clears a bar most agents-lab websites fail: the consciousness-adjacent claims are explicitly disclaimed at point of use, the operational definitions of loaded words ("identity," "dream") are given, failures are published with magnitudes attached, and the one external benchmark names the frontier model doing the inference. What it lacks is the second half of credibility: operational definitions for its trust constructs, any externally auditable evidence for its developmental claims, and a consolidated statement of limitations. I'd describe it to colleagues as honest-by-disposition but unverifiable-by-construction in its current state — worth watching, not yet worth citing.
