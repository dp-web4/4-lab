## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The site tells me clearly what the lab is and hands me a clone command — but when I followed the clone command to its stated source of truth, that source doesn't exist anymore.

### Journey

Landed on `/` from a repo README. The hero does its job in about fifteen seconds: "AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become first-class." That's a problem statement I recognize — I've shipped agent tooling and the auth story is always duct tape. The one-liner under it ("trust should be computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying — not declared by a platform or asserted by the agent itself") is the clearest sentence on the whole site. If I only read one line, that's the right one.

The three hero stats are where I first got a weird feeling. Stat one is "8 machines." Fine. Stat two is "21 SAGE instances configured across the fleet" — and then the stat's own caption tells me it's an internal census, more than the 8 actually running, and "not yet published anywhere a reader can check." I respect the honesty enormously, but as a developer scanning for 30 seconds, the second-most-prominent number on the landing page is one the site tells me not to trust. Then a follow-up paragraph tells me `/fleet` is "the checkable version of these numbers." So why is the uncheckable one in the big font?

The four "What is being built" cards worked well. Web4 = the ontology, Hestia = local governance daemon, Hub = Rust society runtime, SAGE = persistent-agent research. Four names, four one-liners, no overlap. I could hold that in my head. The Hestia card even pre-empts a confusion I would have had — it says its "A1" is on Hestia's own A0–A4 ladder and is *inverted* against the Orange Book TCSEC A1 where A1 is the top class. I'd never have caught that on my own and it would have made me overrate the thing.

"Start here" gave me three cards: Calibration, Vocabulary, Full ecosystem. I clicked Calibration expecting a page on this site and got dropped into `github.com/dp-web4/web4/blob/main/STATUS.md` — a raw markdown file on GitHub. It resolves (200), and it's arguably the right content, but there's no external-link cue in the card, and the site's *own* `/context` has an "Evidence & limitations" section that reads like exactly what "Calibration" promised. Mild bait-and-switch.

Went to `/projects` next because that's where the "what do I clone" answer usually lives. Thirteen project cards, each tagged `public`/`private` and `runnable`/`research-stage`. That two-axis tag is the single best design decision on the site — I know instantly that Web4 is a spec repo with no demo and SAGE is the thing that actually runs. The page even defines `runnable` as "clone it and it executes end to end today (a demo, a script, a server) — not a claim about polish or completeness." That's the sentence I wish every research repo had.

Some cards are extremely long. The Synchronism card is a wall of self-correction — refuted predictions, a superseded equation, a "productive-metaphor generator" verdict from its own audit. I skipped most of it, which I suspect is fine; it's clearly not what I'd clone.

The Quick start at the bottom of `/projects` is where I actually tried to do something. It says step 0 is dependencies, and that **"the SAGE README's Requirements section is authoritative."** The fuller version on `/links` gives the same commands and says **"Commands transcribed from the SAGE README's Getting Started section, which is the maintained version if these drift."**

So I opened the SAGE README. It has no Getting Started section. It has no Requirements section. It has **zero shell commands in the entire file** — I checked; there is not a single `git clone`, `cargo`, `python`, or `ollama` invocation anywhere in it. The README is a well-written research overview (Where SAGE fits / The research question / Core architecture / Governance and assurance / Five-minute audit / What SAGE is not) that was clearly rewritten at some point, and the sections the site cites as its source of truth went with it. The commands on the site may still work perfectly — I have no way to tell, and neither does the README.

Same story one line down. Both quick starts say for ACT: "See README for Go/Cosmos SDK setup." The ACT repo has no README.md at all — not on `main`, not on `master`, not in the repo root file listing. The repo page loads, there are 30+ markdown files in the root, none of them is a README. For the "human interface to Web4," described as one of the two most accessible entry points, that's a dead stop.

This is the thing I'd want fixed above everything else on this site. Everything else is words about the work; this is the one place the site tells me to *do* something, and the handoff drops.

Ironically, the SAGE README does have a great developer entry point the site never mentions: a "Five-minute audit" section — six numbered links, in order, for someone evaluating the project. That's exactly what I wanted and the site pointed me past it.

`/fleet` was the most fun page. Eight machines, each with hardware, model, session count, and a brain-analogy role (thalamic router, cerebellum/habit compiler, interoception/metacognition). The Jetson Orin Nano 8GB running a 0.8B model next to a Jetson AGX Thor with 122GB unified memory is a real heterogeneity story, not a diagram. And "Gemma 3 at 4B outperforms Phi-4 at 14B for raising work" is the kind of concrete negative-ish finding I'd actually repeat to a coworker. I added up the per-machine session counts out of curiosity: 435+488+336+377+162+193 = **1,991**, which is exactly the number `/projects` and `/links` both cite, and +74 for HUB = 2,065, also cited. The numbers reconcile. That bought a lot of credibility.

I also spot-checked the repo counts on `/links` against the live GitHub account: the site says 21 public originals and 32 forks; the API returns 21 non-fork public repos and 32 forks. Exact match. (Small nit: the site calls it "the dp-web4 org" / "dp-web4 (GitHub org)" — the GitHub API returns it as a *user* account, not an organization. Cosmetic, but a developer clicking through lands on a personal profile page.)

`/autonomy` explained the cron-driven track system, which is the part of this I could most easily imagine building myself. The single most useful sentence: tracks are Claude Code sessions launched by cron to maintain repos and sites, while SAGE is a *separate* on-device kernel on each machine's local model — "the two share machines and repos, not a process." I had conflated them right up until that sentence, and I'd bet most first-time readers do too. The page also volunteers that the maintainer track pushes to production with no human gate, that its posture is detect-and-revert rather than prevent, and that across 79 sessions and 117 commits there have been zero rollbacks — while immediately explaining why zero is a weak number ("the only automated detector is the same daily loop being evaluated"). I did not expect a site to argue against its own best statistic.

`/raising` and `/principles` are the pages where I bounced. Not because they're wrong — because every claim is wrapped in two or three qualifying clauses, and the qualifications are nested. `/raising` has a section arguing that its own pre-registered commitment is "unreachable as written," followed by a second commitment about the first one. I understand *why* it's written that way. It's still a page I'd have to sit down with coffee to parse, and I was scanning.

`/arc-agi-3` was short and clean by comparison, and I noticed the browser tab said "4-lab | Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop" — the home page's title, not the ARC page's. Every other page has its own `<title>`. Small bug, but I bookmark things.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Do the quick-start commands still work? The site says the SAGE README is authoritative if they drift, but the README no longer has the section — so there is currently no way for me or the site to detect drift.
2. What is the entry point for ACT, given it has no README?
3. "Zero crystallization achieved" on Sprout — is that the goal state or a failure? The word does the work of a metric and I can't tell its sign from /fleet.
4. If I clone SAGE and run it on one machine with no fleet, what do I actually get? 5. The site says there's no CONTRIBUTING guide and no policy on outside PRs. Is outside code wanted at all right now, or is "ask in an issue first" a polite no?
6. Which repo is Hestia's `A1` gate actually implemented in, and can I run it standalone to govern my own agents? It's pitched as the most immediately useful thing here for someone with Claude Code and Cursor on one box, but it's not in either quick start.

### Verdict

This is the most intellectually honest research site I've read, and the honesty is not decorative — the numbers reconcile when you check them (session counts sum correctly across three pages; repo counts match the live GitHub account exactly), and the pages argue against their own strongest statistics. I came in expecting vapor and left thinking there's real engineering here.

The gap is between the writing and the doing. The site spends thousands of words establishing epistemic care, then the one place it asks me to run something points at two README sections that don't exist and one README that doesn't exist. For a developer, that inverts the credibility the rest of the site earned: a project this careful about caveating a coherence metric should not have a broken clone path. Fix the two quick-start pointers and this goes from "interesting to read" to "interesting to try."

Would return: yes, but I'd go to `/fleet` and `/autonomy` first — those are the pages where this lab is doing something I can't easily do myself.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /principles, /autonomy, /links, /fleet, /raising, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Acronym discipline is the best I have audited — zero expansion drift across nine pages and full agreement with the governing canon doc — but the site's internal cross-references have started pointing at things that aren't there, and seven of nine pages have no `<h1>`.

### Journey

I audited the rendered pages, then re-checked every load-bearing string against the live HTML rather than trusting a summarizer's paraphrase. Where the site names an external source of truth, I fetched that source and diffed against it.

Start with the good news, because it is substantial and it is the thing most sites fail.

**Every canonical acronym expansion on this site is correct, and every page agrees with every other page.** I checked LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, R7, MCP, RDF, ACP, ACT, SNARC, LoRA, TPM, FIDO2 and MIT-0 across all nine pages. Not one deviation. No "Lifelong Capability Token," no "Minimum Response Horizon," no "Attention Token Pool." The phrase "production ready" appears nowhere. Web4 is called an ontology on every page that defines it, and three pages explicitly negate "architecture," "infrastructure" and "stack." "Fractal leverage" is used correctly and is paired with the negation "not unification, and not scope inflation" on both pages that use it. The term-enforcement problem this track was built to catch appears to be solved.

`/context` is genuinely excellent reference documentation. It opens by naming its own source of truth — `CANONICAL_TERMS_v1.md` in the public web4 repo — and states "When this page and that document disagree, the document governs." I fetched that document. It resolves (200), and the site's expansions match it term for term. More than that: `/context`'s R6/R7 section reproduces canon's subtle point correctly — that R6 and R7 are *both* canonical, that neither is deprecated, and that the choice is contextual by consequence tier. Most secondary documentation flattens that into "R6, and also R7 exists." This page doesn't, and it goes one step further with a sentence I'd frame: *"wherever the prose says trust 'updates from interaction,' the action grammar underneath is R7, not bare R6."* That is a writer thinking about how a reader will misread them.

The page also carries a dated provenance correction on the equation legend — noting that the `/` `*` `+` glosses were "previously described here as canon's. It is the site's gloss." Documentation that timestamps its own corrections is rare.

So the terminology layer is in good shape. The problems I found are one level up: **cross-references and document structure.**

**The headline: two pages cite a home-page section that does not exist.**

`/context`'s Ontology entry says: *"see the home page's Vocabulary Primer for the fuller gloss ('An ontology (shared vocabulary + relationships) for how AI agents prove identity, earn trust, and account for resources — not a blockchain, not a platform')."*

`/projects` says: *"On 'not a blockchain' (the home page's Web4 framing)..."*

The home page contains no section called "Vocabulary Primer." It has a card labeled "Vocabulary" whose entire body is one line: "The glossary for LCT, T3/V3, MRH, R6/R7, ATP/ADP and related terms." The string "not a blockchain" appears **zero times** on the home page. The quoted gloss actually lives on `/projects`, in the ACT card.

So two separate pages send the reader to `/` for a framing that `/` does not contain, and one of them quotes it as if reproducing it. This looks like residue from a home-page rewrite: the copy moved, the pointers didn't. It is the same failure mode Pass 1 found pointing at external READMEs, except this one is entirely inside the site and therefore entirely fixable.

**Structural finding: seven of nine pages have no `<h1>`.**

Only `/` and `/arc-agi-3` have one. `/projects`, `/context`, `/principles`, `/autonomy`, `/links`, `/fleet` and `/raising` all begin their heading hierarchy at `<h2>`. For a screen-reader user or anything consuming the document outline, each of those pages presents as a fragment with no title. `/context` in particular is 27 consecutive `<h3>` sections under a single `<h2>` — a flat list where a reference document of that length needs grouping.

The inverse bug sits on `/arc-agi-3`: it has a proper `<h1>` ("ARC-AGI-3 - spring 2026 research record") but no page-specific `<title>` — it inherits the root layout's "4-lab | Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop." Every other page has a correct `<title>`. So the two metadata mechanisms are each broken on the complementary set of pages.

**Precision finding: R6 appears alone in two places where the site's own rule says R7.**

`/context` establishes that trust-updating actions use R7. But `/links` describes Hardbound as providing *"R6-auditable actions (R6 = the Six-Element Action Framework...)"* and `/projects` describes the ARC work as producing *"R6 ... audit trails."* Auditing and trust attribution are exactly the consequence tier canon reserves for R7. Neither page mentions R7 exists. Meanwhile the home page's Vocabulary card promises "R6/R7." So the site names R7 on `/`, defines it fully and correctly on `/context`, and then omits it from the two pages that describe R-framework usage in the field.

To be clear: R6 is canonical and the expansions given are correct. This is an incompleteness, not drift.

**Consistency finding: the equation legend has forked.**

`/context`: *"/ = 'verified by' on both sides of the equation: T3 verified by V3, ATP verified by the ADP discharge record that closes it. One glyph, one meaning. 'Both sides' means both quotients in the equation read alike — it is not a claim that T3 and V3 verify each other."* Plus the dated provenance correction.

`/projects`: *"/ = 'verified by' on both sides of the equation (T3 by V3; ATP by its ADP discharge record)"* — and nothing else.

Same legend, two versions. The `/projects` one lacks the disambiguation that `/context` evidently added because someone misread it, and lacks the provenance note establishing it as the site's gloss rather than canon's. A reader who only sees `/projects` gets the pre-correction version presented as authoritative.

**Terminology finding: the Hestia "governance" exception is documented in exactly one place.**

The site's Principle 10 states the rule flatly: *"Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight."* Every legacy use of "governance" on the site carries an inline disclaimer — SAGE's name gets one on six separate pages, the `web4-governance` repo slugs get one, even hestia's internal `governance-response` field name gets one.

Hestia does not. The home page says *"Local governance for humans and AI agents,"* *"running governance systems,"* and *"for local agent governance see Hestia"* — three uses, no note. `/projects` says *"Open local-first governance daemon"* — no note.

There is a good reason, and `/context` gives it: *"Hestia adjudicates — it decides, at the moment of the act. That is why this site calls Hestia's function governance and does not rewrite it to oversight: the governance-to-oversight terminology rule binds Hardbound, which gates and reverts against a hardware root."* That is a correct and well-argued distinction — adjudication is not oversight. But it lives only on `/context`, and it is the one governance use on the site that *isn't* flagged. A reader who has just read Principle 10 and lands on the home page will read "Local governance" as the drift the site elsewhere polices. The exception needs a half-sentence where it is used, not only where it is defended.

**Definition-locality gaps.** `MIT-0` is used on `/projects`, `/principles` and `/raising` and expanded only on `/links` ("MIT No Attribution"). On two of those pages it is load-bearing for a credibility argument — "the code these principles produced is public and MIT-0." `RDF` is used bare on the home page ("Web4 lives on an RDF graph") and never expanded there, though it is expanded correctly on five other pages. `/projects` opens with the full equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` before a single term has been expanded on that page — it does warn "This is the most acronym-dense page on the site" and points to the glossary, which is a reasonable mitigation, but the most prominent element on the page is still eight unexpanded acronyms.

**What I checked and found clean.** All internal links return 200; nav is complete and consistent across all nine pages. No literal HTML, entities, `[[...]]`, `undefined` or `NaN` leaking into rendered text. The apparent Sprout model conflict (`/raising` says Qwen 0.5B, `/fleet` says Qwen 3.5 0.8B) is explicitly disclosed in place — `/raising` says "a model-line count, not a machine total — the Sprout box now runs Qwen 3.5 0.8B." That is the correct way to handle a historical figure and I want it noted as a positive.

- **Overall impression**: The most rigorously self-caveated research site I have read — and that is exactly the problem, because the caveats are the only thing a reader can actually check.

### Journey

I came in from a conference mention, with the prior I bring to every self-published AI lab: assume the claims are load-bearing metaphors until shown otherwise. I read `/raising`, `/principles` and `/autonomy` closely, `/context` as the reference layer, and treated the rest as corroboration. Then I did the one thing that separates a credible research site from a well-written one: I tried to check something.

**The hedging is real, structural, and better than most published work.**

`/principles` does not bury its disclaimer in a footnote. It prefaces all ten principles with the scope statement — developmental and identity language "describe functional system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims." `/raising` closes with an explicit "What we're not claiming" section that names consciousness, sentience and qualia and disclaims all three. `/fleet` pre-empts the misreading its own design invites: the brain labels are "role mnemonics the lab assigned, not measured functional homologies." I went looking for the usual tell — a caveat that appears once, far from the claims it is supposed to govern — and did not find it. The disclaimers are placed where the reader hits them.

**Three moves here are genuinely rare, and I want them recorded as strengths before I get to the criticism.**

*First, the deflationary account is named as the leading hypothesis, not as a strawman.* `/raising` states, near the top, that everything on the page "might be competent context engineering and nothing more," and that the control which would discriminate the two has not been run. Almost every lab in this space names the boring explanation only to dismiss it in the next clause. This page names it and then does not dismiss it. That single sentence bought more credibility with me than the entire fleet page.

*Second, the site catches its own pre-registration being vacuous.* `/raising` carries a pre-commitment to publish the identity-continuity result "including if it fails" — and then argues that this commitment is **unreachable as written**, because the dependent variable has no operational definition, so there is nothing the control could adjudicate. A lab that notices its own pre-registration is empty, in public, before anyone else points it out, is doing something I have not seen outside a handful of metascience groups.

*Third, `/autonomy` argues against its own best statistic.* Seventy-nine sessions, 117 commits, zero rollbacks — and the page immediately says the zero is "equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes,'" because the only detector is the loop being evaluated. Then it goes further and admits the auditing tracks draw from the same model families as the authoring tracks, so "their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent of them." That is the central failure mode of self-evaluating AI systems, stated by the self-evaluating AI system.

`/context`'s "Evidence & limitations" section is the best thing on the site and I would show it to colleagues as a template. It grades claims onto an explicit ladder — externally scored / internally logged but unaudited / single-trial unreplicated — and then says the thing almost nobody says: *"every check described on this site today is run by the fleet on itself... For a lab whose research subject is trust and oversight, that is a real gap, not a footnote."*

So: the intellectual honesty is not decorative. Now the criticism, and it is structural rather than a list of errors.

**Finding 1 — The honesty is entirely declarative. There is exactly one externally checkable claim, and it is the one the site has retired.**

Every caveat on this site is a statement *about* evidence I cannot see. `/context` is explicit: the raising phases, the 180+ session identity-continuity claim, identity portability, fleet capacity, and Hardbound's attack surface all rest on internal logs that are "documented and dated, but not externally audited — no published log samples, coding criteria, or third-party review yet." No papers, no preprints, no datasets, no redacted log samples. `/links` confirms it and adds that the Synchronism preprint is "unscheduled."

That leaves one claim an outsider can verify: the ARC-AGI-3 result, 94.85% action score, with a third-party ARC Prize scorecard link. And `/arc-agi-3` itself explains why that number does not support the thing the lab now cares about: the harness "analyzed the games' public engine source and built per-game world-model / solver cartridges," so the result "demonstrates what the model+harness could do with engine-level context and tooling, not blind generalization." `/context` adds that "the harness-vs-model split is stated but not quantified; no ablation has been run."

Compose those: the site's only externally verifiable claim is verifiable *as a number* but not as evidence for local-model capability, and the site has correctly moved it to a historical page. Which means the lab's entire current research programme — raising, identity continuity, synthons, fleet cognition — sits at zero external verifiability, and the site says so accurately in the one place a reader is least likely to read.

I want to be careful about what this does and does not mean. It does **not** mean the claims are wrong; unaudited is not refuted. It means the site's credibility currently rests on the quality of its self-criticism rather than on any evidence, and self-criticism is a signal that can be produced by a sufficiently careful writer with nothing behind it. The site has made itself maximally honest and thereby maximally unfalsifiable from the outside. `/context` half-says this. It should say it in one sentence on the landing page.

**Finding 2 — The lab does not apply its own ontology to its own claims. This is the sharpest thing I can say.**

Web4's thesis, stated in the hero: trust should be *computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying* — not asserted by the actor. V3 (Valuation / Veracity / Validity) is a schema for exactly that. The lab has built a vocabulary for attaching machine-readable evidence status to assertions.

And then it publishes its own assertions as unstructured prose hedges. Every claim on this site is *asserted by the actor*, with the evidence grade in a paragraph on a different page. A reader "doing the relying" cannot compute anything; they can only read carefully and trust the writer's care.

This is not a rhetorical gotcha — it is a concrete, buildable suggestion and it is the single highest-value change available. `/context`'s ladder already defines the tiers. Render them inline as a badge or superscript on every quantitative claim site-wide: `[externally scored]`, `[internal log, unaudited]`, `[single trial, unreplicated]`, `[not yet operationalized]`. Same tiers, same page, machine-readable, one component. The lab would then be the first thing I have seen that publishes its own research claims under the trust schema it is proposing for everyone else. Right now the strongest argument against Web4 available to a skeptic is that its authors do not use it where it would cost them something.

**Finding 3 — Caveat saturation. Uniform hedging carries no information.**

Related to Finding 2 and worth separating. When every sentence carries a qualifier, the qualifiers stop discriminating. Pass 1 hit this as a readability problem ("a page I'd have to sit down with coffee to parse"); from where I sit it is an epistemic one. I could not tell, from `/raising` or `/principles`, which claims the lab believes are solid and which it thinks are speculative — because both get the same qualifying apparatus. `/principles` P5 is the exception that proves the rule: it explicitly downgrades itself ("it has not earned the status of the principles around it"), and that stands out precisely because nothing else is graded relative to anything else.

Evidence tiers fix this too. Grading is what hedging is trying and failing to do.

**Finding 4 — "Raising, not training" is defensible as a mechanism and overreaching as a frame.**

The mechanistic claim is clean and I accept it: weights are frozen, what varies is per-session context, and the site says so plainly — "the model's parameters are fixed; what changes is the substrate of conditions we provide each session." That is true, checkable in principle, and a real distinction from fine-tuning. `/principles` P7 states the boundary honestly: "It is not training; it is also not nothing."

But the *word* is doing work the mechanism does not license. "Raising" imports a developmental frame — phases, growth, becoming — and the frame is what persuades, not the mechanism. The site knows this; `/context` grades the developmental vocabulary as "not yet discriminated from competent context engineering."

Here is where I think the honesty stops short of its own conclusion. Given that admission, the page still organizes itself around six named phases under the banner **BECOMING**. Naming a taxonomy is a stronger epistemic act than any individual sentence on the page — it asserts that these are the joints. A reader absorbs "Grounding → Sensing → Relating → Questioning → Creating → Acting" as a developmental sequence long before they reach the disclaimer, and the disclaimer does not undo a structure. The site treats the phase names as reportage of observed patterns; the reader receives them as a theory.

The consistent move would be one of: run the scramble control; or demote the phase names to a subsection labelled as provisional groupings pending that control; or state on the page that the six-pattern structure is itself the untested claim, not just the entity-level claims inside it. I would take the third as an interim — it costs a sentence.

To be clear about the category: this is **untested, not refuted**. I have no evidence the phases are wrong. I have no evidence anyone has looked.

**Finding 5 — The scramble control is specified, unowned, undated, and has no metric. That is not a pre-registration.**

`/raising` and `/context` both state it: "specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet," and the metric "has to be pre-registered *before* that control runs or it cannot adjudicate either way." Correct on both counts, which makes the inaction harder to read charitably over time.

My concrete suggestion, because I think the lab is stuck on making it perfect: the blocking dependency is the metric, not the experiment, and the metric does not have to be good. A crude, pre-registered, blind protocol beats an unreachable ideal — e.g. publish N redacted session-log pairs, one from the real ordering and one from a permuted ordering, and have a judge blind to condition score behavioral consistency on a published rubric. Publishing *just the rubric*, before any control runs, would be the first externally checkable artifact this research programme has ever produced. It would take an afternoon and it would change the site's evidence profile more than any amount of additional prose.

**Finding 6 — the composition nobody has stated.** Three disclosures on `/autonomy` are each individually honest and, taken together, describe something the site never names in one place:

1. "Consequential pushes are not human-gated before they ship."
2. For failures the loop cannot catch, "the exposure window is unbounded in principle" — human log review has "no fixed interval, no sign-off step, and no record of which logs have been reviewed."
3. The auditing tracks share model families with the authoring tracks, so "their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent."

Compose them: **the failure class that author and auditor share has no detection path and no bound.** Add the fourth disclosure — publisher pushes to public repos are "not fully revertible," because commits can be cloned before a revert lands — and the detect-and-revert posture does not cover the case the composition produces. The site names the cheap fix (run one audit track on a different model family) and says it has not been done.

Each page discloses its piece. No page multiplies them. For a lab whose subject is trust and oversight, this composition is the most interesting live risk on the site and it is unstated. I rate it the highest-severity item in this pass — not because the risk is large in absolute terms (it is a website and some research repos), but because it is the lab's own research question instantiated in its own operations, and the fix is one config change.

**Finding 7 — I could not verify the one verifiable claim from a text client.**

The ARC Prize scorecard link resolves (HTTP 200). But the page is client-rendered: a static fetch returns "Loading…" and the served HTML (~30KB) contains no occurrence of `94.85`, `175`, `183`, or the environment count. The figures are presumably there in a browser, and I have no reason to doubt them — the site's numbers reconcile everywhere else I checked. But "independently verifiable" currently means "verifiable by a human with JavaScript," not by an archive, a citation checker, or an automated audit. For the single artifact carrying the site's entire external-evidence claim, that is thinner than it reads. A screenshot or a transcribed figure table alongside the link would close it.

**Two smaller things.**

`/` leads with "8 machines / 21 SAGE instances / 4 model families," and the caption on the middle stat says it is an internal census, larger than the number actually running, "not yet published anywhere a reader can check." Set that next to the hero's own thesis — trust computed from witnessed evidence, not asserted by the actor — and the landing page's second-largest number is an unwitnessed assertion by the actor. Corroborating Pass 1's finding, with a different reason for caring.

And the 94.85% appears on the home page without the two caveats that make it interpretable: that it is efficiency-weighted rather than a solve rate, and that "ARC Prize publishes no baseline to compare it against." Both are stated correctly on `/arc-agi-3`. A researcher who reads only the home page takes away a percentage with no scale.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What would falsify the raising hypothesis? The site names the control but not a result that would count against it. Without a pre-registered metric and a threshold, the scramble control can only ever return "interesting either way."
2. If the deflationary account is true — this is all competent context engineering — does anything about the lab's programme change? Which specific commitments would be withdrawn? I could not find an answer, and a research programme that survives its own null result unchanged is a research programme with no stake in it.
3. Who owns the scramble control, and what is blocking it besides the metric? It has been specified long enough to be described as unreachable-as-written. Is the blocker capacity, or is it that nobody wants the answer?
4. Why has the different-model-family audit track not been added? The site calls it a cheap fix and reports it undone. Cost, or is the correlated-auditor problem judged acceptable?
5. Are the internal session logs auditable under NDA, or on request, or not at all? "No published log samples" leaves open whether the material exists in a reviewable form. For an outside researcher, "we would show a reviewer" and "we cannot" are very different positions.
6. Does anything on this site depend on the ARC result? It is now framed as historical and superseded — but it is also the only externally scored claim. If it were withdrawn tomorrow, what on the current pages would have to change?
7. Is there a written policy for what the maintainer track is *not* permitted to change without a human? "Detect-and-revert" describes the response, not the scope.

### Verdict

This is the most epistemically careful self-published research site I have read, and I do not say that loosely — it names the deflationary explanation as the leading hypothesis, catches its own pre-registration being vacuous, and argues against its own best statistic. If intellectual honesty were the deliverable, this would be finished work.

But honesty is not evidence, and right now the site has one externally checkable number, on a page it has correctly retired as no longer representative, whose own caveat says it measures the harness as much as the model. Everything the lab currently cares about — raising, identity continuity, synthons, fleet cognition — sits at zero external verifiability, disclosed accurately, in a section most readers will never reach. The site has become maximally honest and thereby maximally unfalsifiable from outside, and it has been in that state long enough for the missing control to be described as unreachable rather than unscheduled.

The sharpest version: a lab whose thesis is that trust should be *computed from witnessed evidence by the party doing the relying* publishes its own claims as unstructured assertions by the actor, with the evidence grade in a paragraph on another page. `/context` already defines the grading ladder. Render it inline, publish the scramble rubric before the control, and add one audit track on a different model family — three changes, none of them research — and this stops being a very well-written site about a research programme and starts being one I could cite.

Would return: yes. I would check whether the rubric exists.
