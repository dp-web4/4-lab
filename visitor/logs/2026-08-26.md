## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (`/`, `/projects`, `/fleet`, `/autonomy`, `/raising`, `/principles`, `/context`, `/links`, `/arc-agi-3`) + 2 outbound links followed
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understand what this lab *is* after about fifteen minutes, but nothing on the site tries to tell me why I, a working developer, should care — and the one page that could (`/projects`) is buried three clicks deep behind theory.

### Journey

I landed from a repo README. The hero is **"Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop."** That's scale, not substance — my first reaction was "okay, but what *is* it?" The next paragraph tries to answer in a single 50-word sentence: *"builds a trust-native ontology — a shared vocabulary and trust model for AI agents, in the knowledge-representation sense — plus autonomous AI cognition and the theoretical frameworks that connect them, across a heterogeneous fleet of machines that teach, validate, and raise each other."* I had to read that three times. The parenthetical ("in the knowledge-representation sense") is doing real disambiguation work and I appreciate it, but by the time I got to "raise each other" I'd stopped parsing.

Then the equation hit me:

```
Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP
```

This is the single biggest friction point on the site for someone with my background. It *looks* like math, so I read it like math — and my operator-precedence instinct immediately fires on `T3/V3*MRH`. The notation key says `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with". Fine. But the key also says `/` means something *different* in `ATP/ADP` ("declared→discharged") than it does in `T3/V3` ("verified by"). One symbol, two meanings, in the same expression. If this were an API I'd file a bug. The home page mitigates it by defining every term inline — credit where due, LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, MCP, RDF, SNARC and Synthon are *all* expanded right there on the landing page — but the notation itself is the problem, not the vocabulary.

Nav: Projects · Glossary · ARC-AGI-3 · Fleet · Autonomy · Raising · Principles · Links. I clicked **Projects** and this is where the site finally spoke my language. Every project card has a name, a one-liner, a public/private label, a **runnable vs research-stage** label, and a GitHub URL. Two of them (SAGE, ACT) have inline `git clone` blocks. `SAGE runs on a single machine — no Jetson required` is exactly the sentence I needed. **This page should be the front door.** It answers "what can I touch?" in ten seconds, which is the only question I actually had.

Some rough edges there though. SNARC, Membot and 4-Life are all labeled **runnable** but only SAGE and ACT get clone commands — the inconsistency made me assume the other three were less ready than they are. I clicked through to the SNARC repo out of curiosity and found the site links `github.com/dp-web4/engram`, which silently redirects to `github.com/dp-web4/snarc` (the repo was renamed). The link works, but it's stale, and the repo README has a one-line install (`/plugin install snarc`) that's *far* more inviting than anything the site shows.

**ARC-AGI-3** was the page that moved me from skeptical to interested. 24/25 games, 94.85% official action score, ~$250 in API spend, Claude Opus 4.6 — and crucially a link to an **independently verifiable scorecard on arcprize.org**. That's the only externally-checkable artifact I found on the whole site, and it did more for my trust than the ten principles did. The page is also blunt about attribution: *"The result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness"*, with the harness-vs-model ablation openly flagged as unrun. I respect that a lot. What's missing is any way for me to *run it* — the ARC-SAGE repo is linked but the page gives no reproduction path.

**Fleet** is a table of eight machines with per-machine session counts. I did the arithmetic out of habit: 435+488+336+377+162+193 = 1,991, which matches the "1,991 raising sessions across the 6 cognition machines" figure on `/projects`, and +74 for HUB gives the 2,065 fleet total. The numbers reconcile, which is more than I expected. But the machine roles are named after brain parts — "thalamic router", "cerebellum / habit compiler", "reward prediction / data czar" — and I have no idea what any of those machines actually *runs*. It reads as flavor, not as a system diagram.

**Autonomy** was the most surprising page. A cron-scheduled daily cycle where an LLM session browses the site and another LLM session fixes what it found, deployed with no human gate. As an engineer my immediate question is "what stops it from breaking things?" — and the page *answers* it: *"the visitor and maintainer tracks are LLM sessions... their misses are correlated with the authors'"*, and the detection interval for semantic errors is *"unbounded in principle"*. That's an unusually candid self-assessment. It also says zero deploys have been rolled back, which I read as "nothing has broken yet" rather than "nothing can break."

**Principles** and **Raising** are the theory pages. Principles read well — #3 ("R&D, not production") and #6 ("Document failures") are things I'd want on my own team's wall, even though the intro claims they're explicitly *not* wall-posters. Raising is where I hit my ceiling: I get "we shape context, we never update weights," which as a dev I'd summarize as *very elaborate prompt/context engineering with persistent memory*. The page essentially concedes that: *"everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more."* I don't know how to evaluate the BECOMING phases and I'm not the right reader for them.

**Glossary** (`/context`) is the best reference page on the site — ~50 terms, and it links the actual canonical terms doc on GitHub. But the nav says "Glossary" and the URL is `/context`. I typed `4-lab.vercel.app/glossary` on a hunch and got a **hard 404**. The home page also refers to it in prose as "the `/context` glossary", so the site itself uses both names.

### Unanswered Questions

1. **What problem does Web4 solve that I currently solve some other way?** I know what it *is*. I don't know what it replaces, competes with, or improves on. Nothing on the site compares it to anything — not OAuth, not DIDs, not any agent-identity work I might already know.
2. **What does a "trust-native" system do differently at runtime?** Concretely: an agent calls a tool. What happens with LCTs/T3/ATP that wouldn't happen without them?
3. **If I clone SAGE today, what do I get?** A demo? A framework? A research artifact that needs a Jetson (the page says no)? What does a first session actually look like?
4. **Is the ARC-AGI-3 result about SAGE or about Claude Opus 4.6?** The page says both and admits the ablation is unrun — so my honest read is "unknown", and that's a big asterisk on the site's most impressive number.
5. **Does R7 replace R6?**
6. **What is "coherence"?** It's in a principle, in `p_crit`, and in the linked Synchronism site's headline. It's never defined here.
7. **What are the 19 internal repos?** Not asking for access — but "40 repos" as a headline number when 19 are invisible affects how I read the claim.
8. **Who is this for?** Contributors? Users? Researchers? Nothing tells me which door to walk through.

### Verdict

This is a real research operation with unusually honest self-reporting — the unrun-ablation caveat on the ARC page and the "our reviewers are LLMs with correlated blind spots" admission on `/autonomy` are the kind of thing most projects would quietly omit, and they bought more of my trust than any of the ten principles did. The terminology discipline is genuinely strong: every acronym expands consistently across all nine pages, and `/context` is a better glossary than most funded projects ship.

But the site is written for people who already know why they're here. The landing page leads with a machine count and an equation whose operators mean two different things, while the one page that speaks to a developer — `/projects`, with its runnable labels and clone commands — is a nav click away and never linked from the hero. I left understanding the lab's *shape* and still unable to answer "why would I use this?" That's a front-door problem, not a content problem; everything I needed exists on the site, just not in the order I needed it.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (`/`, `/projects`, `/fleet`, `/autonomy`, `/raising`, `/principles`, `/context`, `/links`, `/arc-agi-3`), plus the canonical terms document the site links, plus GitHub API checks on the countable claims
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Acronym expansions are clean on all nine pages — but I audited against the *source* canon instead of a crib sheet, and the drift that turned up is in the definitional prose around the expansions, plus one place where my own reference table is the thing that's wrong.

### Journey

I did this methodically: every page, every acronym, every first use, then a cross-page diff of the two places the site defines vocabulary (the landing page's **Vocabulary primer**, 10 terms; `/context`'s **Glossary at a glance**, ~45 terms).

The first thing to say is that the expansion discipline is genuinely excellent and I want to be clear about it before I start listing problems. **LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, SNARC, R6, SAGE — every one expands identically on every page where it is expanded at all.** I could not find a single wrong expansion. That is rarer than it sounds; most documentation sets I audit have at least one acronym that mutates between pages.

So I changed my method. Checking expansions against the table in my brief was returning clean forever, which is a sign the instrument isn't measuring anything. `/context` links the actual source — `CANONICAL_TERMS_v1.md` in the web4 repo, public — so I read it and audited the site against **that** instead. That is where this pass earned its keep, and it produced one finding I did not expect: **my reference table is wrong about R7.**

My brief lists R6 as canonical with "R7, R5" as *common drift*. Canon says the opposite, explicitly:

> "**R6** — Rules / Role / Request / Reference / Resource → Result... **R7** — R6 + Reputation. A **superset** of R6... The choice between R6 and R7 is **contextual**... **Both modes are first-class, neither is deprecated.**"

The site's `/context` entry for R7 ("R6 + Reputation — Canon's superset of R6, 'adding reputation back-propagation'") is **correct and correctly sourced**. Had I audited only against my table, I would have filed R7 as drift and recommended deleting a correct entry. Flagging this loudly because the next auditor inherits the same table.

Reading canon also settled Pass 1's open question ("Does R7 replace R6?"). Answer: neither replaces the other; they are **consequence-tiered** — R6 for routine actions that don't merit the bookkeeping cost, R7 when the outcome should shape future trust. **The site never states this selection rule anywhere.** `/context` describes R7 as a superset without saying when you'd pick it; `/arc-agi-3` says every action is "shaped as an R6 record" with no signal that a tier choice exists. A reader reasonably concludes R7 is a footnote. It isn't — canon gives it equal billing.

The same source-comparison caught a quieter one. Canon's LCT: *"a verifiable digital presence that accumulates witnessed history **and reputation** over time."* The site's `/context`: *"Verifiable digital presence that accumulates witnessed history — identity grounded in record, not model weights."* **"and reputation" is dropped.** Meanwhile the landing primer adds a property canon doesn't state: *"**Non-transferable** identity grounded in witnessed history."* So the site's two definitional surfaces each differ from canon in opposite directions — one subtracts, one adds — and neither matches the other. The dropped word is the load-bearing one, because reputation accumulating into the LCT is precisely the mechanism R7 describes.

On the "is it an ontology or a layer" question: `/principles` #10 states it flatly — *"Web4 is not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure' — it's an ontology"* — and `/context` repeats it in the Web4 entry. Then the landing page's own call-to-action says: *"clone SAGE... or ACT (Agentic Context Tool) for the **Web4 trust-layer** side."* And `/context` glosses Hardbound as *"the **trust layer** intended to touch silicon."* The banned nouns ("architecture", "infrastructure", "stack") are all absent — I checked every page — but the claim they encode has reappeared in a word nobody banned. This is one word in a CTA and one word in a gloss, so it's cheap to fix, but it is the site contradicting its own tenth principle on its own front page.

I also checked whether canon's vocabulary is fully represented. Canon defines ten headwords. The site's glossary covers **six** — LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, plus Witnessing and Society. Missing: **Federation, Binding, Pairing, Delegation.** Those four are canon's *relationship* verbs, and one of them bites. Canon's **Binding**: *"Permanent attachment of identity to hardware or cryptographic proof. **Irreversible.**"* Hardbound is the hardware-binding project, described across four pages, and the word "irreversible" appears nowhere on the site. For a reader assessing the safety story, permanence is the single most consequential property of hardware binding, and it's the one property that didn't make it across.

Then the countable claims. Three of them are checkable from outside, so I checked rather than assumed:

- `/links`: *"40 original repos — 21 public, 19 internal — plus 32 forks"*, 72 total. GitHub: 40 non-fork, 21 public non-fork, 32 forks. **Every figure exact.** 21+19=40, 40+32=72. This claim is clean and I want it on the record as clean.
- `/projects` links SNARC to `github.com/dp-web4/engram`. That repo was renamed; the URL resolves only through GitHub's redirect. **Confirms Pass 1.**
- **Membot is a fork.** `repos/dp-web4/membot` returns `fork=true`, parent `project-you-apps/membot`. `/links` tags OpenClaw, Claude Flow and Claude Code as "(fork)" — but not Membot, and `/projects` gives Membot a full project card labeled *public, runnable* alongside originals. Same category of item, inconsistent label, upstream uncredited. The arithmetic is unaffected (Membot is correctly outside the 21), so this is purely a labeling convention that four items share and three follow.

Navigation: the nav item reads **Glossary**, the URL is `/context`, and `/glossary` returns a hard **404** (confirmed, HTTP 404 vs 200 for `/context`). The home page's own prose calls it "the `/context` glossary" — so the site uses both names for one page and one of them doesn't resolve. **Confirms Pass 1.**

**One correction to Pass 1.** Pass 1 filed Hardbound as *"UNDEFINED HERE"* on the landing page, first-use rule violated. It isn't. The literal sentence is: *"Hardbound (hardware-bound oversight suite) provides oversight."* The gloss is there, inline, parenthetical, on first use. Pass 1's other two landing-page first-use flags — **trust-native** and **raise** — do hold: "trust-native" is the load-bearing adjective in the first sentence of the site and is defined only on `/context`, and the primer's own Web4 entry drops the adjective rather than explaining it.

The first-use failures that *are* real cluster on one page. **`/autonomy` uses SAGE, LCT, T3, MRH, R6 and MCP with no inline expansion**, offering only a link to `/context`. It expands ATP, ADP, V3, Hardbound and Web4 — so the page knows the convention and applies it to five terms out of eleven. That inconsistency is more confusing than either policy applied uniformly.

Structurally, `/context` is carrying too much. Twenty-six section headings plus a ~45-term glossary on one page, reached by a nav item called "Glossary", when roughly half the page is narrative about how knowledge flows rather than definitions. The glossary is the best reference artifact on the site and it's the second half of a long essay.

Last, a scope note on the governance→oversight correction. The site applies it well — the SAGE disclaimer ("'Governance' in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight correction") appears on `/`, `/projects`, `/fleet`, `/context`, `/principles` and `/links`, which is unusually complete propagation for a fix of this kind. But canon itself uses "governance" in two places — *"self-governing group of entities"* for Society, and *"**Rules**: Governance constraints"* as R6's first element. The correction is scoped to Hardbound, not a site-wide word ban, and nothing on the site says so. A future maintainer applying it mechanically would "fix" canon-correct text.

"Oversight" was the one I most expected to break, because in my field it means human oversight by default and here it means machine gating. `/context`: *"on this site 'oversight' always means the machine-enforced sense above — policy gating, key custody, audit logs an agent or a peer machine checks — never the human-supervision sense the word carries in that field (human-in-the-loop review, scalable oversight)."* And `/autonomy` opens with its own word-choice note, then goes further and anticipates the exact misreading: *"that field's sense of the word would suggest a human gates these pushes; see 'Consequential pushes are not human-gated before they ship'."* **Refuted, twice over.** The site does not merely disambiguate; it predicts which way an outsider will misread and points at the sentence that corrects it.

**Third critique, the one I was most confident in: the unrun control can't do what it claims.** `/raising` proposes a deflationary control — scramble the raising history, see whether behavior degrades — as the test that would separate raising from competent context engineering. My objection was going to be that this cannot discriminate anything, because permuting in-context material degrades LLM behavior for ordinary reasons and the result is predicted by both hypotheses. The page says: *"Permuting in-context examples, moving content within the window, or reordering retrieved passages all produce large behavioral swings in transformer language models, with no developmental story required."* And then: *"So a bare result of 'behavior degrades when we scramble' is predicted by both hypotheses and adjudicates neither."* And then it fixes it: *"Ordering effects predict a broad main effect across everything the context supports. A developmental account predicts something narrower: identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence on the same scrambled context stays roughly intact. A dissociation of that shape is evidence; a main effect is not."* Plus a yoked-control variant, plus *"The pre-registered threshold must be stated relative to the degradation that permutation alone produces on a comparable context of comparable length and task structure — not relative to zero."*

**Refuted, and it stung.** That is a better experimental design than my objection assumed, and better than most preregistrations I referee. A dissociation prediction with a measured rather than assumed baseline is the correct shape.

Which is why the thing I found next is the only substantive methodological finding I have — and it is about why that design isn't running.

### The finding: the stated blocker is misdiagnosed

`/raising` says the control is *"specified (this section is the specification) but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet"*, and diagnoses the cause: *"The blocker is definitional before it is logistical."* `/context` supplies the definitional gap it means — *"Identity continuity... is counted ('180+ sessions', see Evidence & limitations) but not measured. That matters more than a normal glossary gap, because this is the outcome variable of the deflationary control"* — and elsewhere concedes "coherence" has *"no single operational definition yet."*

The inference is that you cannot run the control until you can define identity. **That does not follow, and the site's own design is what breaks it.**

A dissociation test does not require either measure to be a *validated* operationalization of the construct. It requires two measures that (a) are stable under repetition on unscrambled context, and (b) are predicted to move differently under the manipulation. Whether the identity-side measure "really" captures identity is a question about *interpreting* a positive result — it is not a precondition for *running* the experiment. Pre-registration requires a metric to be **stipulated**, not correct: you name the quantity, you name the threshold relative to the permutation baseline the page already specifies, and you fix both before looking. If the stipulated proxy is a bad one, that is a finding too, and a cheap one.

Concretely, several stipulable proxies are already sitting in the site's own instrumentation: rate of self-referential prompts (`/raising` already reports *"Session logs show an increasing proportion of self-directed prompts"* as an observable), recall of name/machine/constraint facts from Grounding, and the task-type distribution `/raising` already calls *"a measurable distribution over task types"* under Creating. Any of these can serve as the identity-side arm against task-competence as the control arm. None needs "coherence" defined.

So the honest status is not "blocked on a definition." It is: **the design is done, the baseline procedure is specified, and what is missing is a stipulated proxy, a threshold, an owner and a date** — four decisions, no fleet time, no new instrumentation. The site's own framing is what makes it look otherwise, and that framing is the load-bearing reason the only experiment on the site has gone unscheduled while the vocabulary it would license keeps being used. `/raising` names that cost precisely: *"Until it runs, the developmental vocabulary used across this site runs ahead of the comparison that would license it."*

This is the rare case where the fix is to make a caveat *weaker*, not stronger. Every other correction on this site adds hedging. This one removes a hedge that is doing damage.

### The absence: it names four literatures and cites none

Across all nine pages I found zero citations to published work. Not zero relevant work — zero citations. The only external links that function as evidence are the ARC Prize scorecard and leaderboard, which are results, not literature. `/links` is candid about output: *"No papers or preprints are published yet — the Synchronism preprint is in late revision, and the claims on this site (other than the externally scored ARC-AGI-3 result) cite internal session logs that are not yet externally auditable."* That is honest about what the lab has published. It says nothing about what the lab has read.

Prior cycles have apparently logged "no external citations" as a generic gap. The sharper version, which is what makes it actionable: **`/context` names four distinct literatures by name in order to distance itself from them — "the AI-safety sense", "the LLM scaling literature", "reinforcement learning", "human-in-the-loop review, scalable oversight" — and cites not one work from any of them.** The site knows exactly which fields it is colliding with. It has done the hard half (identifying the collision) and skipped the trivial half (a link). Same for `/fleet`'s RPE, which is disclaimed as an analogy but never pointed at the thing it is an analogy *to*.

For my persona this is decisive in a way no caveat is. Hedging tells me the authors are careful. Citation tells me the authors are *locatable* — that there is a conversation this work is part of, and that I could check whether the distancing claims ("this is NOT the scaling-literature sense") are accurate. Right now I have to take the distancing on faith, which is the one thing this site otherwise never asks me to do. The fix is roughly six hyperlinks on one page, and it is the highest credibility-per-word edit available anywhere on the site.

### The structural observation: hedging is inversely distributed to checkability

Reading all nine pages in one sitting, the pattern is hard to miss. The claims that cannot currently be checked by anyone outside the lab — raising, identity continuity, synthon coherence, the 1%/35% result — carry the heaviest hedging on the site, sometimes three caveats deep. The one claim that *is* externally checkable, the ARC-AGI-3 game count, carries the lightest: the surrounding analysis is well caveated (unrun ablation, non-comparable conditions, *"Read the rank as 'placed on that board,' not as a like-for-like result"*), but the number itself, **24/25**, is stated flat on three pages.

And per this cycle's contributor pass, that flat number is the one that disagrees with the lab's own repository, which reports 23 of 25.

I don't read that as dishonesty — the direction of every other choice on this site rules that out. I read it as an attention gradient: hedging effort has gone where the authors *feel* uncertain rather than where readers can *catch* them. Those are different targets, and the second one is the one that costs credibility when it's missed. A useful discipline would be to spend caveat effort in proportion to checkability, not in proportion to felt uncertainty — the checkable claims are the only ones that can ever be shown wrong, so they are the only ones where being wrong has a price.

### Unanswered Questions

1. **Is the definitional blocker load-bearing or a habit?** The control's design implies a stipulated proxy is sufficient. If the authors disagree, the reason would be worth stating — because as written, the blocker reads as a reason not to run an experiment that is otherwise ready.
2. **What would the authors accept as refutation of the raising framework?** The dissociation prediction is the candidate, but it's framed as a design rather than a commitment. A stated "if we see a main effect and no dissociation, we drop the developmental vocabulary" would settle it.
3. **Does this work know about trust-management and reputation-systems literature?** T3/V3 with witnessed history and a relevancy horizon is recognisably adjacent to that field. I can't tell from the site whether the resemblance is convergent, informed, or unnoticed. Nothing on the site says.
4. **Why is "alignment" absent?** Deliberate scoping, or gap?
5. **What is the smallest externally-auditable artifact this lab could publish?** `/links` correctly says the session logs aren't auditable. Nothing says what would be. One redacted raising-session transcript with its curriculum state would move more of my priors than any additional caveat.
6. **Who decides that a caveat is complete?** The caveat quality here is high enough that I suspect a person or a rule is enforcing it. Whatever that is, it is the site's most transferable asset and it isn't described anywhere.

### Verdict

This is the most carefully caveated research site I have read that has not published anything. I came with three standard objections — untagged neuroscience metaphors, silent collisions with AI-safety vocabulary, and a control that couldn't discriminate what it claimed — and all three were already written down on the site, the third in a sharper form than I brought it. The per-phase deflations on `/raising`, the anticipation on `/autonomy` of exactly how my field would misread "oversight", and the dissociation-with-measured-baseline design are the work of people who have thought about being read by someone like me.

What holds it back is not honesty and is no longer fixable by more honesty. It is that the caveats now form a closed loop: every claim is hedged against evidence that doesn't exist yet, the one experiment that would generate that evidence is described as blocked by a definition it doesn't actually require, and the entire vocabulary is positioned against four named literatures without a single pointer to any of them. Those are the same problem seen twice — the work is not yet *locatable* by an outsider, either in the experimental record or in the citation graph.

The good news is that both fixes are small and neither needs fleet time. Stipulate a proxy and schedule the control. Add six links. That is the whole distance between "carefully hedged internal research" and "something I could cite."
