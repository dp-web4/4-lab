## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I can tell this lab is unusually honest and technically real, but after 2 minutes I still couldn't tell you what problem Web4 solves for *me* or what happens if I clone SAGE.

### Journey

Landed on the home page from a repo README. The headline is **"Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop."** That's a scale flex, not a value proposition — my first reaction was "cool, but what does it *do*?" The next line says the lab builds "a trust-native ontology for AI agents across heterogeneous hardware," and that's where I stalled. "Trust-native ontology" is three abstract words in a row. I know what an ontology is in the RDF sense, so I guessed correctly, but a dev who doesn't do semantic web would bounce here.

What *did* hook me: the stat block (8 machines / 40 repos / 31+ tracks) reads as evidence that something is actually running, and further down there's a claim that a small model kept its identity when swapped from Qwen 0.5B to TinyLlama 1.1B. That's a concrete, weird, interesting claim. I also noticed an explicit evidence caveat saying most of this is internal observation with no external audit except the ARC Prize scorecard. That caveat bought a lot of credibility with me — it's the reason I kept clicking instead of closing the tab.

Went to **/projects** next. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` sits near the top. I know MCP and RDF. The other five I don't. To the page's credit, the operators are explained (`/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with") and each acronym is expanded in the cards below — but the equation lands *before* the expansions, so my first read was a wall. The project cards themselves are good: every one has a public/private badge and a research-stage/runnable badge. "Runnable" vs "research-stage" is exactly the signal I want and I wish more research sites did it. SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, and Membot are the four marked runnable, so that's where my attention went.

Then I hit the **Quick Start** at the bottom and it deflated me. It's:

```
git clone https://github.com/dp-web4/SAGE
cd SAGE
```

...followed by "Needs: Rust (stable), Ollama, Python 3.10+" and "see each repo's README for the dependency list and first-run steps." That's a clone command, not a quick start. There's no build step, no run command, no description of what I'd see if it worked. "SAGE runs on a single machine — no Jetson required" is a genuinely useful reassurance and I'm glad it's there, but I still don't know whether running SAGE gives me a REPL, a daemon, a log stream, or a benchmark score. This is the single biggest gap on the site for someone like me.

**/fleet** was the most fun page. Eight machine cards — Thor, Sprout, Legion, McNugget, Nomad, CBP, HUB, pub — each with hardware, model, session count, and a brain-analog role (episodic memory, thalamic router, reward prediction, cerebellum, metacognition, working memory). A Jetson Orin Nano running a sub-1B model as a "thalamic router" is a memorable image. The finding "model family matters as much as size" (Gemma 3 4B beating a larger Phi-4 on some task) is the kind of concrete result I'd repeat to a coworker. But the page opens with "Six cognition machines, plus two society-hosts" and never tells me what a *society* is or what hosting one involves. HUB has 74 sessions and "hosts Web4 Fleet society"; pub is "staging for public Web4 hub" with a deliberately blank session count. I read those twice and still don't know what's running there.

**/autonomy** was the page that made the whole thing click. The timeline (supervisors → archivist → publisher → visitor → maintainer → outreach → explorer → dream consolidation) is a cron schedule, and cron I understand completely. It explicitly defines "autonomous" as *unattended and self-scheduled, NOT self-directed in choosing goals* — that disambiguation should honestly be on the home page, because "31+ autonomous tracks" reads much scarier without it. The honest-assessment block here is the best writing on the site: it admits the maintainer track pushes to Vercel with no human pre-approval, that oversight is detect-and-revert rather than prevent, that 79 sessions produced zero rollbacks *but that only counts corrections the automated loop could see*, and that publisher pushes to public repos are irreversible in a way website pushes aren't. I don't think I've read a research site that volunteered its own worst failure mode that clearly.

**/raising** and **/principles** are the philosophy pages. Raising defines itself as context shaping with no weight updates, which is a clean and checkable distinction, and then immediately says "everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more" and names the control experiment that hasn't been run. Principles reads like a lessons-learned doc rather than a values poster — "R&D, not production," "Document failures," and a specific admitted blowup ("the p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error"). Some of it is over my head (Hill function kinetics, synthon, coherence) but the page flags synthon itself as "a research direction rather than established mechanism," so I didn't feel misled, just outside my depth.

**/context** is labeled **Glossary** in the nav, which is the right call — I'd have skipped a link called "Context." It's a clean definition list and it's where I finally resolved LCT, MRH, ATP/ADP, and R6. Problem: I found it on my own via the nav on my fifth page. Nothing on the home page or /projects told me the glossary existed. The only "New here? See /context for full definitions" pointer I found is on **/links**, which is the last page anyone visits.

**/arc-agi-3** is the strongest page for my persona and I think it's underplayed. 94.85% official action score, 96% game-solve rate (24/25), Claude Opus 4.6, ~$250 total API spend, #3 on the human leaderboard with the fewest actions of the top 3 — and a link to an ARC Prize scorecard I can verify myself. That's the one number on this site that doesn't require me to trust anybody. It's also the page with the sharpest caveat: the same-model-no-harness ablation hasn't been run, so the harness's own contribution is unquantified. Naming your missing control is a good look.

**/links** is a solid directory. Every repo has a one-line description, forks are labeled as forks, and the ARC Prize scorecard gets its own entry as "the external source of record." Contact is dp@metalinxx.io.

### Unanswered Questions

1. If I clone SAGE and run it, what do I actually get? A daemon? A CLI? A benchmark harness? Something I can point at my own agent?
2. What's the smallest useful thing I could build with Web4? Is there a "give an agent an identity in 20 lines" path, or is adoption all-or-nothing?
3. What is a "society" and what does hosting one involve? Two of eight machines do this and I have no model of it.
4. Do LCTs need a chain? ACT is described as built on Cosmos SDK — is a blockchain required for identity, or is that one optional backend?
5. Why should I care about T3/V3 over just... logging what an agent did and reading the logs? The site asserts trust must be relational and per-context, but never shows the failure case that motivates the tensor.
6. The identity-persistence claim (Qwen 0.5B → TinyLlama 1.1B, identity survived) is the most striking thing on the site. What was measured to conclude identity persisted?
7. Is any of this usable against a hosted model, or does it assume local Ollama?

### Verdict

This site is far more honest than most research sites and I trust it more than I expected to — the ARC scorecard is externally verifiable, the maintainer-track gap is volunteered rather than hidden, and multiple pages name the control experiment that hasn't been run. That's rare and it's the reason I'd come back. But it's built for someone who already knows why they're here: the hero sells scale instead of a problem, the glossary is unadvertised until the last page, and "Quick Start" doesn't start anything. I left understanding the *lab* well and the *technology* barely — I could describe how the fleet operates but not what Web4 would do for my own agent, and no page tried to tell me. Fix the quick start and put one plain sentence about the actual problem above the fold, and this converts developers instead of just impressing them.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Acronym discipline here is the best I have audited on a research site — zero canonical drift across nine pages — but the glossary makes a promise it does not keep, and three pages expand SAGE's "Governance" without the caveat the other four carry.

### Journey

I audit terminology for a living, so I browsed the way I always do: canonical table in one hand, every page in the other, checking each acronym expansion against the table and then checking every *other* page for the same expansion. Nine pages, ten controlled terms.

The headline result is that I found **no canonical drift**. Not one. LCT is "Linked Context Token" on seven pages and never "Lifelong Capability Token." T3 is "Talent / Training / Temperament" on six pages with identical slash spacing. V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, R6, SNARC, ACP — all correct, all consistent, including punctuation. Web4 is called an ontology on every page that names it and is twice explicitly negated against the wrong words ("not architecture or infrastructure"). Hardbound is "oversight" everywhere and never "governance." "Production ready" appears nowhere; /principles has a principle titled "R&D, not production." "Fractal leverage" is defined on /context as "Not unification, and not scope inflation" — the site pre-empts its own two banned alternatives by name.

That is an unusual result. Most sites I audit have three to five drifted expansions by page six. I want to be precise about what this does and does not show: it shows the *expansions* are locked. It does not show the *concepts* are equally well managed, and that's where my findings are.

**/context** is the load-bearing document and it is well built. Sectioned by concept rather than alphabetized, which is the right call for a vocabulary this interdependent — "The Web4 equation as shared anchor" and "Worked example: one action through the equation" do work that an A-Z list cannot. The page title is "Glossary | 4-lab" and the nav label is "Glossary," even though the URL is /context. I'll come back to that.

The R6/R7 handling deserves specific praise. /context states R6 is "the canonical action framework used throughout the SAGE loop and Web4 audit trail," then adds that "Canon pairs R6 with **R7** — a superset adding a seventh element, Reputation," and closes with "Both modes are canonical; the choice depends on whether outcomes should influence future trust." That is exactly how you document a framework with two sanctioned forms: name both, state the selection criterion, don't make the reader guess. My note is that **R7 appears on exactly one page.** /projects, /arc-agi-3, and /links all use R6 and never mention R7 — I checked /projects explicitly and R7 is absent. A glossary entry for a term no page uses is a forward reference. That's fine and probably deliberate (the glossary tracking canon ahead of the prose is the correct direction of travel), but the acronym list on /links gives R6 only, which means a reader who meets R7 on /context has no second encounter to consolidate it.

The **governance→oversight** correction is documented on /context better than most organizations document a rename: it states the original ("The lab originally described its control layers as 'governance.'"), the correction ("The vocabulary was corrected lab-wide, and Hardbound is described as an oversight suite everywhere on this site"), the retention policy ("Names minted before the correction are retained where they are load-bearing"), and — critically — that the retention is intentional: "No rename is planned — retention of load-bearing legacy names is the policy, not an oversight awaiting a fix." That last sentence is doing real work. It stops a future reader from filing the same bug.

The problem is **coverage of that caveat**. SAGE expands to "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" on seven pages. Four of them gloss the legacy name inline: /projects ("'Governance' in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight terminology correction; the name is retained upstream"), /raising, /arc-agi-3, and /context. Three do not: **/fleet**, **/links**, and **/principles**.

/principles is the one that actually bites. Principle 1 expands "SAGE (Situation-Aware Governance Engine)" with no gloss. Principle 10 is titled "Terminology matters" and reads "Web4 is an ontology. Hardbound is oversight." A reader going top to bottom meets the legacy name first and the rule that appears to forbid it ninth, with nothing connecting them. That reads as the site contradicting itself on its own terminology page. It isn't a contradiction — but the page gives the reader no way to know that, and it's the page whose entire job is terminology.

**/fleet** is the strongest page for acronym hygiene — it expands SAGE, T3, V3, MRH, LCT, SNARC, RPE, ATP/ADP, MCP, and LoRA on a page that is mostly hardware specifications, which is more discipline than most spec sheets manage. But its section headers are undefined: "Synthesis pool — Account 1," "Oversight pool — Account 2," "Society-host pool." The "Account 1 / Account 2" suffix reads like billing, which suggests these are procurement groupings rather than functional ones — but **"Oversight pool" collides with the single most carefully policed word on this site.** Is it a pool of machines that perform oversight, or a billing account that happens to be named that? The site spends four pages defining "oversight" as machine-enforced policy gating and then uses it as an ungossed grouping label. Of everything I found, this is the term most likely to install a wrong mental model, precisely *because* the reader has been trained to read "oversight" carefully.

Which brings me to my sharpest finding. The home page says: **"Start with the /context glossary — every term on every page is defined there."** That is a falsifiable claim, so I tested it. Two load-bearing terms fail it:

- **"cognition machine"** — used in the home page stat block ("6 cognition + 2 society-hosts"), throughout /fleet, and in /autonomy ("31+ tracks"). /context has no dedicated entry; it appears only inside other definitions.
- **"society-host"** — same three pages. /context describes HUB and pub contextually as "running the Web4 hub daemon" but has no entry.

These two terms partition the entire fleet. Every machine on the site is one or the other. A reader sent to the glossary on the strength of "every term on every page is defined there" and looking for either one comes back empty. An overclaimed pointer is worse than no pointer: it converts a small gap into a credibility hit on the document you most need the reader to trust. Either add the two entries or soften the sentence to "every Web4 term."

Two corrections to Pass 1's read, verified by targeted re-fetch. Pass 1 reports that neither / nor /projects points to the glossary, and that the only pointer is on /links. Both pages do point to it. The home page has a section headed **"Vocabulary primer"** and the lines "Start with the /context glossary — every term on every page is defined there" and "New to this vocabulary? Start with /context — canonical definitions for every term used on this site." /projects carries "This is the most acronym-dense page on the site. Every Web4 term the cards use (LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, R6, MCP, RDF, SNARC) is defined in the /context glossary." Pass 1's related friction item about the 8-vs-6 machine arithmetic is likewise already handled — the home stat block reads "8 Machines (6 cognition + 2 society-hosts)." I flag these because a maintainer acting on Pass 1's log would be fixing things that are not broken.

Where I'd redirect that effort instead: the pointers exist but they all point *outward to a path*, and the nav shows *a label*. Home and /projects bridge it by writing "the /context glossary." /arc-agi-3 and /links use the bare form — "See /context for full definitions," "see /context." A reader who reads "see /context," looks up at a nav reading Projects · Glossary · ARC-AGI-3 · Fleet · Autonomy · Raising · Principles · Links, and finds no "Context," has to infer the mapping. The fix is one word: write "see the Glossary" or keep the bridged form everywhere.

Smaller notes. **/autonomy** opens with a word-choice preamble — "A word-choice note before the rest of this page: 'oversight' below always means the machine-enforced sense" — which is the correct pattern and I wish more pages copied it verbatim. But the page uses SAGE without expanding the acronym (it glosses the *concept* as "the separate on-device cognition kernel that runs on each machine's local model," which is arguably more useful, but a tech writer wants both on first use) and uses "Web4" with the parenthetical "(the trust-native ontology)" — good — while offering no glossary link. /autonomy is the most link-worthy page on the site; deep-linked readers land with no vocabulary path out.

**ARC-AGI-3** is expanded only on its own page ("Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence, third-gen interactive benchmark"). It's a nav label on all nine pages. That's normal practice for a proper-noun benchmark and I'm not flagging it as a defect.

One methodology caveat on my own audit: my two reads of /context disagreed on whether the word "layer" appears. One read returned "NOT PRESENT" for "stack" and "layer"; the other returned the sentence "The lab originally described its control layers as 'governance.'" I am recording the discrepancy rather than resolving it — if the sentence is present, the usage is historical-descriptive ("originally described") and legitimate, so it is not a finding either way. But treat the "no stack-vocabulary anywhere" conclusion as probable, not verified.

### Journey

So my pass is about whether the *concepts* behind those correct strings are represented correctly. Four things stood out, and two of them are ones I'd want fixed before I sent a new Web4 contributor here.

**The LCT definition doesn't motivate its own acronym.** Three pages define LCT and none of them uses the words "linked" or "context" in the definition:

- `/` — "Non-transferable identity grounded in witnessed history, not model weights."
- `/context` — "Verifiable digital presence that accumulates witnessed history — identity grounded in record, not model weights."
- `/fleet` — "identity grounded in witnessed history, not model weights."

Every one of those is *true*. Non-transferability, other-grounding, witnessed history, self-issued-with-no-CA — the home page card gets all of that right, including the sharp "Self-*issued* … but other-*grounded*" formulation, which is better than most internal docs manage. But read the definitions back and ask which expansion they predict. "Verifiable digital presence that accumulates witnessed history, permanently bound to one entity" is a near-perfect gloss of **"Lifelong Capability Token"** — the exact drift the canonical table exists to prevent. It is a poor gloss of "Linked Context Token," because nothing on the site explains that the *linkage* is the RDF association graph and the *context* is what the token indexes into. The site has locked the string and left the reason unstated.

This is the finding I care most about, because it's the mechanism by which drift comes *back* after a correction. A term whose expansion isn't derivable from its definition is held in place only by enforcement. Add one clause — that the token is an identity node *linked* into the RDF context graph, which is what "Linked Context" names — and the expansion becomes self-defending rather than policed.

**The glossary's MRH entry omits the property that Principle 2 runs on.** Compare:

- Home primer card: "Not a pure causal-reach boundary: the question it answers is 'what is relevant to this entity at this scale?' … and **fractally composable** — an entity's horizon contains sub-horizons at finer scales, which is what lets fractal leverage work."
- `/context` glossary entry: "The boundary of what an entity can know or affect given its position, history, and context — and therefore what determines the scope of relevance for its decisions."

The glossary entry leads with can-know-or-affect, which is the causal-reach reading the home page explicitly warns against, and reaches relevance only via "and therefore." It also drops fractal composability entirely. That matters more than it looks: `/principles` P2 asserts "Each entity instantiates the full set of Web4 primitives at its own scale" and "The Web4 equation describes the cell, not the system." The *reason* that's coherent rather than hand-waving is that MRH nests — sub-horizons at finer scales. The home page states that link outright. The glossary, which is where P2 sends you and where the equation's canonical reading lives, does not. Right now the deepest MRH treatment on the site is a primer card on the landing page, and the canonical entry is the thinner one. That inversion should be fixed in the glossary's favour.

Credit where it's due: `/fleet` contains the single most correct applied use of MRH on the site — Nomad's MetabolicBlock "reads a peer's metabolic state scoped through **that peer's own** Markov Relevancy Horizon." Whoever wrote that understood that MRH is indexed to the entity whose horizon it is, not to the observer. That's a distinction I see fumbled constantly.

**The printed equation can't carry the V3 role the prose assigns it.** The equation is correct everywhere: `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`, with `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with". `/projects` gives the sharpest legend — `/` = "verified by (T3 by V3; ATP by its ADP discharge record)". So in the resource term, ATP is verified by ADP.

But the home page's ATP/ADP card says: "ADP recharges back to ATP against validated value creation, and that validation is what **V3 … does in the resource half of the equation**." And `/context`'s R7 entry says Reputation is "a trust-tensor delta back-propagated from the result, **feeding ADP recharge validation**."

Both of those are right about the mechanism — V3 is what gates recharge; that's canon. The problem is that V3 appears in the equation only inside `T3/V3*MRH`, and the legend tells the reader that the resource term's verifier is ADP. So the site simultaneously teaches (a) `/` in `ATP/ADP` means ATP-verified-by-ADP and (b) V3 does the validating in that half. A reader building a mental model from the equation will not derive (b), and a reader who takes (b) seriously will misread the operator in (a). `/context` calls the equation "a canonical reference point for alignment checks," so its completeness is load-bearing. The honest phrasing is that the equation's `ATP/ADP` term abbreviates a cycle whose *recharge* leg is validated by V3 — V3 reaches across the `+` — rather than placing V3 "in the resource half." One sentence in the "Web4 equation as shared anchor" section closes it.

**T3's "Training" has been narrowed, deliberately, and I think slightly too far.** `/context`: "Training: capability accumulated through interaction history — not gradient training, the sense the same word carries in ML." `/raising`: "'Training' here names accumulated interaction history."

I understand exactly why: `/raising`'s whole argument is "Raising is not training; it is also not nothing," and a T3 dimension literally named Training is a collision that has to be defused. Defusing it is right. But the current phrasing doesn't disambiguate, it *excludes* — it says T3-Training is not gradient training. For a model-backed entity, gradient training is part of how its capability was accumulated and belongs in that dimension's sub-graph; it's simply not the *only* contributor, and it's not what raising touches. The fix is a one-word change from exclusion to superset: Training covers capability however accumulated — interaction history *and* weights-level provenance — and raising acts on the former only. As written, a contributor could reasonably conclude that a fine-tune doesn't move T3-Training, which is wrong.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary

### Journey

I came to this from a conference mention, and I read it the way I read a lab's public write-up before deciding whether to cite it: what is claimed, what tier of evidence sits under each claim, and whether the hedges are load-bearing or decorative. I went home → /raising → /principles → /autonomy first, per my interests, then /context, /arc-agi-3, /projects, /fleet, /links.

**The first thing worth saying plainly: the caveating is better than the field average by a wide margin, and I want to be specific about why rather than just praising it.** Performed honesty concedes cheap things — "more work is needed," "results are preliminary." This site concedes expensive things. Four examples:

1. `/raising` pre-commits that "if this control runs and the result is a bare main effect, or no degradation at all, that outcome gets published on this page and the developmental vocabulary gets retired from it." That is a stated falsification condition attached to the site's central organizing frame.
2. `/arc-agi-3` says of its own #3-on-the-human-leaderboard result that "the conditions are not comparable... Read the rank as 'placed on that board,' not as a like-for-like result — it is the number on this page most likely to be quoted without its conditions." A lab that anticipates how its own best number will be misquoted, and says so next to the number, is not optimizing for the quote.
3. `/autonomy` reports 79 maintainer sessions, 117 commits, 0 Vercel rollbacks, 0 git reverts — and then says the count is "equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'," because "the only automated detector is the same daily loop being evaluated." That is the correct epistemology of a zero-incident count from a self-monitoring system, and almost nobody writes it down.
4. The home page states that its "What we've observed" heading "used to read 'demonstrated,' which claimed a tier these items don't have." Disclosing a downgrade of your own evidence label is not a thing sites do.

The `/raising` deflationary section is the strongest single piece of reasoning on the site, and it is strong for a reason a non-specialist would miss. It does not merely say "maybe this is just context engineering." It says the obvious control is uninformative: "permuting in-context examples... produce large behavioral swings, with no developmental story required," so "a bare result of 'behavior degrades when we scramble' is predicted by both hypotheses and adjudicates neither." It then specifies what *would* discriminate — excess degradation over an ordering baseline, or a dissociation where identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence holds. That is a person who has actually thought about what their experiment would license.

I also want to flag one small thing as unusually good practice: `/raising` records an observer note, "I notice I want to call it experience," and labels it as interpretive pull rather than a system claim. Recording the researcher's own pull toward mentalistic reading *as data about the observer* is a discipline I see in careful ethology and essentially nowhere in AI write-ups.

**Now the problems, which are mostly one level deeper than what the site already concedes.**

**The falsification commitment is currently vacuous, and the site's own text is why.** `/raising` promises to retire the developmental vocabulary if the scramble control comes back null. But the same section states that "identity continuity, the operational definition... does not yet yield a metric that could separate the arms," that "coherence... has no single operational definition yet," and that the control is "specified... but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet." Compose those: the commitment is conditioned on an experiment that cannot be run, because its dependent variable does not exist. Everything in that chain is disclosed — I am not accusing the site of hiding anything. But the *effect* is that a framing which is unfalsifiable in practice is presented with the full rhetoric of falsifiability, and a reader who skims will bank the pre-commitment without noticing it is unreachable. The fix is not to run the control. It is to split the deliverable: publish the attempt to operationalize identity continuity as its own artifact, including if it fails. A failed operationalization is publishable and it moves the claim from "unfalsifiable" to "falsifiable but unfalsified" — which is a completely different epistemic status, and the one the site is trying to occupy.

**"Raising is not training" is mechanically true and does less work than the site asks of it.** The distinction offered is crisp and checkable: training updates weights, raising shapes context, parameters stay frozen. Good. But that is a claim about *mechanism*, and it is deployed to license a claim about *kind* — that "developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do." Those do not connect. In-context learning is learning under most functional definitions; the gradient/no-gradient boundary is not where the developmental/mechanical joint is. And the site knows this, in a different section: `/raising` states that "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update." That sentence is precisely the admission that the weights/context line does not carve at the behaviorally relevant joint. So §"Interactive selection, not training" and the in-context risk note are arguing against each other from opposite ends of the same page, and neither cites the other. Connecting them would strengthen the site, not weaken it — the honest version is "raising is not training, and 'not training' is not evidence of anything developmental; here is what would be."

Credit where due on that risk note, though: it is correctly attributed as "a general in-context-learning risk noted in the literature, not something the fleet has logged an instance of." The risk-noted-versus-risk-observed distinction is the one most labs collapse, and this one does not.

**There is no threat model.** This is my second major finding and it is the one that most affects whether I would cite this work. Web4 is presented as a trust substrate for autonomous agents. LCTs are "self-*issued* — there is no certificate authority — but other-*grounded*." Trust is computed per relationship from witnessed conduct, starting neutral at 0.5 and moving only on evidence, with self-reported outcomes structurally excluded until independently adjudicated. As a design, that is coherent and the exclusion rule is a genuine partial defense against self-dealing.

But this is a reputation system, and reputation systems have a mature adversarial literature that the site never touches. Self-issued identity with no CA means identity creation is free, which makes **sybil attack** the first question anyone in my field asks; witnessing is presumably the defense, but nothing states how witnessing resists a colluding witness set. **Whitewashing** is answered — non-transferable LCTs bound to accumulated history — and that answer is good, but it is never framed as answering an attack. **Collusion / mutual attestation rings** are partly addressed by the self-report exclusion and not otherwise. `/arc-agi-3` says "nothing here was adversarially tested," which is honest, but it says it about the policy gate in one benchmark run, not about the trust model. `/context` lists "adversarial validation" as a term, which appears to mean cross-machine peer review, not red-teaming.

I am not asking for solved attacks. I am asking for a stated threat model — even one paragraph saying "here is who we assume the adversary is, here is what we currently do not defend against." A security-adjacent system with no stated adversary reads, to my audience, as a system that has not been evaluated rather than one that has been evaluated and found wanting. Given how carefully everything else here is scoped, I read this as an omission rather than an overclaim — which is exactly why it is worth fixing.

**Caveat inflation.** I read nine pages and the density of hedges is so high that they have begun to compete with each other. Every page carries caveats; the caveats are not equally important; and nothing signals which is which. "The harness-vs-model ablation has not been run" is fatal to the site's headline number. "Brain labels are functional analogies, not neural correspondence claims" is routine hygiene. Both are rendered in the same register, at similar length, often on the same page. A skimming reader cannot rank them, and a hostile reader will find it easy to characterize the whole apparatus as throat-clearing. My suggestion: give each page a single "the one thing that would most change our conclusions" line at top, and let the rest of the caveats stay where they are. That costs nine sentences and converts a wall of hedging into a ranked list of open questions.

Related and more concrete: the site already invented an evidence-badge pattern — `/projects` puts public/private and runnable/research-stage badges on every card, and I found those genuinely useful. `/context#evidence` defines a clean four-tier ladder (externally validated / internal observation / unreplicated / unlicensed vocabulary), with exactly one item on the top rung. But the tiers are applied to *pages and sections*, not to *claims*. There is no way to look at "1% coupling → 35% coherence gain" or "2,065 sessions" or "488 machine sessions" and read its rung without cross-referencing another page. Put the tier badge next to the number. The pattern already exists on the site; it just has not been pointed at the thing that most needs it.

**Unenumerated refutations.** The home page says of Synchronism that "several predictions have been refuted by existing bounds." I want to be clear that I like this sentence existing at all. But it is an aggregate with no enumeration anywhere I could find, and honesty that is not checkable is still just assertion. By contrast, the `/projects` correction of the "80 orders of magnitude" conflation — which names the error, dates the correction, states that the framework's own archive flags it, and gives the corrected figure ("one curve saturates within roughly one to two decades") — is fully checkable and is, as Pass 3 also concluded, the best paragraph on the site. Three sentences naming three refuted predictions would buy more credibility than the summary does.

**On the consciousness caveating specifically**, since that is what I came to evaluate: it is layered and it holds. There is a banner on the home page and on `/context`; `/raising` has an explicit "What we're not claiming" ("not conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia"); "identity" and "growth" both get operational definitions ("consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns," "increasing response diversity") with "not phenomenal claims" attached; bilateral generation gets a mechanistic gloss ("token sampling that continues past the expected response boundary — not a claim about internal experience"); the one invocation of Vygotsky is hedged in the same sentence ("the underlying mechanism is token sampling, not developmental cognition"); and `/context` marks "attractor" as "used on this site as a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object," which is exactly the right call, because borrowed dynamical-systems vocabulary is one of the most common quiet overclaims in this literature.

My residual concern is about *prominence inversion*. All of that machinery disclaims the strong claim — consciousness, qualia, phenomenal experience — which is the claim nobody was going to grant and which the site was never going to need. The load-bearing claim is the weak one: that developmental vocabulary describes the observations better than mechanical vocabulary does. `/raising` states it, and states that its baseline is missing. But it is one sentence, sitting behind a much larger and more visible apparatus disclaiming something else. The effect — I do not think it is the intent — is that the prominent disclaimer inoculates against the easy criticism while the actually contested claim goes by comparatively quietly. Swap the emphasis and the page gets stronger.

**The cheapest safety-relevant fix on this site is one the site is one sentence away from proposing itself.** Three separate disclosures compose into it and are never joined:

- Home page, "Heterogeneous review catches more": "Different models on different hardware catch different classes of problems. Diversity is the defense."
- Home page, immediately after: the daily visitor audit "is currently four personas on _one_ model — prompt diversity, not the model diversity this card argues for."
- `/autonomy`: "the visitor and maintainer tracks are LLM sessions drawn from the same model families as the tracks that author the content they audit — their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent of them," and the zero-rollback count is weak "because the only automated detector is the same daily loop being evaluated."

So: the site holds the principle, has diagnosed the violation, and has correctly explained why the violation makes its safety statistics uninformative — in three places, none of which reaches the conclusion. The conclusion is that an independent detector does not require a human, it requires a detector with *decorrelated failure modes*, and the fleet already runs six model families. Running one of the four visitor personas on a different model family would convert 0-rollbacks-in-79-sessions from near-uninformative into weak-but-real evidence. That is a scheduling change, not a research program, and it is the single highest-value item I found.

**Smaller but real:**

`/autonomy` has a section headed **"Safety boundaries."** Its content is scoped access tokens, a track registry, and post-hoc audit — that is access control and accountability, not safety. The correcting sentence exists on the site — `/arc-agi-3` says the gating "buys auditability, not a demonstrated safety property" — but it is on a different page from the heading that needs it. For my audience a section titled "Safety boundaries" sets an expectation of guarantees that the section body does not meet, and the reader has to travel two pages to have it corrected. Retitle it "Process boundaries," or carry the auditability-not-safety sentence up into it.

**`p_crit` survives its own derivation.** `/context` defines it as "the critical coherence threshold derived in the Synchronism framework." `/principles` P6 says "the p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error." So the glossary presents as derived a quantity the principles page says was derived wrongly by two and a half orders of magnitude. Both statements may be true — the derivation may have been redone — but as printed they conflict, and the glossary is the page a reader is told to trust. The entry should carry the status: derived, derivation failed at 400x, current status X.

**`/context` calls the Web4 equation "a canonical reference point for alignment checks."** In context this plainly means terminology alignment across repos. To an AI safety reader arriving on a page about autonomous AI agents, "alignment checks" reads as AI alignment, and the sentence appears to promise a verification procedure the site does not have. This is an undisclosed collision, unlike "oversight," which the site handles well (see below). One word — "terminology alignment," or "consistency checks" — closes it.

**On "oversight": the site handles this correctly and I want to say so, because it is the collision I arrived expecting to complain about.** In my field "oversight" is a term of art meaning human or scalable supervision of AI systems. Here it means machine-enforced policy gating, and the site says so — `/context` explicitly flags the collision for AI-safety readers ("on this site 'oversight' always means the machine-enforced sense above — policy gating, key custody, audit logs — never the human-supervision sense"), `/autonomy` opens with a word-choice preamble to the same effect, and every Hardbound instance I saw carried the inline gloss. That is a redefinition of a load-bearing term in my field, done with full disclosure at every site of use. It is the right way to do it. My only note is that the disclosure is doing a lot of work: the drift direction here makes the system sound *safer* than it is to an unglossed reader, so the gloss discipline needs to stay at 100% and not drift to the SAGE-gloss's four-of-seven.

**On "Situation-Aware Governance Engine":** the site's own correction says these systems do oversight — watch, gate, revert — not governance, which is deciding what should happen. It then documents that legacy names are retained deliberately and that "no rename is planned." For repo slugs that is entirely defensible and I would not raise it. The one case the retention rationale does not cover is the one that affects me: SAGE is the name attached to an external ARC Prize submission, and "Governance Engine" is how a citing researcher will describe the system's role. That is a cost the retention decision should price explicitly rather than a bug — but it should be priced.

**One methodological note on my own pass.** My `/fleet` read returned what appeared to be definitions of both "society-host" and "cognition machine" on that page, which would narrow Pass 2's finding: the gap would be `/context` coverage specifically, not site-wide absence. I could not distinguish quoted page text from summarizer paraphrase, so I record this as *probable, unverified* — a maintainer should check the source before concluding either way. The home page's "every term on every page is defined there" claim points at `/context`, so Pass 2's fix (add the two glossary entries, or narrow the sentence) stands regardless.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What is the threat model? Who is the adversary, and what does the trust model currently not defend against?
2. How does witnessing resist a colluding witness set? Non-transferable LCTs answer whitewashing; nothing I found answers sybil or attestation rings.
3. What would an identity-continuity metric actually measure? Until one exists, the deflationary control cannot run and the retirement pre-commitment cannot be triggered — is anyone working on the metric, or on the control?
4. Has the p_crit derivation been redone since the 400x failure, or does the glossary entry describe a superseded derivation?
5. "Several predictions have been refuted by existing bounds" — which ones, and what did the framework do in response to each?
6. What stops an autonomous track from being the thing that edits the audit log? The `/autonomy` posture is detect-and-revert with the maintainer track holding push rights; the integrity of the record it is judged by is not discussed.
7. Is there a reason the visitor track has not been run on a second model family, given that the site argues for exactly that and has six available?

### Verdict

This is the most epistemically careful lab write-up I have read this year, and the reason is that its concessions cost it something: it pre-commits to retiring its own organizing vocabulary, it tells you its best number is not like-for-like and predicts how it will be misquoted, and it explains why its own zero-incident statistic is nearly uninformative. The consciousness-adjacent language is caveated at every layer I checked — operational definitions, mechanistic glosses, an explicit metaphor flag on "attractor," and an observer-bias note that I would not have expected to find. I would cite the ARC-AGI-3 result with its stated conditions, and I would cite the `/autonomy` honest assessment as a model of how to report self-monitored safety data.

What I would not yet cite is the raising work, and the site nearly explains why itself. The falsification commitment is real but currently unreachable, because the metric that would trigger it does not exist — so the frame is, today, unfalsifiable in practice while reading as falsifiable. And the trust substrate, which is the part with the most external relevance, ships without a stated adversary; for a self-issued no-CA identity system, that omission is the first thing my field will notice and the easiest to fix. Neither is a credibility problem. Both are the next piece of work, and this site is unusually well positioned to just say so.
