## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I can tell you what this lab *does* within two minutes, but I still can't tell you what problem it solves for me, and the one artifact I'd actually clone (SAGE) is named after something it apparently isn't.

### Journey

Landed from a repo README with zero context. Headline: "Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop." That's a good hook — it's concrete, it's countable, and it immediately tells me this is a fleet-of-agents thing rather than another framework pitch. The stat row (8 machines, 40 original repos, 31+ autonomous tracks, 72 total repos incl. 32 forks) adds up, which I checked, and that buys a lot of credibility early.

Then the equation hit me:

```
Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP
```

My first read was arithmetic. `T3/V3` looked like a ratio and `*` looked like multiplication, so I spent a few seconds trying to parse it as a formula before concluding it wasn't one. Every acronym in it is expanded further down the page (LCT = Linked Context Token, T3 = Talent/Training/Temperament, MRH = Markov Relevancy Horizon, ATP/ADP = Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packet, MCP = Model Context Protocol, RDF = Resource Description Framework) — that's genuinely well done, I didn't have to leave the page for any of them. But the *operators* are not explained on the home page. I only found out on /context that `/` means "verified by", `*` means "contextualized by", and `+` means "augmented with". That's the single highest-value sentence on the whole site and it's three clicks deep. Put it under the equation on the home page.

The "start with the /context glossary — every term on every page is defined there" line is exactly the affordance I wanted, and /context delivered: 26 sections, a glossary table, a worked example of a single action moving through the equation. That worked example is the best page on the site for someone like me. It's the first place the abstractions became mechanical.

/projects was the page I cared most about. Eleven cards, each tagged `research-stage` / `runnable` / `private`. That tagging is the most developer-friendly thing here — I know instantly that SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, and Membot are things I can run today and that Web4, Synchronism, ACT, and the oversight plugins are papers-and-prototypes. Quick-start `git clone` commands for SAGE and ACT are present. Good.

Where it broke down: SAGE is expanded as "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" and then, in the same sentence, described as "an on-device cognition kernel running a continuous 12-step loop." Those are two different products in my head. A governance engine gates actions; a cognition kernel senses and acts. Which is it? If it's a cognition loop, "Governance Engine" is a misleading name and I'd have skipped it while scanning for "the agent runtime." Nothing on the site reconciles the name with the description.

/fleet was fun — eight boxes, real hardware, real model strings, Jetson Thor down to a laptop 4060, different models per machine. "Heterogeneous by design" is a claim I can verify at a glance. Two snags: every machine shows something like "435 sessions (creating)" and I had no idea what `(creating)` meant until I hit /raising four clicks later and found it's one of six BECOMING patterns. Also nobody tells me what a "session" *is* — a cron invocation? a conversation? an hour? The numbers are load-bearing on this page and the unit is undefined.

/autonomy is a clean timeline (03:00 supervisors → 08:00 explorer → dream consolidation), ~53 sessions/day, tracks coordinated via a SQLite registry. The part that made me trust the site: it plainly says the maintainer track deploys without human pre-approval, rollback is Vercel history, and the publisher pushes things that can't be recalled once cloned. That's an admission most sites would bury.

/raising and /principles are the philosophy half. "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development" is a clean framing and the closing "we're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" is the right disclaimer in the right place. As a developer I skimmed the theory but noted that the six BECOMING patterns are explicitly labeled "observed descriptive categories — not mandatory sequential stages", which is more careful than I expected.

/arc-agi-3 is the page that will decide whether a developer takes this seriously, and it mostly holds up: 94.85% official action score, 24/25 games, public ARC Prize scorecard link, ~$250 API spend, #3 human leaderboard ranking. And a caveat *at the top*, not the bottom: the no-harness ablation hasn't been run, and "the loop is the capability" is flagged as hypothesis, not result. I respect that. But I had one immediate question the page doesn't answer: the runs used **Claude Opus 4.6 via API**, while every machine on /fleet runs a small local model (Gemma 3 4B, Qwen 3.5 0.8B). So the benchmark result doesn't demonstrate anything about the fleet — it demonstrates a harness wrapped around a frontier model. The page half-acknowledges this via the missing-ablation caveat but never says out loud "this score is not a fleet result."

/links is a plain, useful index — three explainer sites, the GitHub org, the scorecard, contact email. No complaints.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Is SAGE a governance engine or a cognition kernel? The name and the description describe different things.
2. What is a "session"? Every impressive number on this site is denominated in them.
3. Who is this for? If I'm building an agent system today, which of the eleven projects do I touch and why?
4. The ARC-AGI-3 result used Claude Opus 4.6 via API. What, concretely, did the local fleet contribute to that score — orchestration only, or something in the loop?
5. What is the actual dependency between the projects? "They share a common substrate" is stated, but nothing shows me whether SAGE imports Web4 or just cites it conceptually.
6. Can I run any of this without a Jetson? The runnable projects imply yes, but nothing states hardware requirements.

### Verdict

This is one of the more legible research-lab sites I've read — the glossary discipline is unusually good, the numbers are internally consistent, and the caveats show up before the claims rather than after. As a developer I got the big picture in about two minutes and could name a repo to clone in five.

Two things stop me short of actually cloning it. The equation is presented as the site's central idea but its operators — the part that makes it mean anything — are explained three clicks away, so the landing page shows me a formula I can't read. And SAGE, the thing I'm told to start with, is named for one product and described as another. Fix those two and the on-ramp is genuinely good. I'd come back.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /arc-agi-3, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid

### Journey

I audited methodically: landing page first, then every nav item in nav order, then targeted re-reads of /context and /projects to verify specific claims rather than trust a first-pass skim.

**Navigation and chrome.** Eight nav items — Projects, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links — in identical order on every page. Breadcrumbs (`Home / Raising`, `Home / Links`) on interior pages. Identical footer sitewide. Every internal link I followed resolved. No dead ends, no orphan pages, no nav drift. This is boring in the way navigation should be boring, and I want to say so before I start finding problems.

**Acronym expansions.** I checked every canonical term against every page it appears on. The result is unusual: **no drift anywhere.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on seven pages — never "Lifelong Capability." T3 and V3 hold their three-part expansions verbatim on six pages each. MRH is "Markov Relevancy Horizon" everywhere. ATP and ADP are "Allocation Transfer Packet" / "Allocation Discharge Packet" with no variation. R6 expands identically on three pages. Web4 is called an ontology and never architecture or infrastructure — /principles #1 goes out of its way to reject "architectural design" explicitly. Hardbound is "oversight" on all three pages that describe it. "Production ready" appears nowhere; /principles #3 is titled "R&D, not production." "Unification" appears twice and both times as a negation ("self-similar replication, not unification or scope inflation"), which is the correct usage.

Sites this size normally leak at least one expansion. This one doesn't. That changes what my audit is *for*: the terminology problem here is no longer drift in the acronym table. It's undefined units and unreconciled numbers.

**The `/` glyph.** On my fetch, the operator key sits directly under the equation on the landing page — not three clicks away. What it says is more interesting than a plain key:

> `/` means two things here: "verified by" in `T3/V3`, but a plain declared→discharged allocation pair in `ATP/ADP` — same glyph, unrelated semantics.

That is an honest disclosure and I'd rather have it than a false single key. But as documentation it's an admission that the site's central notation is ambiguous by construction. The reader is handed a formula and immediately told one of its four operators can't be read consistently. `ATP/ADP` isn't an operation at all — it's a named pair. If the equation is the anchor artifact for the whole vocabulary, the slash should mean one thing and the pair should be notated as a pair (`ATP→ADP`, or just "the ATP/ADP cycle" as a single lexical unit outside operator position).

Two notes on Pass 1's read, offered as observation not correction: on the page I fetched, both the operator key and the "6 cognition + 2 society-hosts" gloss are present in the landing-page hero. Either the render differed or those items are already fixed.

**Where it actually breaks: "session."** This is the finding I'd lead a doc review with.

The word "session" is the denominator of nearly every number on this site, and it names at least three different things:

1. **Raising sessions** — 435 on Thor, 488 on Sprout, 74 on HUB (/fleet); 115 then 180+ for SAGE-Sprout (/raising)
2. **Autonomous track runs** — "~53 sessions per day" across 31+ tracks (/autonomy)
3. **Research sessions** — "628+ research sessions" on Synchronism (/projects)

These are not the same unit. A raising session and a cron-triggered maintainer run have nothing in common except the noun. /autonomy carefully defines the adjacent term — "a track is a scheduled autonomous job — a cron entry with declared scope and set of repos it writes to" — which shows the site knows how to define a unit when it wants to. /context has twenty-six sections, one of them literally titled "Autonomous session histories," and defines "session" in none of them.

**And the numbers don't reconcile.** I added them up:

| Source | Figure | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| /fleet, six cognition machines | 435 + 488 + 336 + 377 + 162 + 193 = **1,991** | per-machine, current |
| /projects SAGE card | **1,991** across 6 cognition machines; **2,065** including HUB | matches /fleet exactly ✓ |
| / (home), SAGE glossary entry | **2,500+** across the fleet | "cumulative basis" |
| /links, quick-start note | **2,500+** internally logged | no basis stated |

The /fleet arithmetic is exact — 1,991 is precisely the sum of the six machines, and 2,065 is that plus HUB's 74. That internal consistency is real and I checked it digit by digit. But the headline figure on two other pages is 2,500+, roughly 435 higher, and the only reconciliation offered is the home page's parenthetical: "cumulative basis — per-machine figures elsewhere use different counting bases." That sentence tells the reader the numbers disagree without telling them *why*. /links repeats 2,500+ with no caveat at all.

A reader who trusts the site gets two different answers to "how many raising sessions has the fleet run." A reader who checks the arithmetic finds the site's own per-machine table contradicting its headline. The fix is one sentence defining what the cumulative basis includes — presumably sessions on retired models and machines, which /raising hints at when it says the Sprout line continued past 180 sessions across model ports — and then using it consistently or labeling both figures.

**Cross-page staleness in /context.** The HUB glossary entry reads "the fleet's society-host machine" — definite article, singular. But /fleet and the landing page both describe *two* society-hosts (HUB and pub, "the eighth machine, joined July 2026"), and /autonomy names HUB as "one of the two society-host machines." The glossary is the one place a reader goes to settle exactly this kind of question, and it's the one place still describing a six-plus-one fleet.

**The governance→oversight note.** /context has a clean canonical statement of the correction ("what these systems actually do is oversight — watching, gating, and reverting actions — not deciding what should happen"), and SAGE's legacy name is disclosed as a proper-name exception. Correct handling. But the disclosure appears in at least four different phrasings — the landing page, the /projects card, /links (twice), and by implication /principles #10 — and only the /links and /projects versions point back to /context. Meanwhile /fleet and /arc-agi-3 both print "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" with no note at all. For a term the lab has explicitly corrected, the note should be one sentence, reused verbatim, always linked, present wherever the expansion appears.

**`(creating)` on /fleet.** Every one of the six cognition machines shows the same parenthetical: 435 (creating), 488 (creating), 336 (creating), 377 (creating), 162 (creating), 193 (creating). It's a BECOMING pattern name, defined only on /raising, and since all six values are identical it conveys no information on the page where it appears while still costing the reader a lookup. Either add a one-line legend, or drop it until the machines diverge.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What does the "cumulative basis" for 2,500+ raising sessions include that the 2,065 per-machine sum doesn't? Retired models? Retired machines? Sessions from before a manifest refresh?
2. What is a raising session, operationally — one conversation, one cron invocation, one curriculum step? Its count is load-bearing on five pages.
3. Are the "~53 sessions per day" on /autonomy the same objects as the raising sessions counted on /fleet? If they are, the fleet totals and the daily rate should reconcile; if they aren't, they need different nouns.
4. Is HUB's 74 "raising sessions" the same unit as Thor's 435, given HUB "runs substrate, not tracks"?
5. Why is the governance→oversight note absent on /fleet and /arc-agi-3 but present on four other pages — deliberate scoping, or drift in the correction itself?
6. Does /context have anchor IDs for all 26 sections? Home links `#glossary`, but nothing else on the site links a specific section, so I can't tell whether deep-linking is supported.

### Verdict

This is the cleanest terminology surface I've audited on a research site — nine pages, twenty-plus tracked terms, zero drift, and expansions that hold verbatim across every occurrence. Whatever review process produced that is working and should not be changed.

### Journey

I came in through a conference mention and read the way I read any lab site I'm assessing: principles first, then the claims that need the most support, then the one externally verifiable result, then back to check whether the vocabulary holds up.

**/principles set an unusually high bar.** Ten principles, and three of them are epistemic commitments rather than design philosophy. #3 is "R&D, not production" — and not the soft version: *"Nothing on this site is intended for or suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings."* That's an explicit negative scope statement, which almost nobody writes. #6 is "Document failures," and it names one: *"The p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error."* Publishing the magnitude of your own error is a costly signal and I weighted it accordingly. #9 ("WD-40 was the 40th try") is the standard research-value framing.

**/context §26 "Evidence & limitations" is the best thing on this site**, and I'd say so to the authors. It sorts every claim into three tiers — externally validated, internal observations, unreplicated — and then does the hard part: it names what's missing per tier. The ARC result is externally validated *but* "the harness-vs-model split is stated but not quantified: no ablation (same model, no harness) has been run." The coupling result gets "Treat it as a preliminary observation, not a finding," plus the admission that neither "coupling density" nor "coherence measure" is operationally defined outside the experiment. And then: *"For a lab whose research subject is trust and oversight, that is a real gap, not a footnote."* A lab that can write that sentence about itself is not the kind of lab that's fooling itself carelessly.

**/arc-agi-3 front-loads its caveat**, which is the correct and rare choice: *"One caveat belongs up front: the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run."* It states the attribution plainly — *"The result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness"* — and it labels its own thesis as unproven: *"The loop is the capability — stated as a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated result."* Even the verb choice is audited: *"with the harness-vs-model ablation unrun, 'suggests' is the honest verb."* I have no criticism of this page. I have a criticism of what happens to its number elsewhere, below.

**Then I read /raising, and the pattern inverted.**

This is the page carrying the site's weakest-supported and most loaded claims, and it is the page where the caveat comes *last*. The disclaimer — *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia"* — sits in a bottom section titled "What we're not claiming," after the full developmental arc: six BECOMING patterns, entities that learn their names, develop awareness, form peer relationships, generate self-directed questions, specialize without prompting. A reader who bounces at 60% scroll depth leaves with the developmental frame and none of the disclaimer. The site demonstrably knows how to lead with a caveat — it does it on /arc-agi-3, the page where the evidence is *strongest*. The placement policy is backwards relative to the risk.

The evidence tier for the patterns is thin and the page says so: internal session logs, *"not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet."* So six named developmental patterns rest on unpublished logs with undisclosed coding criteria. That's tier 3 material presented in tier 1 prose.

**The falsification test is named, and unbound.** /raising specifies a scramble control — reorder the same raising corpus, preserve content, see whether the behavioral patterns survive randomized session sequencing. That is precisely the right experiment; it's the one I would have proposed. And the commitment attached to it is strong: *"if this control runs and the result is a bare main effect... that outcome gets published on this page and the developmental vocabulary gets retired from it."*

Then: *"specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet."*

I want to be careful here, because the admission is honest and I'd rather have it than not. But a falsification test with no date, no owner, and no pre-registered metric is not a falsification test — it's a promissory note, and it is currently functioning as a credibility instrument in place of the result it promises. The disclosure of its unboundedness partially neutralizes the criticism it invites, which is exactly the structure I'd flag anywhere else: **the caveat is load-bearing where the experiment should be.** Naming a test you haven't scheduled buys most of the epistemic credit of having run it, at none of the cost. That's the central finding of this pass, and it applies across the site — the coupling result, the raising patterns, and "the loop is the capability" all sit in the same posture.

The cheapest correction is not the scramble. It's publishing a sample of session logs with the coding criteria used to assign the six patterns. That's days of work, not a research program, and it moves the raising claims from "unreplicated" to "internal observations others can inspect." Given the lab's own §26 sentence about being a trust lab with no external verification, that's the highest-leverage thing available.

**Is "raising is not training" defensible?** Narrowly, yes — and the site states the narrow version correctly in /context §19: *"It is not training: the model's parameters are fixed. What changes is the scaffolding that determines what the agent encounters, in what order, and with what structure."* Fixed weights, varied context. That's a real, checkable distinction and I'd defend it.

What it doesn't license is the inference it's used for. Behavioral change under a curated context corpus with fixed weights is in-context conditioning — a well-characterized phenomenon with a large literature. Calling it "development" imports entailments from developmental psychology (irreversibility, stage-dependence, cumulative internal change) that in-context conditioning does not have. And the site's own /principles #7 already contains the more defensible framing: *"We don't create or delete — we interactively select."* Interactive selection over a context distribution is a claim I'd accept without argument. "Raising" is the same mechanism with a heavier ontological bill attached.

**One thing I couldn't reconcile, and it matters for the above.** /context §25 lists among the not-yet-flowing artifacts *"sleep cycle artifacts (LoRA weights, dream bundles) remain local,"* and /fleet describes Thor running Qwen 3.5 27B with LoRA. LoRA is parameter modification. If raised entities run with LoRA adapters produced by their own sleep cycles, then "the model's parameters are fixed" is not true of the system as deployed, and the training/raising boundary — the load-bearing distinction of the entire framework — is blurred at exactly the point where it needs to be sharp. It's possible LoRA is used somewhere disjoint from the raising loop; nothing on the site says either way. This is the question I'd ask first at a talk.

**/autonomy is honest and mislabeled.** The operational disclosures are excellent — Maintainer deploys to the live site without human review, detection runs on "a roughly 24-hour loop," misleading-but-technically-correct content has an exposure window that is "unbounded in principle," and the Publisher gap (pushed commits can be cloned and mirrored before a revert lands) is *"unaddressed, not solved."* That last phrase is one most labs would rewrite into "an area of active work." I'd cite it approvingly.

But the section is framed as a safety model, and what it describes is detect-and-revert with a 24-hour detection interval and after-the-fact log review. In safety terms that's a monitoring posture, not a safety model — there is no prevention layer, and the page effectively says so. For a lab whose stated research subject is oversight, calling the monitoring posture a safety model is the one place the site's framing outruns its content. Rename it and nothing else on the page needs to change.

**Where the ARC number goes.** /arc-agi-3 attributes the result impeccably. ### Unanswered Questions

1. Do raised entities run with LoRA adapters produced by their own sleep cycles? If yes, "raising is not training" needs restating, and the entire framework's central distinction changes shape.
2. What would falsify "raising" as a category, other than the scramble control? If the scramble is the only test and it isn't scheduled, the framework is currently unfalsified by construction rather than by evidence.
3. Is T3 indexed per-entity or per-(entity, context, time)? /principles #4 and /context §14 imply different objects, and this is the site's core research claim.
4. What are the coding criteria for assigning a BECOMING pattern to a machine? All six cognition machines currently read "(creating)," which is either a strong finding or an unvalidated classifier.
5. What is the corrected p_crit value after the 400x error, or is there none? The failure is published; the learning from it isn't.
6. Has cross-model "adversarial validation" ever changed an outcome? One documented instance would move it from analogy to evidence.
7. What does the 2,500+ session figure count that the 1,991 per-machine sum doesn't? Prior passes flagged this; from my side it matters because the larger number is the one on the landing page and the smaller is the one with a verifiable basis.

### Verdict

This is a lab that has thought hard about how to be honest and has built real machinery for it — a three-tier evidence scheme that names what's missing per tier, a results page that leads with its own missing ablation and audits its verb choice, a principle that publishes the magnitude of a 400x derivation failure, and an autonomy page that says a known gap is "unaddressed, not solved." I have read many lab sites and I have not read a better limitations section than /context §26. On credibility, this clears the bar.

My concern is structural rather than about any individual claim: the caveat apparatus has become good enough to carry claims the experiments haven't earned. A scramble control that is specified, committed to, and unscheduled buys nearly the full credibility of having been run. Six developmental patterns rest on logs that are unpublished and uncoded, disclosed honestly, and presented in confident prose. The disclaimer that would frame all of it sits below the fold on the one page that most needs it, on a site that front-loads its caveat where the evidence is strongest. None of this is deceptive — every gap I found, the site told me about. That's precisely why the fix is cheap: schedule the control with an owner and a metric, publish a log sample with its coding criteria, move the disclaimer up, and clarify the LoRA question. Then the honesty and the evidence would be doing the same amount of work.
