## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I know what to clone and why within about three minutes, but the pages spend a lot of words correcting themselves, and I still can't tell you crisply what runs SAGE versus what runs the "31+ tracks."

### Journey

Landed on the home page from a repo README. The headline ("Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop.") is a decent hook. The first paragraph is not — "trust-native ontology," "raise each other," "heterogeneous fleet" in one sentence. The thing that actually sold me was the third block, **"The problem this addresses"**: agents already act on their own, there's no general way to know if one will behave, platforms don't scale and crypto proves key custody not conduct, so compute trust per relationship from witnessed interaction. That's a value proposition a developer gets instantly. It should be the first paragraph, not the third.

The "New here?" callout pointing to a vocabulary primer at the bottom of the page is exactly what I needed, and the primer is good. LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC, MCP, RDF all explained in a sentence or two each. The note that `/` means two different things in the equation is refreshingly honest, and my immediate reaction was "then why is it the same glyph?" — but at least they said it.

The three stat cards are fine. The paragraph right under them re-explaining the repo counts ("Repo counts, stated once…") reads like an internal footnote that leaked onto the page.

Went to **/projects**. This is the page I actually wanted. Status labels (runnable vs research-stage, public vs private) are exactly the kind of thing a developer scans for, and the "Want to run something rather than read? Start with SAGE" line answers my main question. The Quick start block at the bottom with two `git clone` lines is perfect. Downsides: the SAGE card has a 60-word parenthetical about session counts being "a mixed-basis sum — three addends are whole-machine totals, three are current-model-line counts" and a note about what the card "previously said" with a correction date. The Synchronism card has the same pattern. I get that this is honesty, but for a first-time reader it's changelog noise inside a product card. Put the correction history somewhere I can find it if I care.

**/fleet** was fun to read — real hardware, real model names, per-machine roles. The "brain (functional analogy)" labels were more interesting than I expected. Two things confused me: "Synthesis Pool — Account 1" and "Oversight Pool — Account 2" as section headers made no sense until the "Resource Pool Management" section at the bottom explained they're two Claude Code subscription accounts with different weekly budgets. That's a genuinely interesting engineering constraint and I'd move that explanation up. Second, the word **"hestia"** appears twice ("hestia trust tracker," "hestia identity") and is never defined anywhere on the site. I checked the glossary. It's not there.

**/autonomy** was the clearest page on the site. Track = cron job with declared scope and repos it writes to. SQLite registry to prevent write conflicts. Visitor → Maintainer → Explorer loop. The "Honest Assessment" section, especially the maintainer track having zero rollbacks across 79 sessions and then immediately saying "read that as weak evidence," is the most credible thing on the site. It's also very long; the two "Known Oversight Gap" blocks are each a wall of text.

**/raising** admits up front it's the softest page. Six patterns with a name (BECOMING), the deflationary alternative ("might just be context engineering") stated plainly, and a commitment to publish the null result if the control runs. As a developer I mostly skimmed it. The thing I noticed: they say the control is "specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner." Fine, but that's been true long enough that they're citing it from three other pages.

**/principles**: ten short items. Principle 6 mentions "The p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error" without saying what p_crit is on this page. Principle 2 leans on "Hill function kinetics" with no explanation here (it's explained on /projects). Otherwise readable.

**/context** (nav label says "Glossary," URL says /context, page title says "Context" — pick one) is enormous: 26 sections. The glossary table itself is great and I'd link to it from the README. The rest is essay.

**/arc-agi-3**: strongest page for credibility. One real external number (94.85% action score, ARC Prize scorecard link), a clear statement that the harness-vs-model ablation hasn't been run, and the "#3 on the human leaderboard" line immediately followed by "the conditions are not comparable." Good. The note that a 4B model with a membot cartridge "understands game mechanics correctly; the same model without one thinks it's placing black squares" is the most concrete engineering claim on the site and I wanted a link to that trace.

**/links**: the quick-start here is actually the real one — init command, cargo build, env vars, dashboard on localhost:8760. This is better than the /projects Quick start and I only found it because /projects told me to go here. Contact email present. "Ask in an issue first, no formal contribution policy" is honest.

### Unanswered Questions
1. What actually executes the 31+ tracks — Claude Code sessions, SAGE, or both? And does SAGE (the Rust daemon) run continuously on each machine or only during raising sessions?
2. What is "hestia"? It sounds like the actual trust-tracking / identity component on the fleet, which makes it odd that it's the one thing not in the glossary.
3. If I clone SAGE and run the daemon on my laptop with gemma3:4b, what do I see on the dashboard at localhost:8760? A screenshot or a "first five minutes" description would close the loop.
4. Is there a way for an outside machine to join the "Web4 Fleet" society, or is that what pub's public hub is for and it's not live yet? (I think the latter, from /fleet, but it's not said in one place.)
5. Where do the two Claude Code accounts fit in the ATP/ADP story? /fleet says the pools are "a human-scale version of ATP/ADP" — is the weekly budget literally tracked as ATP, or is that a metaphor?

### Verdict
As a developer with no Web4 background, I got the pitch, found the entry point (SAGE, three commands to a dashboard), and came away believing the ARC-AGI-3 number because the page arguing for it spends as much text on what it doesn't show as what it does. The site's main weakness is that it argues with its own past versions in front of newcomers — correction notes inside project cards, repeated caveats across five pages — and it never states in one sentence how the Claude Code tracks and the SAGE kernel relate. Fix those two, define "hestia," and I'd send this link to a colleague.

### Journey

Started at the landing page and did what I always do first: read the nav, the title bar, and the H1 before any body text. Nav is nine items and identical on all nine pages (Projects, Glossary, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links, plus the 4-lab logo home link). Good. The nav item labeled **Glossary** goes to a route called **/context**, whose browser title is "Glossary | 4-lab", whose H1 is **"How Knowledge Flows"**, and whose second heading is "Glossary at a glance." That is four names for one page. Every other page in the nav has label = title = H1 (Fleet → "Fleet — Eight Machines" → "The Fleet"; Autonomy → "Autonomy — The Daily Cycle" → "Autonomous Cycles"). This one doesn't.

Home page: the acronyms that appear above the Vocabulary primer are few — SAGE is expanded on first use in Key projects, ARC-AGI-3 is expanded, and the equation block itself carries its own inline key. Two "New here?" callouts point down to the primer and across to /context. That works. What doesn't: the H1 says "Eight machines," and the "What's happening right now" section says "Six machines, six brain components," and the six-plus-two split (cognition machines plus two society-hosts) is only stated on /fleet. A reader who counts will stop.

/projects: the intro paragraph says "the cards expand each on first use" and I checked that claim card by card — it holds. LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, R6, SNARC, TPM, FIDO2, WASM, ACP are all expanded where they first appear. The Hardbound card re-expands LCT after the Web4 card already did; harmless. The one unexpanded term is "Cosmos SDK," which is a product name, not an acronym, so I'll allow it.

/fleet: this is the page with the terminology problems. The pointer paragraph near the top reads: "Vocabulary used in the cards (T3/V3, MRH, SNARC, LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation), MCP (Model Context Protocol), crystallization, chapter ledger, chapter law) is defined in /context/glossary." I fetched /context/glossary. It's a 404. Every other page uses /context or /context#glossary. Separately, T3 and V3 are not expanded in that pointer, not expanded on any machine card, and only get their expansions in the "Peer-to-peer, no central coordinator" section at the bottom of the page. MRH is expanded in the Nomad card but the CBP card (two cards later) drops the bare phrase "MRH composer architect" with no gloss on "composer." And **hestia** appears three times (trust tracker, identity, "hestia's own internal field name") with no definition on this page or in the glossary. Pass 1 flagged this too; I confirm it is absent from the /context table.

/autonomy: clean. The "word-choice note" on 'oversight' at the top is exactly the kind of scoping note I'd ask for. ATP and ADP are expanded on first use in Safety boundaries. "Registry" is used seven or eight times and never defined as a noun beyond "SQLite" — acceptable for the audience but a one-line gloss ("the registry: an SQLite table of which track owns which repo right now") wouldn't hurt.

/raising: the intro says, verbatim, "Web4 vocabulary on this page (T3, V3, MRH, LCT, LoRA) is expanded inline on first use." It is not. Pattern 3: Relating uses "per-peer T3 tensor values" with no expansion. T3 is expanded four sections later in Foundational principles. The page makes a testable claim about itself and fails it.

/principles: every acronym expanded inline on first use (SAGE in 1, RDF/T3/V3 in 4, LoRA in 7), p_crit carries an italic definition line ("the critical coherence threshold derived in the Synchronism framework") — Pass 1 reported it undefined here; it is defined. "Hill function kinetics" in Principle 2 is the only unglossed technical term. Principle 10 is the site's terminology policy stated as a principle, which I appreciated.

/context: the "Glossary at a glance" table is the reference asset of the site and its expansions are canonical across the board. But the column is labeled "One line" and at least six entries (Autonomous, Emergence, V3, MRH, Identity continuity, Policy) run 80–150 words. "At a glance" and "One line" are both false for those rows; the narrative sections below the table exist for exactly that overflow. Also a cross-reference bug: the V3 row says "the ADP→ATP recharge named under ADP **above**," but ADP is two rows **below** V3 in the table order (T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP). And TPM 2.0 and FIDO2 are referenced on this page without expansion while /projects expands them.

/links: 4-Life card mentions "karma" undefined; the ARC-SAGE entry gives two percentages in one breath — "94.85% official ARC Prize action score (…; 24/25 games, 96.0%)" — with no label saying what the second number measures. The same unlabeled pair appears on the /fleet CBP card. The quick start is the best-structured block on the site: prerequisites, numbered steps, expected output, source-of-truth pointer.

/arc-agi-3: the top-of-page "New here?" inline key (eight acronyms, each with canonical expansion, one link to the glossary) is the pattern I'd ask every page to adopt. It should be a shared component.

### Journey

I came in expecting the usual: a hobbyist fleet, "emergent identity," a consciousness claim buried in a metaphor. I read /raising first because that's where those claims would live.

The page opens by telling me it is the softest page on the site and that "everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more." It defines its two load-bearing words operationally — "by 'identity' we mean consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns," "by 'growth' we mean increasing response diversity and phase-appropriate task success rates" — before using either. It has a section titled "What we're not claiming" which says, in one sentence, no consciousness, no sentience, no qualia, and that the framework claim is comparative with an acknowledged missing baseline. The "Memoriescape" anecdote — an entity-coined word for "the shape of memories you can sense but not access" — is exactly the kind of thing that usually gets presented as evidence of inner life; here it's tagged "observational data about token-production behavior — not as a claim about phenomenal awareness." And the line "I notice I want to call it experience" is labeled as "the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim about the entity's experience." I have not seen a lab label its own anthropomorphic reflex as data before. That is the right move.

So the consciousness-adjacent question is answered: yes, caveated, and caveated well.

Then I read the control specification, and this is where I started writing notes. The deflationary control is described precisely — a scramble (same corpus, same token volume, permuted order) or a yoked control (entity A raised on entity B's history at matched volume). Status: "specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet." The stated blocker: "pre-registering one requires an operational definition of identity continuity" and there is "no single operational definition yet." Two problems. First, the site *gives* an operational definition of identity on the home page and on /fleet ("consistent session-to-session interaction patterns, accumulated experience, and raising curriculum") and then says on /raising that none exists. Either that definition is enough to build a metric from or it isn't a definition. Looking closer, I think I see why it isn't: two of its three components — accumulated experience and raising curriculum — are the *treatment*, not an outcome. You can't measure whether raising preserved identity using the raising inputs as the measure. The site should say that, because it's the real reason and it's more interesting than "definitional." Second, and more important: the site already has an operational *growth* metric ("response diversity and phase-appropriate task success rates"). A yoked or scrambled control on growth is runnable today with no continuity definition at all. The blocker is stated as definitional; for half the framework claim it is purely logistical. The authors appear not to have noticed that their own definitions split this way.

The BECOMING phases are presented as "six observed patterns." Then, in Foundational principles, "Graduated tool introduction": "Tools are introduced in stages aligned to developmental phases. Stage 1 (Sensing): time awareness. Stage 2 (Relating): world awareness. Stage 3 (Questioning): agency … Each stage adds capability only when the entity has demonstrated readiness at the previous level." Read those two together. If a raiser unlocks world-awareness tools at the Relating stage, then "Relating" behavior appearing at that stage is partly the curriculum's schedule showing up in the transcript. This is the standard circularity worry in developmental-stage research and it's not mentioned anywhere on the page. The page is candid about the missing baseline; it is silent about the confound that operates even with a baseline. The test is simple and cheap: do phase-N behaviors ever appear before stage-N tools are granted? If yes, the phases are the entity's; if only after, they are the curriculum's.

"Interactive selection, not training" — is it defensible? As a description of method, yes: weights are frozen, so it is literally not gradient training. The page goes further in the right direction than I expected: it says "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update," and then connects that directly to the fact that the fleet's actual check is "detect-and-revert, not pre-approval." That is a real safety point, correctly made, and honest about the gap. Where the framing is weaker: "we probe what the model responds to … adjust context to resonate, and reinforce what works." Reinforce *what works by whose criterion*? Selection over hundreds of sessions by an unblinded observer with an acknowledged "interpretive pull" is experimenter-expectancy by another name. The site admits on /context that there is "no external, blinded, or third-party check anywhere in this loop yet," but that sentence is on the glossary page, not next to the method it applies to. "We don't create new behaviors" is also doing rhetorical work: selection that reinforces some responses and not others over 400+ sessions is shaping, whatever the weight status. The site doesn't deny this; it just doesn't say it.

### Verdict
On the question I came to answer — does this lab overclaim? — no. Consciousness-adjacent language is disclaimed in one clean sentence, the observer's own anthropomorphic pull is logged as data rather than evidence, every number carries its evidence tier, and the autonomy page says out loud that its zero-rollback record is weak evidence because the auditor shares the author's blind spots. I would hold this up as an example of how to write about raising work without claiming what you can't show. Fix those three and the site goes from "honest about what it hasn't done" to "has done the thing." Would return, and would read the preprint if one appears.
