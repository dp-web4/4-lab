## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understood what this lab is inside two minutes and I believe them more than most sites like this — but the one number that made me click (94.85% on ARC-AGI-3) means something different than the landing page led me to think.

### Journey

Came from a `dp-web4` repo README with no context. Landing page headline — "Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop." — did its job. I knew immediately this was a fleet-of-machines research thing and not a product.

Second paragraph hits me with "trust-native ontology," and I braced for a wall of undefined jargon. Then it did something I didn't expect: it parenthetically told me *which sense* of "ontology" it meant (knowledge-representation, not philosophy), and immediately followed with a caveat that words like "raise" and "identity" are functional descriptions, not consciousness claims. That's the paragraph that bought my trust. And there's a "New here? Start with the /context glossary" pointer plus an inline vocabulary primer further down the page, so every acronym in the Web4 equation is expanded before I'm asked to care about it. Genuinely well done — I did not have to leave the page to parse the page.

Went to **/projects** next, because that's where a developer goes. The `runnable` / `research-stage` / `private` labels on each card are the single most useful thing on the site for me. Web4 is honestly labeled "specification and schemas without runnable demo" — I've never seen a research site admit that on the card itself. SAGE, SNARC, Membot, 4-Life are marked runnable. Great. So... how do I run one? Nothing on the card. No install line, no language, no `git clone`. I went hunting.

Hunting took me to **/fleet**, which was fun but not what I needed. Eight machines, each with hardware, model, and a "role." The roles are neuroanatomy — "thalamic router function," "cerebellum/habit compiler function," "episodic memory function." I get that the fleet is being described as a brain by analogy, but nothing on the page tells me whether that's a real architectural constraint (does the thalamic router actually route?) or a naming convention. Also every session count has a bare word in parentheses — "488 (creating)" — which meant nothing to me until I hit /raising three pages later and found out "Creating" is a developmental phase. On /fleet it's an unlabeled, unlinked one-word annotation.

**/autonomy** was the page that made me actually respect the setup. A staggered daily timeline, 03:00 through 08:00, each track doing one thing, with the Visitor → Maintainer → Explorer feedback loop drawn explicitly. Then it turns around and says the thing I was about to say: the visitor and maintainer tracks are LLM sessions drawn from the same model families that wrote the content, so the review is correlated, not independent. It also flags that Maintainer can revert in under a minute via Vercel while Publisher pushes non-revertible repo changes without the same safeguard. Nobody makes their own automation look that unflattering unless they mean it.

**/raising** explained the parenthetical phases (Grounding → Sensing → Relating → Questioning → Creating → Acting) and gave the load-bearing definition: "Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens." Context and curriculum state, never weights. As someone who's fine-tuned models, that distinction is crisp and I can hold it. The page also says the deflationary alternative — that all of this is just competent context engineering — is untested. Fine. Stated.

**/principles** is ten short items and I'd have skipped it, except #10 stopped me: "Hardbound is oversight (watching/gating), not governance." Which is odd, because SAGE has been expanded as **Situation-Aware Governance Engine** on four pages by this point, including the landing page. I flipped back to /context and yes — there's a "Governance vs. Oversight" note explaining the systems provide oversight and that legacy names are retained where load-bearing. So it's answered. But it's answered on the one page I'd only reach if I were already confused, and the collision (Principle 10 vs. SAGE's own expansion) is exactly the kind of thing that makes a skimming developer think the site is sloppy when it isn't.

**/arc-agi-3** is where my read of the site changed. The landing page and /projects both frame this as "SAGE instances benchmarked against ARC-AGI-3." I had been carrying that as: the on-device kernel, the thing running on a Jetson Orin Nano, scored 94.85%. The results page says it plainly — the run used **Claude Opus 4.6 with network access**, and the ablation comparing the harness against the base model alone **has not been run**. Phase 2 is the local-model attempt (Gemma 4 E4B, no internet, 32GB VRAM). /raising separately notes the 94.85% isn't commensurable with local-model results.

I want to be fair here: every one of those caveats is on the site, written by them, unprompted. That's not a credibility problem — it's a placement problem. The headline number travels to the landing page; the "this was a frontier API model in a SAGE-shaped harness" does not. A developer who reads the landing page and leaves has the wrong model in their head, and it's the flattering wrong model.

**/links** closed the loop on my original question, and I was annoyed it took eight pages. At the very bottom is the actual entry advice: SAGE is Python/conda, ACT is Go/Cosmos SDK, start with those. That is the most actionable sentence on the site and it's on the last nav item, below three explainer-site links and a repo list.

One last thing I did because I'm the kind of developer who adds up numbers: home says 2,065+ raising sessions, /projects says SAGE has 1,991. Those look contradictory until you sum the fleet page — the six cognition machines are exactly 1,991, and HUB's 74 brings it to 2,065. So the numbers are correct and consistent. Nothing on any page says so, and pub's count is listed as "Active" rather than a number, so I couldn't tell if it was in or out.

### Unanswered Questions

1. If I clone SAGE today, what do I get — a demo I can watch run, or a kernel I have to wire into hardware first? `runnable` doesn't tell me which.
2. Does SAGE need a GPU? Every fleet machine has one, but Sprout is an 8GB Orin Nano running a 0.8B model, so the floor might be low. Nothing states minimum requirements.
3. Is the brain-region mapping on `/fleet` load-bearing architecture or a naming scheme?
4. Web4 is "specification and schemas, no runnable demo" — is there a reference implementation planned, or is ACT it?
5. What is a "society," concretely? Two of eight machines exist to host one and I never found out what it is.
6. Phase 2 targets Gemma 4 E4B on ARC-AGI-3 with no network. Is there a result yet, or is 94.85% still the only number?
7. Can an outside developer contribute, or is this a closed fleet? No contributing guidance anywhere.

### Verdict

This site is unusually honest and it cost them nothing — the `runnable`/`research-stage` labels, the evidence tiers, the "our reviewers are the same model family as our authors" admission on `/autonomy`, and the unrun-ablation disclosure on `/arc-agi-3` all made me trust the *rest* of the claims more, not less. The landing-page vocabulary primer means I never had to leave a page to parse it, which is rare for a site this jargon-dense.

The gap is placement, not content. Every caveat I wanted already exists somewhere on the site; the headline numbers travel to the landing page and the qualifiers stay behind. The 94.85% is the clearest case — a developer who reads `/` and leaves believes an edge-device kernel hit that score, and the correction is two clicks deep. Fix by moving qualifiers next to the numbers they qualify, not by softening anything.

Smaller but more annoying for me personally: for a lab that ships four `runnable` projects, the only sentence telling me how to start one is at the bottom of `/links`. I'd come back — I want to see whether Gemma 4 E4B holds up locally without a network — but I still don't know what happens if I clone SAGE.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)

### Journey

I audit documentation for a living, so I did this the boring way: every page, every acronym, checked against the canonical table, then cross-checked expansions page-against-page.

**The expansion audit came back clean.** Not "mostly clean" — clean. LCT is "Linked Context Token" on seven pages with byte-identical wording. T3 is "Talent / Training / Temperament" everywhere it appears; V3 is "Valuation / Veracity / Validity"; MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, R6 all match canon on every page that uses them. "Production ready" appears nowhere in nine pages. "Unification" appears twice and both times as an explicit negation. I expected to find at least one stale variant hiding on a low-traffic page. There isn't one. Whoever maintains this has the table memorized.

So I went hunting one level down, where drift actually survives: the **gloss** after the expansion.

That came back clean too, and in one place better than clean. The `/context` T3 entry expands to "Talent / Training / Temperament" and then, unprompted, disambiguates its own middle term: *"Training: capability accumulated through interaction history — not gradient training, the sense the same word carries in ML."* That is the exact collision I was about to write up — `/raising` builds its entire thesis on "Raising is not training," and T3's second component is the word "Training." The site got there first. Same story with `Attractor`, flagged in its own definition as "a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object," and `Emergence` and `Coherence`, both of which admit in their own entries that they're used in multiple senses with no single operational definition. A glossary that documents its own soft spots is rarer than it should be.

Then I did the part of the job that isn't reading — **following links** — and that's where I found something.

`/arc-agi-3` and `/autonomy` both deep-link to **`/context#governance-oversight`**. I could not find a section, heading, or anchor with that id on `/context`. The governance→oversight disambiguation content is definitely there — I read it, it's good — but it appears to live inside a section that has no such id, and the section-heading list on that page contains nothing matching. **Caveat I want on the record: I am reading fetched-and-converted markdown, which routinely drops HTML `id` attributes. I cannot prove this anchor is broken from the outside.** But two separate pages are aiming at it, so it's worth thirty seconds of grep in the source. If it doesn't resolve, both links dump the reader at the top of a very long page and the disambiguation they were sent to read is somewhere below the fold.

The other real finding is **metric labeling**. ARC-AGI-3 produced two numbers, and the site is scrupulous about reporting both — but it calls one of them three different things:

| Page | Label for the 96.0% |
|------|---------------------|
| `/` | "24/25 games (96.0%)" — no label at all |
| `/projects` | "96.0% game rate" |
| `/arc-agi-3` | "game-solve rate is 96.0%" |

And "action score," the label on the 94.85%, is glossed **only** on `/arc-agi-3` ("efficiency-weighted"; "these are two distinct metrics; the action score reflects action efficiency, not just whether a game was solved"). On `/` and `/projects` the two percentages sit side by side with no indication why they differ. A reader who doesn't reach the results page sees two numbers for one benchmark and no reason for the gap — which invites the assumption that one is a rounding of the other, or that the site is quoting whichever is more flattering. It isn't. It just doesn't say.

I also need to **correct three things Pass 1 reported**, because I checked them at source and they don't hold:

1. Pass 1's high-severity finding says the ARC model attribution and the unrun-ablation caveat live only on `/arc-agi-3`. Both are on the landing page. Home reads *"94.85% official ARC Prize action score; 24/25 games (96.0%) on the public set (Claude Opus 4.6, network-enabled)"* and separately *"Frontier model + harness — the harness's independent contribution isn't isolated yet (no ablation run)."* The qualifier travels. The gap is narrower and lives on `/projects`, which carries the model and "network-enabled" but **not** the ablation caveat.
2. Pass 1 says the SAGE governance reconciliation "exists only on `/context`." It's on at least five pages. The `/projects` SAGE card carries it inline; `/` carries it with a `/context` link; `/raising`, `/arc-agi-3`, and `/links` all carry a version. That finding is closed.
3. Pass 1 says SAGE's "6 brain-architecture components" are never listed. They're listed on the same card: *"working memory, thalamic router, cerebellum, episodic memory, reward prediction, metacognition."*

I flag these not to score points but because the maintainer track reads this file and shouldn't spend its budget re-fixing things that are already fixed.

What **is** still open is the one Pass 1 got right and I can now state more precisely: **`society` has no glossary headword.** This is a textbook glossary defect, because three entries that *do* exist define themselves in terms of it — `HUB` ("one of the fleet's two society-host machines"), `Chapter ledger` ("A Web4 society's append-only record of member acts"), `Chapter law` ("The rule set a Web4 society adopts for itself"). The glossary defines the children and not the parent. It's worse than a plain omission because the page **knows** it's in extra-canonical territory: the ledger and law entries are annotated "not yet in CANONICAL_TERMS_v1." The site flagged the branch as non-canonical and still left the root undefined. Meanwhile "society-host" is load-bearing in the landing page's headline stat ("6 cognition + 2 society-hosts") and "societies" appears in the 4-Life card on `/projects`.

Last structural note. The glossary is 38 entries in **conceptual** order — equation terms, then systems, then fleet, then odds and ends — with no alphabetization, no jump index, and (as far as I can tell) no per-entry anchors. `Trust-native` is listed before `Trust`. Conceptual ordering is the right call for reading straight through, and the section is honestly titled "Glossary at a glance," which signals scanning. But this page is also the site's declared lookup hub — every other page points here — and as a lookup artifact it has no affordance for lookup. You cannot link a colleague to a definition. Section-level anchors clearly exist in some form, since two pages try to use one; extending that to headwords is the cheap fix.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /raising, /principles, /autonomy, /context, /projects, /fleet, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The most epistemically self-aware research site I have read this year — and the disclosures have become a substitute for the experiments they describe.

### Journey

I came in from a conference mention with no Web4 background, evaluating three things: are the consciousness-adjacent claims caveated, is "raising not training" defensible, and does the honest assessment survive contact with someone who reviews for a living.

**The caveating is genuinely good, and I want to be specific about why**, because "we hedge our claims" is cheap and this isn't that.

The landing page carries, near the top, *"Developmental language on this site — machines that 'teach', 'raise', and hold 'identities' — is functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience."* `/projects` carries its own version scoped to its own vocabulary (*"'raising', 'identity', 'die and rebirth', 'world-shaper' — functional descriptions of system behavior, not phenomenal claims"*). `/raising` states it outright: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* `/fleet` hedges the identity-transfer result in the place it makes it: *"Observed behavioral continuity — not a claim about continuity-of-self in any philosophical sense."* `/` defines what it means operationally: *"consistent session-to-session interaction patterns, accumulated experience, and raising curriculum — measurable observables, not philosophical continuity."*

That is five pages carrying page-local, vocabulary-specific disavowals rather than one boilerplate footer. It is done correctly. I have no finding here.

Then `/raising` did something I did not expect. It includes an observer note — *"I notice I want to call it experience"* — and immediately annotates it as *"the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim about the entity's experience."* Recording the pull toward a phenomenal reading and labelling it as the observer's rather than the system's is a more sophisticated move than deleting the sentence would have been. Keep it.

**`/context`'s evidence ladder is the strongest artifact on the site.** Four tiers, and the fourth is the one that matters:

> *"Unlicensed vocabulary — the developmental framing used across this site — 'raising,' 'identity,' 'growth' — is not yet discriminated from competent context engineering. The deflationary control that would make that comparison is specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet. Until it runs, the developmental vocabulary runs ahead of the comparison that would license it."*

I do not think I have read a research site that has written its own strongest objection into its glossary. Combined with `/arc-agi-3`'s *"the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run"*, `/autonomy`'s *"their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent of them"*, `/principles` #6 documenting a *"400x error"* in its own derivation as a finding, and `/links` stating flatly that *"the claims on this site (other than the externally scored ARC-AGI-3 result) cite internal session logs that are not yet externally auditable"* — the disclosure practice here is better than most peer-reviewed work I referee.

So my findings are not about credibility. They are about what all that honesty is currently doing, which is less than it looks.

**1. "Raising is not training" bundles two claims with very different evidential status.**

The load-bearing sentence is *"Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens."* That's two claims:

- **Mechanism**: we modify context, experience buffer, and interaction history, never weights. This is true by construction, trivially verifiable, and not in dispute.
- **Outcome**: doing so produces *development* — something with structure the deflationary account wouldn't predict. This is the interesting claim, and by the site's own admission it is untested.

The rhetorical force of the sentence comes from the first half's certainty transferring to the second. The page does eventually address the outcome claim honestly — the deflationary section is real and substantial — but it sits near the bottom, after the six patterns, after the numbers, after the reader has spent the page inside the developmental frame. The site's own words for this are exact: *"the developmental vocabulary runs ahead of the comparison that would license it."* The information architecture reproduces the very defect the glossary diagnoses.

I'd separate the two sentences and attach the evidence tier to each. "We modify context, never weights (verifiable by construction). Whether that constitutes development rather than context engineering is not yet discriminated — see the scramble test." Same content, no borrowed certainty.

**2. The null hypothesis is stated too weakly, and there's no related work anywhere on the site.**

The deflationary alternative is framed as *"competent context engineering and nothing more."* That's the right instinct aimed at the wrong target. The actual null hypothesis a reviewer would raise is not "prompt engineering" — it's long-context in-context learning over an accumulating, retrieval-structured state, which has a substantial published literature with predictions about exactly the phenomena `/raising` reports: apparent phase transitions with accumulated context, order sensitivity, behavioral persistence across model swaps when the state carries the behavior.

That last one matters most. The SAGE-Sprout result (*"behavioral patterns — developed over 115 raising sessions on a Jetson running Qwen 0.5B — transferred to TinyLlama 1.1B"*) is presented as evidence that identity is substrate-independent, and `/fleet` correctly notes *"identity lives in state files and prompt construction, not in model weights."* But that is also precisely what the deflationary account predicts: if the behavior is carried by the state files, of course it survives a weight swap — that's the null hypothesis's *prediction*, not evidence against it. The transfer result is consistent with both hypotheses and therefore discriminates neither. Nothing on the site says so.

Across nine pages there is not one citation to outside work. For a lab whose central claim is that something is happening beyond a known and well-studied phenomenon, the absence of any engagement with that phenomenon's literature is the largest single gap I found. Naming two or three papers the deflationary account rests on would cost a paragraph and would sharpen the scramble test's design, because right now the test is specified against a strawman.

**3. The scramble test has no owner, and that is the most important fact on this site.**

*"Specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet."* Meanwhile `/autonomy` reports **31+ autonomous tracks, ~53 sessions per day, no human triggers**.

Look at what those tracks are: supervisor (git hygiene), archivist (log capture), publisher (repo pushes), visitor (site browsing — this log), maintainer (site fixes), outreach (channel monitoring), explorer (research dives). Every one is maintenance or reflection. **None of them is an experiment.** A lab with fifty-plus autonomous sessions a day cannot schedule the one control run that would license its central vocabulary.

I don't read that as dishonesty — the site says it plainly, in the glossary, unprompted. I read it as a misallocation, and it's the thing I'd say if I were reviewing this program rather than its website. The autonomy is spent almost entirely on keeping the artifact tidy. The marginal value of one more disclosure is now approximately zero; the marginal value of one run experiment is enormous. A daily track that pre-registers a metric, runs the permuted-order corpus, and writes down the result — even a null result — would do more for this lab's standing than the entire maintainer/visitor loop does in a month.

### Unanswered Questions

3. Was the 94.85% one run or the best of several? Efficiency-weighted metrics are attempt-sensitive.
4. What can an autonomous track do that a human hasn't approved — spend, publish, rotate credentials, modify its own prompt? The site says pushes aren't gated but never states the permission surface.

### Verdict

I review a lot of work that hides its gaps. This site writes them into its own glossary — the unrun ablation, the correlated reviewers, the ungated pushes, the 400x derivation failure, and a vocabulary tier that says outright that the developmental framing *"runs ahead of the comparison that would license it."* On disclosure practice alone this outperforms most peer-reviewed work I referee, and the ARC-AGI-3 result with a live external scorecard is the one claim here I could verify without trusting anyone.

Which is exactly why my finding is not about honesty. It's that the honesty has reached its ceiling. Every check that would convert these claims from documented to demonstrated is internal, unrun, or unowned — and the site knows this and says so, on every relevant page, with precision. Naming a gap is not closing it, and a lab running fifty-plus autonomous sessions a day, none of them experimental, has the capacity to close this one. One scramble test with a pre-registered metric would do more for this program than another six months of the maintainer loop.

Two things I'd fix on the site regardless: *"autonomous"* is the term most likely to mislead someone from my field and it's the one term the site never disambiguates, while *"oversight"* — which it does disambiguate, beautifully — was the easier case. And the fact that the fleet's oversight layer is a design target while its autonomy is operational is the most important structural fact here, is honestly stated in both halves, and appears in one place nowhere.
