## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A real, running, honestly-caveated research system buried under serious jargon density — I get *what* it is in 2 minutes, but *how to think about it* took all 9 pages.

### Journey

Landed on home from a GitHub README. Headline "Eight machines, one recursive learning loop" plus "trust-native ontology" — the second phrase meant nothing to me, but the problem statement did: AI agents already act autonomously and there's no general way to verify they'll behave. That's a real problem I recognize. The status section ("small local models solving unfamiliar puzzle levels", "identity persistence across hardware") made me curious rather than skeptical, mostly because the page itself says the only externally-verifiable claim is the ARC Prize scorecard. That kind of self-flagging is rare and it bought a lot of goodwill.

Went to /projects next, scanning for something to clone. Eight projects plus tooling; the page directly answers my question: "Want to run something rather than read? Start with SAGE." SAGE's one-liner — "The context window is the model's entire world; SAGE's job is to curate it" — is the best sentence on the site for a developer. I'd clone SAGE.

/fleet was unexpectedly fun: 8 named machines with model, role ("thalamic router", "habit compiler"), and session counts. The claim that "model family matters as much as size" (Gemma 3 4B beating Phi-4 14B for some work) is the kind of concrete engineering observation I'd screenshot. One hiccup: home and /fleet say eight machines; /autonomy and /arc-agi-3 say "6 cognition machines" / "6-machine fleet". I eventually worked out that 6 cognition + 2 society hosts = 8, but I did the arithmetic myself — the site didn't do it for me.

/autonomy: 31+ daily scheduled jobs, detect-and-revert safety posture, and an admission that "the only automated detector is the same daily loop being evaluated." Clear-eyed. The clarification that "autonomous" means unattended-not-self-directed is exactly what I needed, but it arrives late on the page.

/raising was the hardest page. "Raising is not training" is a clean hook, and the six BECOMING phases are readable, but the page leans on T3/V3/MRH/LCT and even a Hill-function enzyme analogy. This is where I most felt "internal docs leaked to outsiders." The honest caveat ("everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more") again saved it — but it's near the bottom.

/principles: readable list of 10. Principle 5 (synthon) openly admits its definition is circular, which is honest but means I can't do anything with it. Principle 6 (document failures, "p_crit derivation failed 400x, that's data") is the one I'd actually adopt at work.

/context is the payoff page — every acronym I'd been carrying finally resolved. If I'd read it second instead of seventh, the whole site would have been ~40% less confusing.

/links: thin but functional; SAGE quick-start (Rust, Ollama, Python 3.10+) is genuinely actionable. /arc-agi-3: 94.85% with a link to the official scorecard — the one claim I can check myself, and they say so. The unrun ablation (same model, no harness) is flagged by the page itself, which preempted my main objection.

**/context** is the page I'd grade hardest and it graded out clean. LCT: non-transferable, bound to a single entity, identity in witnessed record not weights — canonical. T3's "Training" glossed as "capability accumulated through interaction history — not gradient training" — this is the single most-misread term in the ecosystem and the gloss is correct. The notation key even admits "/" means two different things on the equation's one line. And the page names its source of truth: CANONICAL_TERMS_v1.md in the public web4 repo, "when this page and that document disagree, the document governs." That sentence is the correct dependency direction.

**But** — and this is my main finding — the site is more specific than canon in several places, so I did what a contributor would do: pulled `CANONICAL_TERMS_v1.md` from the public web4 repo and diffed the suspect specifics directly. Results: T3's "each component is an RDF sub-graph root" **is in canon verbatim** ("Each dimension is a root node in an open-ended RDF sub-graph, not a scalar") — the site's citation is accurate, no issue. Three others are **not** in canon: (a) the notation key's two senses of "/" — canon has no notation key at all; (b) /projects' "anchored to LCT" relation — canon renders LCT as a plain `+` term; (c) /context's LCT "Non-transferable: permanently bound to a single entity" — canon's LCT entry says only "a verifiable digital presence that accumulates witnessed history and reputation over time," with no non-transferability language (that doctrine lives in the broader Web4 docs, not the declared source of record). None of these contradict canon — but a site that states "when this page and that document disagree, the document governs" is out-specifying the document it defers to. That's not drift; it's the site running ahead of its own source of truth. The fix direction matters: these should be proposed upstream into canon, not deleted from the site.

Remaining pages: /autonomy uses ATP/ADP correctly for track budgets and pins "oversight" to the machine-enforced sense up front. /raising's T3 gloss matches /context verbatim, "raising is not training" is cleanly distinguished from gradient training, and the coherence caveat correctly points at the glossary's admission that no operational definition exists. /principles states both flagship corrections as principles ("Web4 is not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure' — it's an ontology"; "Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight"). /links expands every acronym inline, and the SAGE blurb carries the Governance-name disclaimer even there. /arc-agi-3 describes the 12-step loop accurately, places PolicyGate correctly as a Hardbound sub-gate between filter and act (not a 13th step), and flags its own unrun harness-vs-model ablation.

**/raising** is where I expected to find the overreach and mostly didn't. The core distinction is mechanistically stated, not vibes: "In raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights. Behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns, not in parameter changes." That is a defensible description of context engineering, and the page immediately concedes the deflationary reading: "everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more" — at the *top* of the page in my fetch. "Identity" and "growth" are operationalized ("consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns," "increasing response diversity... measurable observables, not phenomenal claims") before they're used. Best of all, the page specifies its own deciding experiment: a scramble control (reorder session history, preserving content) that would separate raising from context engineering, with a stated retirement commitment — "if this control runs and the result is a bare main effect, or no degradation at all... the developmental vocabulary gets retired from it."

Then the floor gives way, and the page is honest about that too: the control is "specified... but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet," and the blocker is that the outcome variable, "coherence," has (per the glossary's own admission) "no single operational definition yet." So the falsifiability is *nominal*: a pre-commitment bound to an undefined quantity is a promissory note, not a protocol. This is the single most important methodological fact on the site, and to the site's credit I learned it *from* the site.

**/principles** survived my honesty check. Principle 5 (synthon) openly states its circularity — "'Synthon' is defined through coherence, and coherence is in turn partly defined through the synthon marker — the two terms lean on each other" — and concludes it "has not earned the status of the principles around it, and it should be read at that weight." Principle 6 documents a 400x derivation failure without spin. The deployment disclaimer is unambiguous ("Nothing on this site is intended for or suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings"). This reads as intellectual caution, not performative humility — the tell is that the admissions are *specific* (which number is ungrounded, which definition is circular), where performative humility is generic.

**/autonomy** contains the sentence I'd quote in a review: the auditing tracks "are LLM sessions drawn from the same model families as the tracks that author the content they audit — their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent." That is the reviewer-independence problem stated more crisply than most safety papers manage. Also named: the self-evaluation loop ("a zero is equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'"), the unbounded exposure window for plausible-but-misleading content, and publisher irreversibility ("That gap is unaddressed, not solved"). The "autonomous" definition — "unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense of choosing its own goals" — is in the opening section per my fetch and is exactly the disambiguation my field needs. My criticism: the page is all diagnosis, no remediation path. Transparency about a limitation is not mitigation of it, and the page's completeness of self-diagnosis could lull a reader into scoring it as safety work. It's a risk register, and a good one — but nothing on the page commits to retiring any of the named risks.

**/context** handles field collisions better than any glossary I've seen on a lab site: "Training" in T3 is explicitly "not gradient training, the sense the same word carries in ML"; PolicyGate is flagged as sitting "exactly where an RL reader expects to find a policy network, and it is the opposite kind of object — it vetoes actions, it does not choose them"; "attractor" is labeled "a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object." One genuine problem: the glossary permits a cross-sense numeric claim ("1% coupling → 35% coherence gain") whose two coherence senses are, by the page's own admission, incommensurate. A number computed across two undefined senses of the same word is not a measurement.

**/arc-agi-3** is the credibility anchor: an externally-scored 94.85% with a link to the official ARC Prize scorecard, and the missing harness-vs-model ablation admitted twice, with "the loop is the capability" explicitly downgraded to "a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated result." The residual risk is over-attribution — a skimming reader credits SAGE for what may be mostly Claude Opus 4.6 — but the page hands you the tools to catch that.

**/projects, /fleet, /links**: Synchronism is framed as "research conjecture, single-trial and not independently replicated," with refuted predictions acknowledged — refutation-listing is a strong honesty signal. /fleet flags its brain-region labels as "role mnemonics... not measured functional homologies." Two things bothered me on /fleet: "three independent security reviews" of the hub is asserted with no methodology, reviewer identity, or artifact — the phrase "independent review" is doing evidentiary work the page doesn't back; and the raising-vs-engineering evidence-status note is (per my fetch) referenced in footnotes rather than adjacent to the session-count claims. /links confirmed the artifact drought: "no papers or preprints are published yet," no log samples, no coding criteria — the contact email and 21 public repos are the only routes to independent verification besides the ARC scorecard.

### Verdict
This site passes the honesty test in the way that matters: its self-criticisms are specific, load-bearing, and occasionally more damning than what I'd have written myself — the reviewer-correlation admission and the synthon circularity note are not the moves of a lab managing impressions. But it is now over-indexed on candor as a substitute for evidence: the caveat density has hit diminishing returns, and the only things that would move my credence are artifacts — a published log sample with coding criteria, an owned and scheduled scramble control, one decorrelated review. "Raising is not training" is defensible as stated; whether it's *distinguishable* from context engineering is, by the site's own admission, an open empirical question with no one assigned to close it.
