## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /arc-agi-3, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: A genuinely interesting multi-machine agent lab with unusually honest caveats, but the landing and projects pages front-load acronyms that only pay off after a detour to /context.

### Journey
Landed on the homepage from a repo README. The headline — "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — is a good hook; I immediately understand this is a distributed autonomous-agent setup. The tagline calls Web4 "a trust-native ontology — a shared vocabulary and trust model for AI agents," which is enough of a gloss to keep me reading. Then the acronym wall hits: LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC all appear on the landing page with a glossary *link* rather than inline definitions. I filed them under "figure out later" and clicked Projects.

Projects is where I'd decide whether to clone anything, and the runnable/not-runnable labeling is genuinely useful — Web4 is honestly flagged as "specification — documents and schemas, no runnable demo," SAGE and Membot and SNARC/engram are marked runnable with repo links. The ARC-AGI-3 card (24/25 games, 96.0%) is the most concrete thing on the site and it's what got me to click deeper. The Web4 equation (`MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`) is displayed with essentially zero inline expansion — eight acronyms in one line. As a developer I read equations fluently, but an equation of undefined symbols is just typography.

/context rescued me. The glossary is clear, one line per term, and after reading it the rest of the site snaps into focus. LCT as "identity grounded in record, not model weights" is a genuinely good one-liner. I'd argue /context should be the second link in the nav, or the landing page should say "start here" louder.

/arc-agi-3 was the credibility test and it mostly passed: real scorecard link, MIT-0 code, cost disclosure (~$250), and — notably — an explicit admission that "the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's... has not been run." That's the kind of sentence that makes me trust the rest of the site. Momentary confusion: the landing page leads with 94.85% and the projects card with 96.0%; the ARC page explains these are two different metrics (action score vs. game solve rate), but until you get there the numbers look inconsistent.

/fleet reads like a fun hardware tour (Jetsons, a 4090 laptop, a Mac Mini, a TinyLlama on an RTX 2060 orchestrating official runs — great detail). The "brain component" role labels are flagged as functional analogies, which I appreciated. /autonomy answered my main implementation question directly: tracks are "a cron entry with a declared scope," coordinated via a SQLite track registry. That demystification is exactly what a developer wants — it's cron jobs and prompts, stated plainly, with an honest note that safety is "detect-and-revert" at the process level, "not cryptographic guarantees."

/raising and /principles are above my ceiling in places (Hill function kinetics, coupling density) but the consistent pattern of stating the deflationary alternative — "raising might just be sophisticated prompt engineering, we haven't run the scramble control" — kept it from reading as mysticism. /links ends with a clear next step: read /context, then clone SAGE or ACT.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Terminology discipline is unusually strong — canonical expansions are consistent across all nine pages — but the site gives two different definitions of the `+` operator in its own equation, and the landing page defines its vocabulary *after* using it.

### Journey
I worked through the nav in order, keeping a running table of every acronym expansion. The nav itself is clean: eight links plus home, all resolving, no dead ends, and the page set matches what the nav promises.

The landing page opens with the acronym-dense pitch (LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC) and then — at the bottom — has a "Vocabulary primer" section that defines all of them, correctly. So Pass 1's "undefined on page" impression is half right: the definitions exist, but they arrive after first use. Standard doc practice is define-on-first-use or a visible "vocabulary below" pointer at the top; a skimmer who bounces off the acronyms in the hero will never learn the primer is three screens down.

/context is the anchor document and it earns the role. Roughly 30 glossary entries, all matching the canonical table, plus several entries I'd hold up as models of disclosure: "coherence" is explicitly flagged as having three distinct senses on the site, HUB is explicitly declared a proper name rather than an acronym, and the "governance" in SAGE's name is marked as load-bearing legacy predating the governance→oversight correction. That correction note is repeated wherever "governance" surfaces (/raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3) — consistent handling of a known legacy term, which is exactly how you retire vocabulary without rewriting history.

The one real inconsistency I found is in the Web4 equation's operator legend. /context states `+` = "augmented with", plainly. /projects states `+` = "a generic conjunction (not uniformly 'augmented with' — see the Web4 card below for where the prose is more precise than the equation)". These are two different definitions of the same operator on the site's two most authoritative equation renderings. The /projects hedge may even be the more careful claim — but a glossary that says one thing and a legend that says "not uniformly that" is the kind of contradiction a reader notices and loses confidence over. Pick one framing and use it in both places (if the honest answer is "the `+` is looser than the other operators," say that in the /context glossary too).

Beyond that, my pass was mostly a parade of CORRECT rows. Every expansion of LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, SNARC, MCP, RDF I found matches canon. Web4 is called an ontology everywhere — /context even explicitly negates "architecture or infrastructure." Hardbound is "oversight" everywhere, never "governance." "Production ready" appears nowhere. "Unification" appears only inside its own negation ("reuse, not unification"). Metric labeling is better than Pass 1 suggested: the landing page does label 94.85% as "official ARC Prize action score" and 24/25 as a solve rate, and /arc-agi-3 keeps the two metrics cleanly distinguished.

Small gaps: FIDO2 appears on /projects without expansion (WebAuthn and CTAP2 are named, FIDO2 itself is not unpacked), MCP is used on /fleet without its expansion, and MCP/RDF appear unexpanded on /links — all terms defined elsewhere on the site, so these are per-page first-use misses, not drift.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /raising, /principles, /autonomy, /context, /arc-agi-3, /projects, /fleet, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The most reflexively honest self-published lab site I've evaluated — the deflationary alternatives are stated by the authors themselves — but the core explanatory concepts (coherence, synthon) lack operational definitions, and all developmental evidence is internal logs with no published coding criteria.

### Journey
I came in from a conference mention with one question: does this lab's language outrun its evidence? I started at the landing page, then went straight to the pages where consciousness-adjacent claims would live — /raising, /principles, /autonomy — before sweeping the rest.

The landing page sets the pattern I'd see everywhere: strong claims first, real caveats second. "Maintained behavioral identity across 180+ sessions" and "synthons — emergent coherence entities that are more than the sum of their parts" arrive in the hero flow; the disclaimer — "functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience" — arrives later and lower. The caveats are genuine and specific (the synthon coupling result is flagged "Preliminary (1 trial, not replicated)" right where the number appears), but the sequencing means a skimming reader absorbs the dramatic version.

/raising is where I expected to find mysticism and instead found the single most disarming sentence on the site: "Everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more." The page names its own deflationary alternatives one by one — bilateral generation "could be continuation sampling past the response boundary," identity portability "could be the mechanical consequence of carrying the same context files" — and then specifies the missing control with unusual precision: "The control has to be a scramble, not a generic replacement," because a scramble destroys order and cross-session attribution without losing task-relevance. That's a correctly designed falsification test, described before being run, with the admission "the comparison has not been run... treat the framework as a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding." The consciousness disclaimer is explicit ("We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia"), and there's even an observer-bias note recording the researcher's own interpretive pull ("I notice I want to call it experience") as data about the observer, not the system. This is the standard I wish more agent-behavior work met. The remaining weakness is evidential, not rhetorical: every behavioral pattern rests on "internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet."

/principles continues the honesty (a 400x-error failed derivation is documented as a principle exhibit, which almost no lab would publish) but exposes the definitional soft spot. /autonomy earned respect on a different axis: safety claims. A publisher track ships public changes with no human gate, and the page says so rather than burying it. As a safety researcher I note the structural risk stands regardless of the candor: consequential public-facing changes deploy before any human reviews them, and human audit has "no committed cadence." Disclosure is not mitigation. But I've read many safety pages that claim more and disclose less.

/arc-agi-3 is the strongest page evidentially: the missing ablation is stated up front and repeated, "the loop is the capability" is explicitly labeled "a working hypothesis, not a demonstrated result," results link to an official external scorecard, code is MIT-0, and cost is disclosed. /context's glossary tiers evidence explicitly (externally validated / internal observations / unreplicated), which is a practice I'd cite approvingly. /links confirmed the evidential picture: one external validation source (the ARC scorecard), no peer-reviewed publications, one contact email.

### Unanswered Questions

### Verdict
This site does something rare: it argues against itself in public — the scramble control it hasn't run, the null hypothesis that everything is "competent context engineering and nothing more," the 400x-failed derivation, the unbounded exposure window. The consciousness-adjacent claims are properly caveated, and "raising not training" is defensible precisely because the site presents it as working vocabulary rather than established finding. What separates this from publishable research is not honesty but operationalization and external evidence: core terms (coherence, synthon) lack testable definitions, and every developmental claim rests on unaudited internal logs. I'd cite the ARC-AGI-3 page today; I'd cite the raising framework only after the scramble control runs.
