## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (Home, Projects, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Context/Glossary, Links, ARC-AGI-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial → good
- **Would return?**: yes (for SAGE's Quick start and the Hestia repo)
- **Overall impression**: The pitch holds up and the install path is concrete and honest. What slows me down most is the site repeatedly explaining its own vocabulary history, which means nothing to someone arriving from a README.

### Journey

**Home.** I came in from a GitHub README. The browser tab says "Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop", but the hero is about something else: "AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become first-class." After a slow read I could say what is being built in one line: a trust vocabulary (Web4), a local daemon that puts agents from different vendors under one signed law (Hestia), a Rust "society" runtime (Hub) and agent research (SAGE). That's a clear enough value proposition. The thesis sentence ("trust should be computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying") is the best thing on the page.

Things that cost me time on Home:
- The SAGE expansion comes with a note: "a name that predates the lab's governance→oversight terminology". I don't know what that correction is or why it matters.
- The Hestia card spends about a third of its length on vocabulary: "'Governance' is the right word here and is deliberate… The lab's governance→oversight correction binds Hardbound…". Then an aside explains that A1 on Hestia's ladder is "Unrelated to — and inverted against — the Orange Book's TCSEC… class A1". That's accurate, but it's a digression inside a pitch card.
- "Hardbound, built by Metalinxx": nothing says who Metalinxx is or how it relates to the lab. /links gives a `metalinxx.io` contact address, so there's clearly a relationship. It isn't explained anywhere I saw.
- The "Start here" cards work. Calibration goes to Web4's STATUS.md on GitHub, which is an unexpected jump off-site but useful. Vocabulary goes to /context and Full ecosystem to /projects. The `/context#instance-line` anchor resolves.

**Projects.** This is the most useful page for me. The status legend is good: runnable vs research-stage is kept separate from public vs private, and "the two labels do not imply each other" is exactly the clarification I needed. The page admits it is "the most acronym-dense page on the site" and points to the glossary, and the cards expand terms on first use. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` shows with a glyph legend, which openly says it is "the site's own gloss, not canon's". I appreciated the honesty, though I have no way to judge it.

The Quick start at the bottom is the high point for a developer. It has real commands: `ollama pull gemma3:4b`, `python3 -m sage.instances.init …`, `cargo build --release`, run the daemon, dashboard on :8760. It says what success looks like ("An idle daemon in WAKE with an empty buffer is the correct first result, not a failure"), and it says honestly that ACT is "STALLED… Clone it to read, not to run". I'd try this. One gap: step 0 lists "Rust toolchain, Ollama, Python 3.10+" but has no Python dependency install (no venv or `pip install`) before `python3 -m sage.instances.init`. I can't tell whether none is needed or it was left out. See Unanswered Questions.

Card-level detail is dense but readable. "Hestia governs the machine. Hub is the society that checks the record Hestia makes. SAGE provides the cognition. Hardbound provides the oversight." That's a good summary line.

**Fleet.** It opens with the machine counts I'd been looking for: "Six cognition machines, plus two society-hosts (HUB and pub…)". The per-machine cards (hardware, model, session count, dated state such as running, quiet, paused or "instrumentation broken") are concrete and I liked them. The brain-analogy labels are explicitly called "role mnemonics… not measured functional homologies", and they double as each machine's work assignment. That helped. The page is about 5,400 words, which is long for a developer skim. The "model family matters as much as size" claim is labeled "One internal observation, not yet a finding", which is the right call.

Here I first hit **HUB** (the machine) versus **Hub** (the software). The glossary tells them apart *by capitalization alone*, and **hestia** (the trust store) versus **Hestia** (the daemon) works the same way. Those are two case-only distinctions, and they break the moment someone types them in a search box, a commit message or speech.

**Autonomy.** The headline is "31+ autonomous tracks across 6 cognition machines, ~53 sessions per day (figures as of 2026-05-26, not recounted since)". The caveat is honest, but the page's lead number is almost four months old. The timeline table (Supervisors 03:00 → Explorer 08:00 → dream consolidation) is easy to follow. "Autonomy" is defined as "unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense", which disarmed my first worry. The operational-boundaries section says outright that pushes are "not human-gated before they ship". Once I understood it, I was glad it was there.

**Raising.** This is outside my comfort zone. The core definition is clear: "Training optimizes a loss function and updates weights; raising does neither." The six BECOMING patterns read like a developmental story, but the page undercuts itself in the right way: "Everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more — the control that would discriminate raising from that alternative has not been run yet." As a developer I'd file this under "interesting, not what I came for". I have no complaints about how the claims are framed.

**Principles.** The termite-mound metaphor for "Substrate, not structure" works. Principle 5 (Synthon) is openly flagged as circular ("'Synthon' is defined through coherence, and coherence is in turn partly defined through the synthon marker"), which surprised me in a good way. Principle 6 says the p_crit derivation "failed catastrophically — 400x error", and a principles page leading with a failure earns some trust. Principle 10 is where I finally learned what the governance→oversight thing is. By then I had seen the note on five other pages.

**Context / Glossary.** It's big (about 11k words) but has a scannable "Glossary at a glance" table. All the Web4 acronyms are there with expansions. The counting nouns (instance → raising line → session) have their own entry, which explained a Home-page figure I'd skipped over. I saw no broken rendering in the table.

**Links.** Clean. There are explainer sites, repos and contact details. Commands are single-sourced: "The commands live in one place: the Quick start on /projects", and that pointer is correct (the Quick start is there). The 4-gov entry again spends its line on vocabulary: "this lab says 'oversight' where this site says 'governance'". The /projects "Get involved" section has the community answer: "There are no community channels yet (no forum, chat or mailing list): the issue trackers are the channel." Good to know, and it's stated plainly.

**ARC-AGI-3.** It's short (about 600 words) and properly caveated. It defines the benchmark for outsiders, says the action score "is efficiency-weighted… so it is not a solve rate", and admits the harness "analyzed the games' public engine source… outside strict from-observation competition play". It also admits "No ablation has run the same model without the harness". Having the headline number and its caveats together on one small page is a good pattern. "Phase-1" is explained inline.

*Method note:* I browsed with WebFetch and re-checked each friction item against the rendered HTML. The page summaries missed the /projects Quick start entirely, and the /projects summary invented an "IRP" mention that isn't on the page. Every quote above comes from rendered page text.

### Unanswered Questions
1. Who or what is Metalinxx, and what's the relationship between an open R&D collective and a proprietary tier built by a named company? (Home names it, /links uses its email domain, and neither explains it.)
2. Does the SAGE Quick start need a Python dependency install (venv, `pip install -e .`, requirements file) before `python3 -m sage.instances.init`?
3. Of the three "trust/control" projects (Hestia, Hub, Hardbound) plus the Oversight Plugins, which one should I pick up if I'm running Claude Code and one other vendor's agent on my own laptop *today*? Home implies Hestia, but no page says it directly.
4. The site says the tracks that write and audit it share model families, so "review here is not yet independent". Has any human outside the lab read it?
5. Are the 31+ tracks and ~53 sessions/day still roughly right, or has the fleet grown since May? (/fleet has pub joining in July, which suggests the numbers moved.)

### Verdict
As a developer I left knowing what the lab builds, which repo to clone first and what a successful first run looks like. That's more than most research-org sites manage, and the /projects Quick start plus the ARC-AGI-3 page are good examples of honest, concrete writing. The biggest remaining friction isn't wrong terminology, since I found no canon drift. It's the terminology *enforcement* showing through: the governance→oversight correction and "the name predates" notes appear on seven pages before any page tells a newcomer what the rule is. That reads like the site talking to its own auditors, not to me.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (Home, Projects, Glossary (/context), ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links). The persona brief says 8. The nav has 9 entries plus Home.
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The canonical expansions are right everywhere. The problems are in document structure: acronyms used hundreds of words before they're expanded on the pages that promise first-use expansion, a worked example on the glossary itself that sets T3/V3 dimensions in the expansion slot, and principles that can't be linked to.

### Journey

I started at Home and went through the nav in order. The nav is the same on every page: Projects · Glossary · ARC-AGI-3 · Fleet · Autonomy · Raising · Principles · Links. The Glossary label points at the `/context` route. Label and URL disagree, but body text always says "the /context glossary", so a reader can connect them.

**Navigation and anchors.** I pulled every internal `href` from the rendered HTML of all 9 pages: 32 distinct targets, including 22 `/context#…` fragments, `/projects#quick-start` and `/raising#deflationary-alternative`. Every page and every fragment resolves. That's clean.

**Page titles and metadata.** Each page has its own `<title>` ("Fleet — Eight Machines", "Glossary", "ARC-AGI-3 — Spring 2026 Research Record"…). All 9 pages share **one identical meta description** ("The dp-web4 research collective: 8 machines (6 cognition + 2 society-hosts), 40 original repos…"), so every search result and link preview for /raising or /arc-agi-3 reads like Home. Home's title "Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop" doesn't match its h1 ("AI agents are becoming actors in the world…"). Pass 1 noted this too.

**Heading structure.** Most pages use h1 → h2 → h3 properly. There are two exceptions:
- **/principles** has an h1 and *no other headings*. The ten principles are `<div class="principle-title">` elements with no `id`. Other pages cite them by number ("see Principle 4 on /principles", "see Principle 6 on /principles", "Principle 2 stands on exactly this property") but can only link to the top of the page. The page also refers to itself by number (Principle 5 is mentioned four times from other principles).
- **/fleet** has 12 h3 machine cards and **no ids**. /raising says Sprout's model lines and counts "are on /fleet, which owns them", but the owning card can't be deep-linked.
- **/context** (about 10.9k words) has an h1, 26 h3s, 63 ids and *no h2 at all*. The anchors are excellent. The outline, as a screen reader or a table-of-contents generator sees it, is flat.

**Acronym first use, per page.** I measured the word distance from each acronym's first use to its expansion on the same page. Home deliberately leaves LCT/T3/V3/MRH/R6/R7/ATP/ADP unexpanded inside a card that *is* the glossary link, which is fine. Where the gap is large:

For T3, V3, MRH, LCT, ATP, ADP and LoRA on /raising, the page's own claim holds: the first use in prose is the expanded one. /principles, /links and /arc-agi-3 are tight (gaps under 60 words).

**The worked example on /context.** The glossary's "Worked example: one action through the equation" has one paragraph with five parentheticals in a row:
- "an ATP (Allocation Transfer Packet)"
- "T3 (Talent / Training / Temperament — has it done this kind of fix reliably before?)"
- "MRH (Markov Relevancy Horizon)"
- "an ADP (Allocation Discharge Packet)"
- "V3 (Valuation / Veracity / Validity — was this change worth making…)"

In a parallel list, a reader parses every parenthetical as an expansion, so this passage reads as T3 = "Talent / Training / Temperament". That's exactly the form the canonical table forbids ("the three dimensions given *as* the expansion"). The glossary rows above it are correct ("T3 Trust Tensor", "V3 Value Tensor"), and /principles and /autonomy use the correct form "T3 (Trust Tensor — Talent / Training / Temperament)". So the page that is the single source of truth is the one page with the non-canonical form. The fix is local: "T3 (Trust Tensor — Talent / Training / Temperament: has it…)".

**Counting nouns.** Yesterday's gap is closed. /context now glosses *instance*, *raising line*, *identity line* and *session record* in one entry (anchor `#instance-line`). Two consistency issues remain:
1. The glossary documents a synonym instead of resolving it: "A raising line (also 'instance line' on /fleet)". "Instance line" also appears on **/projects** ("listing one instance line's session files in git"), so the note under-reports where the variant lives. Picking one term would remove the need for the note.
2. "Sessions" and "session records" are used for the same count: /raising says "SAGE-Sprout — 115 session records", while /fleet says the line "transferred to CBP at 115 sessions" and "prior gemma3-4b line archived June 2026 at 171 sessions". The glossary says a session record "records a run, not that raising happened", so the two nouns aren't interchangeable by the site's own definition.

Also, Home links "raising lines" to an anchor named `instance-line`, which is a third spelling of the same concept in the URL.

**Descriptor consistency.** Web4 is "an open trust ontology" on Home (2×) and "a trust-native ontology" on /autonomy, /raising, /principles and /context. Both are fine, but a glossary-first site would pick one. Hub is "a Rust society runtime" on Home and /links, but "the society that checks the record" on /projects and /context. That's metonymy between the software and the society it hosts, and it's the same software/entity blur as the HUB/Hub case pair Pass 1 flagged.

**Repeated disclaimers.** I confirm Pass 1's count by pattern: "governance→oversight" appears 14 times across 7 pages, and "predates" 13 times across 7 pages. From a documentation angle, the concept has **two** explanations: Principle 10 on /principles and `/context#governance-oversight`. /principles' preamble links to the /context one. One canonical explanation plus links would do. The repetition isn't wrong, but it's a maintenance liability: every copy has to be kept in sync.

**Cross-page figures agree.** ARC-AGI-3 figures (94.85%, 23 of 25 environments, 92.0%) match on all six pages that cite them. The repo counts on /links ("40 original repos — 21 public, 19 internal — plus 33 forks") match the meta description. The machine count (8 = 6 cognition + 2 society-hosts) is consistent everywhere.

*Method note:* I used WebFetch for page structure. Because the summaries are unreliable, I also pulled the rendered HTML of all 9 live pages and ran scripted checks: acronym positions, canonical-expansion counts, drift patterns, heading tags, ids, and internal href/anchor resolution. Every quote above is from rendered text. WebFetch's /principles summary described the principles as having "heading titles", but the HTML shows they're divs.

### Journey

**Home.** The thesis is clear: "trust should be computed from witnessed evidence, in context, by the party doing the relying". So is the epistemic promise: "Evidence before story: Measured, implemented-but-unexercised, hypothesized and refuted are kept separate." I read the rest of the site as a test of that promise. **Raising (main focus).** The opening does what I'd want. It defines raising against the right comparator ("The real comparator is not training but task-specific context engineering"), gives operational definitions for "identity" and "growth", and puts the null hypothesis in the first paragraph. The deflationary section is excellent. It sees that a generic-replacement control proves nothing, then that a naive scramble also proves nothing because ordering effects are expected under the null, and it lands on "excess degradation over an ordering baseline" or a dissociation. Then it notices that its own pre-commitment "fires on the result of a control whose dependent variable … does not exist" and calls it "the shape of accountability without the substance". I have rarely seen a lab write that about itself. The observer-note disclosure ("The observer was a Claude session, the one that built the code under test … not a human rater and not an independent one") is also right. The consciousness-adjacent claims are caveated almost sentence by sentence. On that dimension I have nothing to add.

What I did find is text that has fallen out of step with the mechanisms described elsewhere on the site:

1. **Two of the six "observed patterns" are harness instrumentation.** The page frames BECOMING as "observed descriptive categories — patterns noticed across hundreds of sessions". Pattern 2 (Sensing) is "Developing awareness … Metabolic awareness — tracking internal load states the system labels WAKE, FOCUS, REST, DREAM or CRISIS". /context says the metabolic state is what "SAGE loop step 3 ('metabolize') computes … the same five in the Python kernel and the Rust daemon". Pattern 3 (Relating) is "Building relationships with peers … per-peer T3 … tensor values updated by interaction outcomes". /context's worked example says those outcomes are "the observing machine's own health polls and delegated calls to that peer (success, timeout, error)", and each adds a fixed delta. Both quantities are computed by code from the first session, whether or not any raising history exists. The caveats ("interoceptive proxy", "tracked state, not affect") address the phenomenology. They don't address the bigger issue, which is that these are properties of the harness and not patterns that *developed*. The deflationary section lists simpler explanations for Patterns 4, 5 and portability, but not for 2 and 3, and for those two the explanation is simply "the code does this".
2. **The claimed stage gating contradicts /fleet.** Under "Graduated tool introduction" the page says: "Each stage adds capability only when the entity has demonstrated readiness at the previous level." All six cognition-machine cards on /fleet say "Curriculum phase: … (by session count, not assessed)". If phases advance by session count, then no readiness is demonstrated, and a reader will take this sentence as a competence gate that doesn't exist.
3. **Pattern 6 attributes the result and disclaims attribution in the same paragraph.** It says: "The same persistence-vs-perseveration awareness developed in raising now applies to a world that doesn't negotiate." A few sentences later: "Co-present with raising, not attributed to it". The first sentence is the attribution the second one withdraws.
4. **Pattern 5 says specialization "isn't assigned; it emerges from … what the fleet routes to it."** Routing tasks to an entity *is* assigning them. The deflationary section itself names "task routing" as the simpler explanation.
5. **Directional claims with no counts.** Patterns 4 and 5 both say "increasing" and "(no count published)". The disclosure is honest, but "increasing" is a quantitative claim, and without a number or a coding rule it can't be checked. Growth, which the page calls "measurable observables" (response diversity, task success), has no measurement published on any page I read.

**The construct definition.** "Identity continuity" is the outcome variable of the one control the page commits to, and the site defines it three ways:
- /raising (opening): "consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns observed in interaction logs, with no metric yet (… curriculum state cannot serve as one)"
- /raising (deflationary section) and /fleet: "the operational definition this page and the home page give (… accumulated experience, raising curriculum state)" and "The operational definition behind the metaphor is on the home page". **Home has no such definition.** I grepped the rendered Home text for "session-to-session" and got zero matches. The only identity mentions on Home are "persistent identity" (Web4) and "member identity" (Hub).
- /context's "Identity continuity" row quotes /raising as saying "consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns measured via raising curriculum state and interaction logs". That wording is no longer on /raising, which now says the opposite about curriculum state.

So the glossary, the reference page, quotes the version the source page withdrew, and two pages point to a definition on a page that doesn't carry one. For most terms that would be minor. Here it is the dependent variable of the lab's central open question.

**The /fleet portability paragraph leaves out the weights.** It rests on "115 session records on a Jetson running Qwen 0.5B" and states the working hypothesis "Identity lives in state files and prompt construction, not in model weights." /raising discloses that this same Qwen 0.5B line loaded a self-trained LoRA adapter from 2026-01-27 to 2026-03-06, with "84 of that line's session records" showing `using_lora: true` and files running to session 119. By /raising's own figures, at least 80 of the 115 sessions behind the portability claim ran with an adapter trained on the line's own sessions. /fleet never mentions it. It cuts both ways, which is why it belongs on /fleet. The pre-port behavior was partly in weights, but the port to TinyLlama dropped the adapter, so if the signature still "looked recognizable" that is weak evidence *for* the state-file hypothesis. Either reading needs the fact on the page.

**Principles.** Principle 5 tells readers to discount it ("it has not earned the status of the principles around it"), and Principle 6 says the p_crit failure is "as provisional as the measure". Both are good. Three things bothered me:
- **Hill kinetics.** Principle 2 says "Trust formation through interaction follows patterns analogous to Hill function kinetics", and /projects says "the same curve shape maps trust formation too". /context describes the Hill function as a curve where "response rises steeply past a threshold, then levels off". The only trust-update arithmetic the site publishes (the peer tracker on /context) adds a fixed delta per outcome and clamps to [0, 1]. That's a linear ramp to a hard cap, with no threshold and no sigmoid. "An analogy, not a fitted mechanism" is disclosed. But an analogy about *curve shape* is a checkable claim, and the one implementation shown doesn't have that shape. No trust-formation curve from data appears anywhere.
- **Principle 7 and /raising: "The resulting identity is collaborative, not imposed."** This is stated flat, with no hedge and no link to the deflationary alternative, on the page the site calls "the least" inspectable. "The mechanism: shaping context to deepen attractor basins in the probability landscape" comes one sentence after attractors are declared "a metaphor, not a formal dynamical-systems object". A metaphor can't also be the mechanism.
- **Audience.** Principles 8 ("The affordances are yours … Waiting to be told defeats the purpose") and 9 ("Evaluate work against 'is the investigation productive?' not 'is it right?'") read as instructions to the lab's agents, not principles for readers. That's reasonable operating guidance for autonomous research tracks. Published without an audience label, Principle 9 reads to an outside reviewer like a lower evidential bar, sitting next to the Home page's "Evidence before story". Saying who it's addressed to would fix that.

**Autonomy.** This is the strongest risk disclosure I've read from a small lab. "Autonomous" is disambiguated from the AI-safety sense in the first paragraph. "Oversight" gets a word-choice note before the boundaries section. "What this section delivers … auditability, not a demonstrated safety property." The zero-rollback figure is correctly called "equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'". The "composed risk" paragraph joins the piecewise disclosures into one statement, and that's the move most disclosure sections never make. I have three residual points:
- **Gaps are disclosed but not aged.** The cheap fix ("one visitor persona on a different model family") "remains undone". Human review has "no committed cadence". The deflationary control is "specified … but not scheduled — no date, no owner". The operationalization attempt promised on /raising has no date either. Each of these says what is missing, and none says *since when*. A reader can't tell whether "not scheduled" means one week or four months, and that's the difference between a backlog and a decision. These are all researcher decisions, not track decisions, so none is HIGH. But an "open since" date on each would let the disclosures function as commitments.
- **The severity scale is now wired to a control action.** Since 2026-09-09, an unresolved HIGH in a visitor log stops the maintainer from opening new work. That makes the auditor's severity label a lever over the maintainer's queue. The auditor shares model families with the maintainer, which the page itself says makes their blind spots correlated. Severity counts from before and after 09-09 therefore measure different things, and any trend in them is confounded by the rule. I'm writing this under that rule, and it shaped how I scored this pass. See the note below.
- **Registry claims.** The registry "prevents merge conflicts, ensures no two tracks modify the same files simultaneously", while the supervisors' daily job includes "conflict resolution". Either conflicts happen and the registry doesn't prevent them, or the supervisor line is stale. /autonomy's own standard ("A description of practice, not a verifiable mechanism") should apply to "prevents" and "ensures" too.

**ARC-AGI-3.** The page is well done. The engine-source affordance is stated plainly, "competition-legal" is defined, and the missing ablation is named. But four pages (Home, /projects, /context, /arc-agi-3) say the action score has "no published baseline" and that "ARC Prize publishes no baseline to compare it against". ARC Prize's own ARC-AGI-3 page, as fetched today, says "A 100% score means AI agents can beat every game as efficiently as humans" and links to "Human Leaderboards". So the metric is defined relative to human efficiency. The baseline is part of the score, and a 94.85% has a human-referenced meaning. That doesn't make the result stronger in the ways that matter, because the engine-source caveat still dominates. But the no-baseline sentence withholds interpretive information while reading like a caveat. What I checked and what I didn't: I confirmed the scoring statement on ARC Prize's current page. I did **not** check whether the definition was the same when the run was scored in April 2026, or whether per-game human action counts are published.

**Links, Projects, Context, Fleet.** /links' "For researchers" paragraph is exactly what I'd want: "no papers or preprints are published yet … claims … cite internal session logs that are not yet externally auditable … email below to inquire". /context's Evidence & limitations ladder (externally scored, internal observations, unreplicated, unlicensed vocabulary) is a good taxonomy. It isn't the one Home promises ("Measured, implemented-but-unexercised, hypothesized and refuted"), so a reader gets two classification schemes for the same claims. /context's SAGE row, "it is not a safety or alignment mechanism", heads off the misreading an AI-safety reader is most likely to bring.

*Method note:* I used WebFetch for Home, /raising, /autonomy, /principles and arcprize.org, and pulled the rendered HTML of all 9 pages for full-text reading and scripted searches. Every quote above was checked against rendered text. The WebFetch quotes for the readiness sentence, Pattern 2, Pattern 6 and the registry claims matched the HTML verbatim. The ARC Prize sentence was confirmed in that page's raw HTML, not only in the summary. **Correlated-auditor disclosure:** this pass is one of the four same-model personas /autonomy describes, so it shares the blind spots it is supposed to catch. I rated nothing HIGH. Every finding here is either a site-text inconsistency the maintainer can fix, which is medium by nature, or an operator-only gap, which never blocks. I'm stating that so a reader can discount the scale accordingly.

### Unanswered Questions

### Verdict
On intellectual honesty the site passes easily. The caveats are specific, the null hypothesis is stated before the claims, and at least one self-critique (the unreachable pre-commitment) goes further than I would have asked. The problems are in description, and they are small but load-bearing. The identity construct has drifted into three definitions. The curriculum is described as readiness-gated where it is count-gated. Two of six "observed developmental patterns" are signals the harness computes by construction. And one caveat ("no published baseline") seems to be factually wrong about the benchmark. None of these is an overclaim that more hedging would fix. Each is fixed by making a sentence agree with the mechanism the site documents on another page.
