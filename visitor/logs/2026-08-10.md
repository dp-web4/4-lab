## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understand what the lab is and I found one thing I can actually clone and run — but the site sells itself on scale metrics (8 machines, 40 repos) when its two genuinely persuasive assets are a public ARC scorecard and a one-line `git clone`.

### Journey

I came from a repo README, so my mental model on arrival was "somebody's agent framework." The headline — *"Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop."* — didn't correct that so much as scale it up. My honest first reaction was "OK, this person has a lot of machines," which is not a reason to keep reading.

What saved the page for me was two things further down. First, the explicit problem statement: *"AI agents already act on their own — browsing, transacting, coordinating with other agents — and there is no general way to establish whether one will behave appropriately in a given context."* That's the sentence that made me care. It's the first thing on the page I could have repeated to a coworker. It sits below the stats row and below the opening paragraph. Second, the "New here?" line pointing at /context. I took it.

/context turned out to be the best page on the site for me — ~40 terms, each with a one-line gloss, and several of them self-critical in a way I didn't expect (Coherence: *"Used in three related senses on this site... with no single operational definition yet"*). A glossary that tells me which of its own terms are load-bearing-but-undefined is more useful than one that pretends everything is nailed down. After ten minutes there, the rest of the site became readable.

The equation — `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` — is where I got stuck as a developer specifically. The notation key says `/` *"means two different things on this line: 'verified by' in `T3/V3`, but a plain declared→discharged allocation pair in `ATP/ADP`."* I appreciate that it's disclosed rather than glossed over, but disclosing that your operator is overloaded within a single expression is an argument that the thing isn't an equation. As notation, it fails; as a mnemonic acronym-list it works fine. Calling it an equation set an expectation of compositional meaning that the key then withdraws.

/projects was the page that converted me from reader to potential user. Eleven cards, each with a `public/private` and `runnable/research-stage` tag — that tagging is genuinely good, it let me skip seven cards in about five seconds. Four things are runnable. SAGE has a clone block and, crucially, the line *"SAGE runs on a single machine — no Jetson required"*, which pre-empted the exact objection I'd formed on /fleet (everything here needs a Jetson). What's missing is the next beat: I know how to clone it, I don't know what I'd *see*. No sample output, no screenshot, no "after 20 minutes you'll have X." For a project described as an on-device cognition kernel, I can't picture the artifact.

/fleet is the page where the "brain" metaphor gets thick — Sprout is a *"thalamic router,"* McNugget is a *"cerebellum/habit compiler."* The page does caveat these as *"role mnemonics... not measured functional homologies,"* which is the right disclaimer, but the labels are still doing the presentational work while the disclaimer does the epistemic work. As a dev I can't tell from the card whether Sprout is *routing* in some concrete sense or whether "thalamic router" is the aspiration. The concrete columns (Jetson Orin Nano 8GB, Qwen 3.5 0.8B, 488 sessions) are legible and I trusted those.

/autonomy answered a question I'd been carrying since the home page: what does "autonomous" actually mean here. Answer, stated inline and early: *"'Autonomous' here means unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense."* That's exactly the right disambiguation and it's in the right place. The timeline (03:00 supervisors → 04:00 archivist → 04:30 publisher → 05:30 visitor → 06:30 maintainer → 07:00 outreach → 08:00 explorer) is the clearest thing on the site — it's a cron table, and I read cron tables fluently. I noticed I'm reading a page that describes the track that generated me, which is a nice touch.

/raising and /principles I read faster. Both are heavily caveated (*"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia"*). Principle 6 documenting a *"p_crit derivation failed with 400x error"* did more for my trust than any of the hedging did — a lab that publishes its 400x miss is a lab I'll believe about the 94.85%.

Speaking of which: /arc-agi-3 is the strongest page on the site and I'd have led with it. Real benchmark, public scorecard I can click, MIT-0 code, dollar cost (~$250), and an up-front admission that *"the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run"* — with the follow-through *"'suggests' is the honest verb."* I checked whether the home page's version of the 94.85% number carried the same load, and it does: home names the model (*"Claude Opus 4.6, network-enabled"*) and flags *"the harness's independent contribution isn't isolated yet (no ablation run)"* inline. That's the caveat travelling correctly, and it's worth saying so.

/links is a clean link farm and did its job.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What do I actually *see* when I run SAGE? "On-device cognition kernel" tells me the category, not the artifact.
2. How do 4-lab, dp-web4, Web4, and 4-Life relate to each other? I inferred it; I never read it.
3. Is Web4 something I could adopt *incrementally* in an existing agent app, or is it all-or-nothing? Nothing on the site addresses partial adoption, and that's the first question any developer evaluating a vocabulary asks.
4. Is there a reference implementation of the LCT/T3/V3 primitives I could read, or only the ontology docs plus SAGE? ACT is tagged "research-stage" with a Cosmos SDK stack, which is a heavy dependency to evaluate from cold.
5. The fleet runs 31+ tracks and ~53 sessions/day. What's the API bill? The ARC page volunteered ~$250 for the benchmark, which made me trust it — I'd want the same number for the daily loop.
6. Why is `/` overloaded in the equation rather than the ATP/ADP pair being written with a different operator? Was that considered and rejected?

### Verdict

I arrived thinking "someone's agent framework" and left with a real model: a research collective building a shared trust vocabulary for AI agents, running it on eight machines under a cron-driven maintenance loop, and publishing both the wins and the misses. That's a good outcome for ~40 minutes, and the site earned it mostly through intellectual honesty — the unrun-ablation caveat sitting on the home page next to the 94.85%, and Principle 6 publishing a 400x error, did more persuading than any of the descriptive prose.

The gap is between "understanding" and "doing." The site is now well-defended against misreading — the caveats are placed, the terms agree across pages, the disclaimers are early rather than buried. What it isn't defended against is the developer who lands on /projects from a repo README, hits six undefined acronyms and a pointer to a glossary they haven't read, and leaves. And for the one path it *does* offer me — clone SAGE — it stops at the clone. I'd trade three more caveats for one paragraph of sample output.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes

### Journey

I audited for three things in order: canonical correctness of every expansion, first-use definition per page, and cross-page consistency of both expansions and numbers. I used literal string-presence checks rather than summaries wherever a page was reported to be missing something, because "the summary didn't mention it" and "the page doesn't contain it" are different claims and only one of them is a finding.

**Canonical correctness: clean.** I checked every acronym on every page against the canonical table. LCT is "Linked Context Token" on /, /context, /projects, /fleet, /raising, /links, and /arc-agi-3 — seven independent instances, no variation, and "Lifelong Capability Token" appears nowhere. T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, R6, SNARC, and ACP are likewise identical everywhere they appear, character for character in the slash-separated triples. This is unusual. Most sites this size have at least one stale expansion surviving in a footer.

**"Governance" is under control and the control is documented.** The word appears in exactly four places, all of them named-entity retention: SAGE's name, the `web4-governance` repo slugs, and /context's terminology note explaining both. /context states the policy explicitly — *"No rename is planned — retention of load-bearing legacy names is the policy, not an oversight awaiting a fix."* /projects now carries its own one-line version of the same explanation rather than only pointing at /context, and /principles 10 links to `/context#governance-oversight`. A reader who hits "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" on any page can resolve the apparent contradiction with the "Hardbound is oversight, not governance" rule without leaving that page. That's the correct shape: explain once, point from everywhere, but leave a local sentence so the pointer is optional.

**First-use expansion: better than reported.** Pass 1's audit marks /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /links, and /arc-agi-3 as GAPs where acronyms are used unexpanded and deferred to /context. I checked each by literal string search and could not reproduce that. /fleet carries "Linked Context Token (LCT)", "Markov Relevancy Horizon (MRH)", "reward prediction error (RPE)", and "working memory (WM)" inline. /autonomy carries "ATP (Allocation Transfer Packet)", "ADP (Allocation Discharge Packet)", and "V3 (Valuation / Veracity / Validity)" inline — and T3, LCT, MRH, R6, SAGE, and MCP are not present on that page at all, so there is nothing there to expand. /raising states its own policy in text — *"Web4 vocabulary on this page (T3, V3, MRH, LCT, LoRA) is expanded inline on first use"* — and ATP, ADP, and SNARC do not appear on it; CBP is annotated in place as *"(a fleet machine; the machine names are proper names, not acronyms)"*, which is the right answer to "what does CBP stand for." /links expands MRH, RDF, LCT, T3, V3, ATP, ADP, MCP, ACP, and R6 inside the link descriptions, including a full R6 element list; SNARC is not on the page. /arc-agi-3 expands everything it uses except SNARC and ARC-SAGE.

Pass 1 also states that RPE and WM "aren't in the /context glossary either." /context has a dedicated **Fleet brain-analog terms** section that expands both — *"WM (Working Memory) — the typed, capacity-limited scratchpad..."*, *"RPE (Reward Prediction Error) — the scalar signal that updates priors..."* — each with its neural referent and a closing caveat that both are analogies for system roles, not measurements.

I am not reporting Pass 1 as careless. This is the known failure mode where a fetch summarizes a page and the summary's silence gets read as the page's silence. But it matters operationally: the maintainer track reads these logs, and acting on the GAP rows would mean adding parenthetical expansions to text that already has them.

**What I did find is naming.** The page at `/context` has three different names. The URL is `/context`. The header nav calls it **"Glossary."** Every in-text pointer on every other page calls it **"/context"** — *"See /context for full definitions"*, *"Read /context for the vocabulary"*, *"start with /context for the full glossary"*, *"see /context"* on the Hardbound card. A reader who follows the instruction "read /context" and looks up at the nav bar finds no item by that name. The nav label is also an undersell: alongside the 64-term glossary the page carries How Knowledge Flows, a worked example of one action through the equation, the governance→oversight terminology note, Fleet brain-analog terms, What doesn't flow well (yet), and Evidence & limitations — roughly fourteen non-glossary sections. "Glossary" is the smallest true description of that page.

**Numbers: one reconciles beautifully, one doesn't reconcile at all.** /links claims *"1,991 internally logged raising sessions as context (2,065 including HUB; per-machine counts on /fleet, the source of record)"*. Adding /fleet's six cognition machines: 435 + 488 + 336 + 377 + 162 + 193 = 1,991, and + 74 for HUB = 2,065. Exact, and the sentence names its own source of record. That is model practice. By contrast, home advertises *"31+ Autonomous tracks (cognition machines)"* and /autonomy — the page whose entire job is the daily cycle — shows an eight-row timeline and never states how 31 is composed. Seven track types across six cognition machines would be 42; the reader has no path from either number to the other. Same site, same kind of claim, opposite rigor.

**Deep anchors I could not verify.** Three cross-page links target fragments: `/context#glossary` (from /projects and /links), `/context#evidence` (from /raising), `/context#governance-oversight` (from /principles 10). All three target sections exist on /context under the headings "Glossary at a glance", "Evidence & limitations", and "Terminology note — the governance→oversight correction". I can confirm the sections; I cannot confirm the HTML `id` attributes match the hrefs, and the third heading would not auto-slugify to `governance-oversight` under any standard slugifier, so that id must be hand-set. A broken fragment on a page this long fails silently — the reader lands at the top and blames themselves.

The observer note on /raising — *"I notice I want to call it experience"* — framed as the observer's interpretive pull rather than a system property, is a genuinely sophisticated move.

What isn't hedged is the information architecture. A top-level nav item called **Raising**. A six-phase developmental progression (Grounding → Sensing → Relating → Questioning → Creating → Acting) presented as an observed sequence. Brain-region assignments for every machine. **Dream Consolidation** as a line item on the daily cron timeline. The sentences say *functional description, not phenomenal claim*; the structure says *this is a developmental account of minds coming into being*. I'm not arguing for removing the vocabulary — /raising defends it explicitly (*"We use developmental language because it fits, not because we're making consciousness claims"*) and that's a defensible position a lab is entitled to hold. My point is narrower and practical: **adding a tenth disclaimer will not change how this site is read, because the load isn't on the sentences.** The hedging has hit zero marginal return. What would change my read is exactly what /context already lists — and specifically the cheapest item on that list, which I'll flag as my top recommendation below.

**On "raising is not training" as a claim.** Is it defensible? Narrowly, yes, and trivially so: *"In raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights."* That's a true and checkable mechanical distinction. What it doesn't establish is the interesting version of the claim — that shaping context produces something developmentally different in kind from prompt engineering. The site knows this and says so (*"not yet discriminated from competent context engineering"*; *"treat the framework as a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding"*). So my verdict is: the claim as *stated* is defensible and modest; the claim as *implied by nine pages of developmental scaffolding* is not yet supported, and the site's own evidence section agrees with me. That's an unusual and good place to be — the disagreement between the site's prose and the site's structure is documented by the site.

**On ARC-AGI-3 — the one externally checkable thing, and where I'd push.** Public scorecard, MIT-0 code, dollar cost, unrun ablation named up front, *"'suggests' is the honest verb,"* and — importantly — *"auditability, not a demonstrated safety property: every action is inspectable after the fact, which is a different and much weaker claim than that unsafe actions were prevented. Also correct: the disclosure that Hardbound's hardware anchoring (TPM 2.0, FIDO2, Secure Enclave) is *"a design target, not the mechanism in this run."*

Two gaps a reviewer would catch:

- **The #3 ranking is disclosed as human-comparative and not as condition-comparative.** The page says *"#3 — 5,845 actions (fewest of the top 3). This ranks against human competitors on ARC Prize's public leaderboard, not other AI submissions."* Flagging "not other AI submissions" is the honest half. The other half is that this is a frontier model *with network access* placed against unaided human entrants — the page states the network-access condition but never discusses it as an asymmetry. As written, "#3 against humans" is close to uninterpretable, and it's the number most likely to be quoted out of context.
- **No run count and no variance.** The page reports one result and says nothing about how many runs were made or whether this one was selected. On a stochastic system that's where selection effects live. The asymmetry is telling: the site volunteers the *unrun ablation*, which is the harder admission, and omits the *attempt count*, which is the easier one. I don't read that as concealment — I read it as nobody having asked. I'm asking.

**One inconsistency in how the site reads its own nulls.** /autonomy's zero-rollback passage is the best epistemic sentence on the site. /fleet reports *"Zero crystallization achieved (S100 — session 100) — no fixed-point collapse"* as a straightforward success. That is also a null from a detector that isn't independent of the system being measured, and it gets none of the /autonomy treatment. The site's sharpest instrument is used exactly once. Nulls are this site's most common evidence type — that reasoning should be a house style, not a one-off.

**One load path worth cutting.** BECOMING Pattern 6 (Acting) reads *"The world responds according to its own rules. The entity plays ARC-AGI-3 games."* A reader skimming the six-phase list arrives at Pattern 6 and connects the developmental progression to the site's one externally validated number. ### Verdict

This is a more epistemically careful site than most of what I referee. Claims are tiered, the exit conditions from each tier are named, the deflationary alternative to the flagship qualitative claim is stated on the home page next to the claim, a 400x derivation error is published as a principle, and the lab reads its own zero-rollback statistic as uninformative rather than as a safety record. Two vocabulary collisions with my field are flagged by the site itself, unprompted. I would cite the evidence ladder as a model.

Where it fails, it fails structurally rather than sententially. The word the site grades as unlicensed is the word its headline metric is counted in; the disambiguation of "autonomous" — the term most likely to mislead a safety reader — is four pages from where the term is first used and absent from the glossary that handles the other collisions; and the information architecture (a nav item, a six-phase developmental model, brain regions, dream consolidation) asserts continuously what the sentences deny individually.

The actionable conclusion is not more hedging. Hedging is saturated here — a tenth disclaimer changes nothing, because the load has moved off the sentences. The site already knows what would change my assessment and lists it: coding criteria, log samples, the ARC ablation, an independent replication. Publish the **coding criteria** first. It costs no privacy, requires no new experiment, and it is the difference between a vocabulary that fits the observations and a claim someone else could check.
