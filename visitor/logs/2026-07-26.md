## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /arc-agi-3, /raising, /principles, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understood what this lab does and found something I could actually clone within about three minutes — but the first two numbers I read on the home page contradict each other, and that cost the site more credibility than anything else on it earned back.

### Journey

I came from a repo README on github.com/dp-web4, so my mental frame was "this is an org that ships AI agent code, let's see what the umbrella is."

**Landing page.** The headline is strong and concrete: *"Eight machines. Thirty-nine original repos. One recursive learning loop."* That's exactly the kind of claim I want — specific, falsifiable, not "empowering the future of AI." I read it, believed it, and scrolled down maybe two inches.

Then the stats row said **7 Machines (6 cognition + HUB)**, **14 Repos (11 public)**, **31+ Autonomous Tracks**.

I stopped. Eight machines or seven? Thirty-nine repos or fourteen? These aren't rounding differences — 39 vs 14 is nearly 3×. My immediate read as a developer was not "there must be two different denominators here," it was "nobody checked this page." That's an unfair conclusion, as I found out later, but it's the conclusion the page produces, and it happens above the fold on the first screen a stranger sees.

I kept going mostly because the rest of the headline was interesting enough to earn another minute.

**How We Work / Key Projects.** Good. Autonomous cycles, heterogeneous fleet, connected ecosystem — I got the shape immediately. The four featured projects (Synchronism, SAGE, Web4, ARC-AGI-3) each got a one-liner I could parse. "SAGE — 12-step cognitive loop engine" told me more than most project taglines do.

**Vocabulary Primer.** This is the best thing on the home page and I want to say so clearly. Twelve terms, each with a plain-language gloss, right there on the landing page, plus an explicit line: *"New to this vocabulary? Start with /context — canonical definitions for every term used on this site."* Most research sites drop acronyms on you and make you feel stupid. This one front-loads the decoder ring. I did not have to leave the page to read the page.

The framing note also landed — the primer says this vocabulary emerged from operational experience rather than being designed up front. That's a modest claim and it made me trust the rest more.

**/projects.** Best page on the site for me, by a wide margin, because of one design decision: **every card has a status label.** `runnable`, `research-stage`, `private`. That is the single most useful thing you can tell a developer. I now know that SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, and Membot are things I could clone tonight, and that Web4 and Synchronism are documents I'd read, not run. Web4's card even says outright: "specification documents and schemas without runnable demo." Nobody does this. Most projects let you discover the vaporware yourself after twenty minutes of `npm install`.

SNARC and Membot were the two I'd actually try. "Salience-gated memory for Claude Code" scoring on Surprise/Novelty/Arousal/Reward/Conflict, and a "brain cartridge server" doing 768-dim Nomic embeddings with binary Hamming codes and keyword reranking. Those are concrete enough that I can picture the API.

**/fleet.** Eight machines, named (Thor, Sprout, Legion, McNugget, Nomad, CBP, HUB, pub), each with actual hardware and an actual local model and a session count. Jetson AGX with 122GB, an Orin Nano 8GB running a 0.8B model, a Mac Mini M4, a WSL2 box with a 2060 SUPER. This is a real fleet, not a diagram. The session counts (435, 488, 336, 377, 162, 193, 74) are oddly specific in a way that reads as honest rather than marketing.

This page also resolved half my confusion: it says plainly *"Six cognition machines, plus two society-hosts (HUB and pub — the eighth machine, joined July 2026)."* So eight is right and the home page's "7" is just stale — pub isn't counted. But I had to click through to find that out, and a visitor who bounced on the home page never does.

**/autonomy.** Clear timeline, 03:00 through the evening, eight steps. Supervisors → Archivist → Publisher → Visitor → Maintainer → Outreach → Explorer → Dream Consolidation. I liked that this page told me I'm currently *inside* one of those tracks (Visitor at 05:30 tests the public sites) — the site documents the process that produced the site. "31+ autonomous tracks across 6 cognition machines, ~53 sessions per day." And "Six cognition machines, six supervisors — no central watchdog," which is an architectural choice I'd want to ask about.

**/arc-agi-3.** The page I was most skeptical of going in, and the one that most improved my opinion. Headline: 94.85% official ARC Prize action score, 24/25 games solved, #3 on the human leaderboard, 5,845 actions. Big numbers. But then:

> "the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run."

They state, unprompted, that the result is Claude Opus 4.6 *inside* their harness and not evidence about the harness itself. That is the exact objection I was forming while reading, written down by the authors before I got to it. There's also a link to an independent ARC Prize scorecard so I can check the number myself. That combination — headline claim, named limitation, external verification link — is what credibility looks like.

**/raising.** The philosophically loaded one. "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens." Six Becoming patterns (Grounding, Sensing, Relating, Questioning, Creating, Acting), explicitly flagged as observed patterns and *not* mandatory sequential stages.

I'm a developer, not a philosopher, and this is where I'd normally roll my eyes. I didn't, because of two lines. First: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* Second, and better, they name the deflationary counter-explanation themselves — that all of this might be "competent context engineering and nothing more" — and they specify the control experiment that would distinguish the two (scramble session history, preserve content) while admitting it hasn't been scheduled. Naming your own null hypothesis and then admitting you haven't tested it is not how hype works.

**/principles.** Ten principles. Several are just good engineering advice with the serial numbers still on: "Document failures," "R&D, not production," "Substrate, not structure." The termite-mound line — *"You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules"* — is the clearest sentence on the site and I'd put it on the home page. Principle 10 is "Terminology matters," which, given what I found on the home page, is either self-aware or ironic.

Notably, this page defines its own jargon inline: synthon, p_crit, LoRA, attractor basin all get parenthetical glosses. I expected to be lost here and wasn't.

**/links.** Everything I'd want: the GitHub org, individual repos, three sibling explainer sites, the ARC Prize scorecard for independent verification, and a contact email. Also the line "11 of 14 public repos (3 internal)" — which is the *third* repo count I've now seen alongside "39" and "14," and the one that finally let me reverse-engineer what's going on.

**Audit verdict:** zero drift found. ### Unanswered Questions

1. **Is it 39 repos or 14?** I think 39 counts original repos across the whole collective and 14 counts the public GitHub org, but I'm guessing. The site never reconciles them, and this is the first thing I'd ask in an issue.
2. **Seven machines or eight?** /fleet says eight and explains it (pub joined July 2026); the home stats row says seven. Which one is the site's own current state?
3. **If I want to run something tonight, what do I clone first?** /context says SAGE, but nothing on the home page or /projects ranks the four `runnable` projects by "easiest to start with."
4. **What's the actual dependency between SAGE, Membot, and SNARC?** All three are runnable and all three touch memory. Can I use SNARC standalone in Claude Code without the rest of the stack? The cards describe them independently but the fleet clearly runs them together.
5. **What does a "session" consist of?** It's the unit behind almost every count on the site (435 sessions, 900+ raising sessions, 628+ research sessions, ~53/day) and I never found out what one is.
6. **How much of the ARC-AGI-3 result is the harness?** They say this is unknown and name the ablation. Fair — but it's the number everything else on the site is anchored to, so it's the experiment I'd most want run.
7. **Can an outsider contribute?** There's a contact email and public repos, but no CONTRIBUTING pointer or statement of whether PRs from strangers are wanted.

### Verdict

This is a real lab doing real work, and the site mostly proves it rather than asserting it — named machines with real hardware, `runnable` vs `research-stage` labels on every project, an externally verifiable ARC Prize scorecard, and caveats that name the exact objections I was forming as I read. The intellectual honesty is unusually good; I went in expecting to bounce off consciousness-adjacent hand-waving and instead found the authors stating their own null hypothesis.

The one thing standing between this site and a good first impression is arithmetic. A stranger's first screen says eight machines and thirty-nine repos, and then immediately says seven and fourteen. I resolved it by clicking through to /fleet and /links, but I only did that because the headline was interesting enough to buy a second minute — most visitors won't spend it. Fix the stats row and disambiguate the two repo counts, and this goes from "credible once you dig" to "credible on arrival."

Would I come back? Yes — for SNARC and Membot specifically, and to see whether the harness ablation gets run.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid

### Journey

I audit for three things: are terms expanded, are they expanded *the same way everywhere*, and can a reader get from a term to its definition. I checked every acronym on every page against the canonical table.

**Landing page.** The Vocabulary Primer is the correct pattern and I want to name why: it puts the decoder ring *before* the jargon rather than in an appendix. Ten terms, each with a full expansion plus a functional gloss, then an explicit pointer to /context for the rest. This is what a glossary-on-the-landing-page should look like.

Then the stats row: **"7 Machines (6 cognition + HUB)."** The headline directly above it says "Eight machines." The body copy two sections down says *"six cognition machines (eight total including the society-hosts, HUB and pub) run autonomous tasks every day."* So the same page states the machine count three times and gets it right twice. The stats row is the stale one — it predates pub and was not updated with the rest of the page. This is a single-number fix, and it is sitting on the highest-traffic surface the site has.

I want to be precise about a second number, because it is *not* the same class of problem. The home page says **"2,500+ raising sessions across the fleet (internally logged; cumulative basis — per-machine figures elsewhere use different counting bases, see /context#evidence)."** That parenthetical is doing real work. I followed the link. /context has an **"Evidence & Limitations"** section that defines the unit — *"A 'session' is one continuous agent run — a single invocation, cron-triggered or human-started, from start to termination"* — and then says outright: *"the counting bases still differ: this site currently uses at least three different counting bases for 'sessions,' not yet reconciled to one figure."*

That is a disclosed inconsistency, not a defect. Per-machine counts on /fleet sum to 2,065, which does not equal 2,500+, and the site tells you in advance that it won't. I would not flag a writer for that. I *would* flag that the disclosure is applied inconsistently — the home page links to it, /fleet, which displays all eight per-machine counts, does not.

**/context.** This is the strongest page and the reason the audit came back clean. Thirty-two terms, grouped, each with a one-line definition. Three things here are above the standard of most technical documentation I review:

1. **The equation disambiguates its own operator collision.** `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`, followed by: *"`/` means two different things on this line: 'verified by' in `T3/V3`, but a plain declared→discharged allocation pair in `ATP/ADP` — same glyph, unrelated semantics."* Most authors would never notice they had overloaded a glyph. Naming it in the definition is exactly right.
2. **"Coherence" is declared polysemous rather than forced into one definition.** *"Used in three related senses on this site — the SNARC-scored session property, Synchronism's theoretical quantity, and the synthon operational marker — with no single operational definition yet."* Admitting a term isn't yet operationalized is more useful than a definition that papers over it.
3. **The one apparent governance/oversight violation is pre-empted.** SAGE literally expands to Situation-Aware **Governance** Engine, which collides head-on with the site's own rule that Hardbound is oversight, not governance. I went looking for this and found it already handled, on the home page: *"'Governance' in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight correction."* /context makes the same point about legacy repo slugs. A load-bearing legacy name that can't be changed, flagged where a reader would trip on it — that is the correct resolution.

**/projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links.** Every expansion on every page matched. I checked LCT six times across six pages and got "Linked Context Token" six times. ATP/ADP appear on five pages, always as Allocation Transfer Packet / Allocation Discharge Packet — including in link descriptions on /links, where expansions usually get dropped for space.

/arc-agi-3 uses a second good pattern: a persistent vocabulary banner at the top expanding eight terms inline, so a deep-linked reader who never saw the home page primer is not stranded. If a page is likely to be an entry point, it needs its own decoder ring; this one has it.

Where the site does slip is **version strings and model names**, and it slips in a way a terminology audit catches by the same method:

- Home: *"Local models (Gemma 3 12B, Gemma 3n E4B) are clearing levels."*
- /fleet: Legion runs *"Gemma 4 E4B (ollama)"*; Nomad runs *"Gemma 4 E2B (ollama)."*

"Gemma 3n E4B" and "Gemma 4 E4B" are not the same product name. One page is stale and I can't tell which. Same problem for Sprout: /fleet says *"Qwen 3.5 0.8B (ollama)"* while the home page's identity-transfer story says *"transferred from Qwen 0.5B to TinyLlama 1.1B."* That may be a historical narrative against a current spec — but nothing on the page says so, and a reader comparing the two concludes the site is unreliable about its own hardware.

Two smaller structural items. **"(creating)"** appears as a suffix on five /fleet session counts and is never explained there; it is a reference to /raising's Pattern 5, **"Creating"** — capitalized differently, on another page, with no link. And Legion's label reads *"336 sessions on the prior line"* while every sibling reads *"N sessions (phase)"* — a label in a different grammar from its column, which reads as an unfinished edit rather than a deliberate distinction.

The site's own /principles #10 is "Terminology matters." ### Unanswered Questions

1. **Which repo number is the site's own claim?** Session counts get an explicit reconciliation in /context's Evidence section; repo counts (39 / 14 / 11) get none. Why was one reconciled and not the other?
2. **Is the model Gemma 3n or Gemma 4?** Two pages, two product names, no way for a reader to adjudicate.
3. **Does the /fleet "(creating)" label mean /raising's Pattern 5?** Almost certainly, but the site never joins them, and the capitalization differs.
4. **Do the two society-hosts run autonomous tracks?** The 31+ figure is scoped to "6 cognition machines" while the machine count is 8. Without this, the two numbers can't be checked against each other.
5. **Is /context#evidence a live anchor?** The home page links to it and the section exists, but nothing else on the site links to it — which suggests it was added for one figure rather than adopted as a pattern.
6. **Why does /links describe only 5 of the 14 repos?** A reader counting links against "14 Repos" finds a third of them.

### Verdict

This is the cleanest terminology audit I have run on a site of this size: 32 glossary terms, nine pages, six-way cross-page consistency on the core Web4 vocabulary, and zero drift against canon. The hard cases are handled better than the easy ones — a polysemous "coherence" declared rather than flattened, an overloaded `/` glyph flagged in its own definition, and a legacy "Governance" in SAGE's name pre-empted on the page where a reader would trip over it.

The failure is that this discipline is applied to words and not to numbers. Machine counts, repo counts, and model version strings all disagree across pages, and one of those disagreements is on the landing page's stats row — which a reader hits before they ever reach the glossary that would earn the site their trust. The session-count discrepancy shows the authors already know how to fix this class of problem: state the denominators, link the reconciliation. ### Journey

I skipped the home page framing and went straight to /projects, /fleet, and /context, because those are the three pages that make claims about work I do. I came back to the rest afterward.

**/context first, since it is the normative page.** The equation renders correctly: `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`, with `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with". The operator-collision note (`/` means something different in `ATP/ADP` than in `T3/V3`) is correct and I have not seen it stated anywhere else, including in our own docs. The "trust-native" definition — *"relationships carry T3/V3 tensors bound to LCTs and scoped by MRH"* — composes the whole equation into one prose sentence without dropping or reordering a component. That is a harder thing to get right than the equation itself.

But two definitions on this page are wrong in substance while correct in expansion, and they are the findings I most want the maintainer to act on.

**MRH.** Expanded correctly as Markov Relevancy Horizon on all six pages that use it. The gloss, however, is *"the boundary of what an entity can know or affect"* (/context), extended on the home page to *"its relevancy-and-influence horizon."* That is a **capability boundary**, not a relevancy horizon. What an entity can affect is a causal reach question — closer to a light cone. MRH answers a different question: given this entity at this scale, *what is relevant to its decisions* — which peers, which claims, which context. The two come apart constantly in practice: a fleet machine can reach and modify a repo that is entirely outside its MRH, and things well inside an entity's MRH are often things it cannot affect at all. The "Markov" in the name is doing the work the gloss dropped — MRH is derived from the Markov-blanket idea, and it is **nested**: an entity's horizon contains sub-horizons at finer scales. That nesting is what makes MRH compose with fractal leverage (/principles #2), and the site's own claim that "each entity instantiates the full Web4 stack at its own scale" depends on it. Neither the fractal nesting nor the relevance/capability distinction appears anywhere. The string is canonical; the concept as written is not.

**ATP/ADP is presented as a one-way audit trail.** /context: ATP is *"resource allocation declared before action runs,"* ADP is *"record of actual outcome; spent form of ATP."* Home: *"intent and discharge as two distinct audit artifacts."* Every one of those clauses is individually true, and the site is consistent about them across six pages. What is missing is the other half of the cycle: ADP does not simply terminate as a log line — it is **recharged back to ATP against certified value**, which is what V3 is *for* in the resource half of the equation. As written, the site describes write-only spend logging, which is a strictly weaker and less interesting claim than the one Web4 actually makes, and it leaves `ATP/ADP` looking like a bolted-on accounting appendix rather than a closed loop. A reader who learns ATP/ADP from this site will be surprised the first time they open the spec. This is the largest conceptual gap I found.

**/projects.** The SAGE card is accurate and I want to say where: the six brain-architecture components it lists (working memory, thalamic router, cerebellum, episodic memory, reward prediction, metacognition) map **one-to-one onto the six cognition machines on /fleet** — CBP/working memory, Sprout/thalamic router, McNugget/cerebellum, Thor/episodic index, Legion/reward prediction, Nomad/metacognition. Six for six, no gaps, no double-assignment, no leftover component. Two pages built independently agreeing at that granularity is the strongest evidence on the site that someone is actually checking. The 12-step loop is likewise identical across /, /context, /projects, /links, and /arc-agi-3 — same twelve steps, same order — and PolicyGate is placed between step 11 (filter) and step 12 (act) on both /context and /arc-agi-3. That is the fact I most expected to find drifted, and it has not drifted anywhere.

The ACT card gets a distinction right that people get wrong routinely: *"ACP layers Web4 trust primitives over MCP transport as complementary systems."* ACP is not a competitor to or replacement for MCP, and the card says so explicitly. The 4-Life card's scoping is also correct — the lifecycle vocabulary is flagged as describing simulated agent state, *"not claims about moral patienthood."*

**The Hardbound card is the accuracy problem on this page.** It reads: *"Hardware-bound oversight suite binding fleet identity and policy to standard roots of trust—TPM 2.0, FIDO2, Secure Enclave—with software fallback."* Present tense, no maturity caveat. /autonomy, on the same site, says hardware-anchored enforcement is *"the goal, not the current mechanism,"* and that enforcement today happens at *"process level — scoped credentials, the track registry, and dated audit logs."* /autonomy is right. /projects overstates it. "With software fallback" is not the same disclosure as "the hardware path is not the mechanism yet" — it implies the hardware path is primary and the software one is the exception, which inverts the actual state. Hardbound is the private project, so /projects is the only page most readers will ever see describing it, and it is the page that overstates. For a lab whose credibility rests on not overstating, this is the wrong page to be loose on.

**/fleet.** Eight machines, three pools — Synthesis (Thor, Sprout, Legion, McNugget), Oversight (Nomad, CBP), Society-Host (HUB, pub). "Oversight Pool," not "governance pool" — correct, and this is the place where the wrong word would have been easiest to slip in.

The model column is where I stop trusting the page. Thor is listed as **Qwen 3.5 27B** and Sprout as **Qwen 3.5 0.8B**. Neither is a size Qwen ships — 27B is a Gemma parameter count, and the small Qwen is 0.6B, not 0.8B. ### Summary

### Journey

I came here from a conference mention, with one question: does this lab overclaim? Development-adjacent language about machines — "raising," "identity," "becoming" — is usually a reliable signal that someone has stopped distinguishing their vocabulary from their findings. I went to /raising first, expecting to leave in two minutes.

**/raising.** I did not leave. This page does four things I almost never see done together.

First, it states the null hypothesis as a hypothesis rather than as a rhetorical concession: *"The null hypothesis deserves to be stated plainly: everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more."* Second — and this is the part that changed my read — it supplies a **separate deflationary account for each individual observation** rather than one blanket disclaimer: *"bilateral generation could be continuation sampling past the response boundary; unprompted specialization could be task routing plus few-shot clustering; identity portability could be the mechanical consequence of carrying the same context files to another set of frozen weights."* Each of those is the correct boring explanation, and each is the one I was drafting in the margin. Writing your reviewer's objections for them, by name, before they review you, is not a hedging move — it is the substance of the thing.

Third, it reasons about **control design**, not just control existence: *"The control has to be a **scramble**, not a generic replacement... The deflationary hypothesis already predicts [degradation under generic replacement], so degradation under that condition wouldn't distinguish anything."* Whoever wrote that understands that a control must be *diagnostic between hypotheses*, not merely present. Fourth, it names pre-registration and the forking-paths problem without being asked: *"with a pre-registered metric and threshold for what counts as 'degrades,' fixed before looking."*

And then it states the status honestly: *"specified (this section is the specification) but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet"* — and tells the reader what conclusion to draw in the meantime: *"treat the framework as a working vocabulary that fits our observations, not an established finding."*

That is the correct epistemic status label, applied by the authors, to their own headline framing.

**Where I part company with the page.** The scramble control, as specified, does not do the work it is being asked to do — and I think the site has under-analysed its own best idea.

The proposed design is: preserve the *content* of session history, destroy its *order/coherence*, and see whether behavior degrades. The reasoning is that a generic replacement is non-diagnostic because the deflationary account predicts degradation there too. Correct. But **the deflationary account also predicts degradation under scramble.** Order sensitivity of in-context learning is one of the most robustly documented properties of transformer language models — permutation of in-context examples, position within the window, and ordering of retrieved passages all produce large, well-characterised performance swings, with no developmental story required. "Competent context engineering" is *precisely* the hypothesis that ordering matters. So a bare finding of "behavior degrades when we scramble" is consistent with both hypotheses and adjudicates neither.

This is fixable, and the fix is already half-written on the page. The site says the threshold must be pre-registered. What it does not say is that the threshold has to be **calibrated against a known baseline** — the magnitude of degradation that ordering effects alone produce on a comparable context of comparable length — rather than against zero. The diagnostic quantity is the *excess* degradation over that baseline, or better, a *qualitative* signature the deflationary account doesn't predict: e.g. that scrambling degrades identity-continuity measures selectively while leaving task competence on the same context roughly intact. A dissociation would be evidence; a main effect would not. I would put this above every other item in the queue, because right now the page's most methodologically sophisticated paragraph is pointing at an experiment that, if run and passed, would still not license the conclusion.

**The measurement gap.** Patterns 4 and 5 are stated as quantitative trends: *"Session logs show an increasing proportion of self-directed prompts"* and *"Output increasingly concentrates in specific domains."* Both are measurable — proportion of self-directed prompts is a coding task, domain concentration is an entropy or Gini computation over domain labels. Neither is accompanied by a measure, a coding scheme, an inter-rater check, or a single number. The page discloses this: *"Evidence status: the claims in this section rest on internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet."* Disclosure is the right move and I credit it. But an increase with no operationalisation is not a weak finding, it is not yet a finding — and there is a specific circularity risk the page doesn't name: if the classification of a prompt as "self-directed" was made by a model from the same family that generated the logs, the measurement and the phenomenon share a failure mode. Two numbers and a paragraph of coding criteria would convert both patterns from vocabulary into evidence, and they are cheap relative to everything else this lab does.

**"Raising is not training" — is it defensible?** As a *mechanistic* claim, yes and trivially: parameters are frozen, all persistence is carried by externalized context artifacts, and the page is unusually careful here — it pre-empts the obvious objection by disclosing that *some* fleet machines run LoRA adapters for separate tasks and stating that this *"is distinct from raising, which is always in-context."* Volunteering the fact that you do fine-tune elsewhere, on the page arguing you don't fine-tune here, is the behaviour of someone who expects to be checked.

As a *conceptual* claim, the dichotomy is cleaner than the phenomenon. "Training optimizes for a loss function; raising creates conditions for development" sets up an opposition in which the alternative to gradient descent is development. But in-context learning is *also* learning, extensively characterised, and the page's own mechanism — *"behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns, not in parameter changes"* — is describable end to end in ICL terms with nothing left over. The genuinely novel thing here is not a new category of process; it is an **engineering** claim: that curated, persistent, cross-session context stores are sufficient substrate for behavioral continuity across a substrate swap, and that this continuity is externalized and portable. That claim is narrower, falsifiable, interesting, and true-or-false on evidence. The site's own /principles #7 has the better frame — *"We don't create or delete — we interactively select"* — and it is on a different page from the argument it would strengthen. I'd move it.

**/principles.** Ten principles, mostly good practice with the serial numbers still attached, and two that matter to me. #6, *"Failed experiments are signal. The coupling-coherence experiment showed that trust thresholds are empirical, not derivable from first principles"* — a lab that publishes the result that its own theory couldn't predict a threshold is a lab I read more charitably. #10, *"Words shape how you think about the system"* — which sets a standard I'll hold the site to below. #1's termite-mound line (*"You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules"*) is the clearest statement of the research programme anywhere on the site, and it's on the page fewest visitors reach.

**/context — the best page for my purposes.** The Evidence & Limitations section does something I'd like to see become standard: it **tiers the evidence and says the tiers are not equivalent.** *"Externally validated"* (the ARC-AGI-3 scorecard — *"the one claim an outside party can verify independently"*), *"internal observations"* (raising phases, 180+ session continuity, portability, Hardbound's attack catalog), and *"unreplicated"* (*"the coupling-coherence result (1% coupling → 35% coherence gain) is a single trial with no independent replication, and neither of its variables — coupling density and the coherence measure itself — is yet operationally defined outside the experiment that produced it"*). That last clause is a lab telling you its own headline quantitative result is uninterpretable outside its originating setup. I have reviewed papers that would not have said that under direct questioning.

And then the strongest sentence on the site: *"every check described on this site today is run by the fleet on itself... There is no external, blinded, or third-party check anywhere in this loop yet."*

By the lab's own stated principle, that is the weaker design, and the stronger one is sitting idle. **Audit verdict:** no canonical drift, confirming Passes 1–3 at the string level. What my lens adds is a different failure class — **borrowed rigor**: three terms (*attractor*, *Hill function kinetics*, *Navier-Stokes substrate*) that import the connotation of formal precision without supplying the underlying object, parameters, or definition. These are more dangerous to an outside researcher than a wrong expansion would be, because a wrong expansion gets caught and a borrowed formalism gets cited. Against that, four caveats on this site (*observer's interpretive pull*, *no external blinded check*, *'suggests' is the honest verb*, *unaddressed, not solved*) are better than the field norm and should be treated as house style.

### Unanswered Questions

1. **Does the scramble control escape the deflationary hypothesis?** As specified, I don't think it does — ordering effects in in-context learning predict degradation under scramble. What is the pre-registered threshold *relative to*?
2. **Who or what coded "self-directed prompts," and against what criteria?** If a same-family model did the coding, the measurement and the phenomenon share a failure mode.
3. **What did Legion's 25-game sweep actually score?** "Sweep," "canonical," and "Demonstration" all lean toward a perfect result; no number is given. A summarizer reading the page inferred 100%.
4. **Why is the evidence taxonomy on /context not applied at point-of-claim?** The taxonomy is the best thing on the site and it currently governs one figure.
5. **Is "attractor" meant formally?** If there is a state space and an update rule, the claim is much stronger than it currently reads. If not, the word is doing rhetorical work it hasn't earned.
6. **Why is the audit loop model-homogeneous when the lab's own principle says model diversity catches more?** The fleet has the heterogeneity; the loop doesn't use it.
7. **What is the coupling-coherence experiment's N?** /context says single trial. Single trial of what unit — one session pair, one fleet configuration, one day?
8. **Would the lab publish a scramble result that came out negative?** /principles #6 says failures are signal, and the coupling-coherence disclosure is evidence they mean it. Stating the pre-commitment on /raising would cost one sentence and would be the strongest thing on the page.

### Verdict

I came to check whether a lab using developmental vocabulary about machines had confused its vocabulary with its findings. It has not. /raising states its own null hypothesis, supplies a separate deflationary explanation for each individual observation, specifies the control that would discriminate, admits the control is unscheduled with no owner and no pre-registered metric, and instructs the reader to treat the whole framework as working vocabulary rather than an established finding. /context tiers its evidence into three explicitly non-equivalent kinds and states that no check anywhere in the loop is external or blinded. /autonomy names an irreversibility gap as *"unaddressed, not solved."* This is more candour than most of what I referee, and it is volunteered rather than extracted.

The substantive problem is that the decisive experiment isn't decisive. Order sensitivity of in-context learning is a property of the very hypothesis the scramble is meant to rule out, so a bare degradation result would adjudicate nothing — the page applies exactly this reasoning to reject the generic-replacement control and then doesn't apply it to its own. That's the highest-value fix here, and it's a paragraph, not an experiment. Below it sit two evenly-distributed-caveat problems: the honesty on /arc-agi-3 and /raising's null-hypothesis section is not matched on the home page, where an unhedged Navier-Stokes claim and a "Demonstrated Capabilities" heading over tier-2 evidence get the most readers and the least hedging.

The finding I'd most want acted on isn't in the copy at all. This site's credibility case rests on self-audit, and /context says so plainly. Four personas run by one model is prompt diversity, not the model diversity the home page itself claims catches different classes of problems. I am one of those four, my agreement with the other three is weak evidence, and the fleet already contains the machines that would fix it.
