## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understood what this lab *is* within about five minutes and I believe the ARC number because they showed me the scorecard — but I still don't know what happens if I actually clone SAGE.

### Journey

I got here from a repo README on the dp-web4 org, so my starting question was blunt: what is this org and is any of it runnable?

The landing hero answers the first half fast — "Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop." That's a concrete, checkable claim, and I like that it's immediately followed by an accounting note (40 original + 32 forks = 72, "verified against GitHub org on 2026-07-26"). That's the kind of thing that makes me trust the rest of the page. Nobody bothers to reconcile a repo count unless they've been burned by someone not reconciling it.

The paragraph right under the hero is where I stalled. "The dp-web4 research collective constructs a trust-native ontology—encompassing shared vocabulary and trust frameworks for AI agents in the knowledge-representation domain—plus autonomous AI cognition and theoretical frameworks connecting them across heterogeneous machinery that educates, validates, and develops one another." I read that twice and got nothing on the first pass. It's one sentence carrying four ideas, all in nominalizations. Compare it to the three-card "How We Work" block a screen lower, which lands instantly: 31+ tracks, no human in the loop; desktop-to-Jetson hardware, each machine its own model; four projects that share one equation. Those cards did in nine seconds what the opening paragraph failed to do in two readings. The good version of the intro is already on the page — it's just 400px too far down.

Scrolling on, the Key Projects block is genuinely well done for someone like me. Synchronism gets flagged as "a research conjecture, not established physics" *in the description itself*, not in a footnote. SAGE tells me the loop is 12 steps and names all 12. The ARC-AGI-3 entry gives 94.85% and 24/25 and then immediately says "Frontier model + harness—the harness's independent contribution remains unisolated (no ablation conducted)." I've read a lot of AI project pages that would have stopped after "94.85%". This one didn't, and that bought a lot of goodwill.

The Vocabulary Primer at the bottom is where the equation lives: `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`. Every symbol gets a gloss underneath. I appreciate that the `/` ambiguity is called out explicitly ("identical glyph, unrelated semantics") rather than papered over — that's exactly the kind of thing that would have wasted twenty minutes of my time later.

Clicked **Projects** next, because "runnable" is what I care about. This is the best page on the site for my persona. Twelve cards, each with a status label — `runnable`, `research-stage`, `private`. That single label does more work than any prose could: SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, and Membot are runnable; Web4 and ACT and Synchronism are specs and papers; Hardbound I can't see at all. I now know exactly where to spend my afternoon.

Then I hit "Get involved" and deflated. It's `git clone https://github.com/dp-web4/SAGE`, `cd SAGE`, and then "the README contains Python/conda setup and first session guidance." That is not a quick start; that is a pointer to a quick start. I don't know: do I need a GPU? Does the 12-step loop run on CPU? How long does the first session take? What does it print? For a project whose whole pitch is "runs on hardware from Jetson to laptop," the one question the page doesn't answer is *will it run on mine*. Three lines — minimum VRAM, expected first-run time, and a snippet of what the loop actually outputs — would flip this from "interesting" to "I'll try it tonight."

**Context** is a wall, and I mean that neutrally — it's a glossary and it's supposed to be. 26 sections and a 30-row term table. I bounced off the narrative but the table itself is exactly what I wanted: LCT is Linked Context Token, MRH is Markov Relevancy Horizon, ATP/ADP are Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packets. Every acronym on the site resolves here. The worked example ("one action through the equation": LCT → ATP → T3/V3 scoped by MRH → R6 → execute → ADP → ledger) is the single most useful thing on the site for me — it turned an abstract equation into a request lifecycle, which is a shape I already know. That example is buried at section 12 of 26. It should be section 2.

**Fleet** is fun and concrete: eight machines in three pools, each with hardware, model, and a session count. Thor on a Jetson AGX running a 27B, Sprout on an Orin Nano running 0.8B, CBP on WSL2 with a 2060 SUPER. That last one is roughly my machine, which is a quietly effective piece of persuasion. What's missing for a developer: *how* the models are served. Ollama? llama.cpp? vLLM? The page tells me every model name and no part of the serving stack, which is the first thing I'd need to reproduce any of it.

**Autonomy** was the page that made me take the project seriously. A timeline of tracks (03:00 supervisors, 04:00 archivist, 04:30 publisher, 05:30 visitor, 06:30 maintainer, 07:00 outreach, 08:00 explorer), then a safety section that says out loud that the maintainer track "is the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate," that rollback is ~1 minute via Vercel but detection is on a ~24-hour cycle, and that publisher pushes to repos are worse because "commits cannot be fully unpublished once cloned." That is a real threat model, written by someone who has thought about it, published on their own marketing site. I did not expect that.

**Raising** and **Principles** I skimmed — this is the theory-dense end and I'm the persona who skips theory. Raising's "we're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia" registered as the right disclaimer in the right place. Principles reads like an engineering culture doc; #3 ("R&D, not production") and #6 ("Document failures," which cites their own p_crit derivation failing by 400x) are the two I'd quote to a colleague.

**ARC-AGI-3** closes the loop: 94.85% action score, 24/25 games, #3 on the human leaderboard with the fewest actions of the top three, ~$250 in API spend, and a link to the actual ARC Prize scorecard. Then the caveat again — Claude Opus 4.6 with network access, while the real competition is local and offline, and the ablation separating harness from model is unrun. The cost figure is a nice touch; it's the number that tells me whether I could replicate this, and almost nobody publishes it.

**Links** is a plain link page and does its job. Contact is dp@metalinxx.io.

Net: I came in asking "what is this org." I'm leaving able to answer that, plus knowing which four repos are actually runnable and which claim is externally verified versus internally observed. What I can't answer is the thing I'd need to actually participate — what running any of it looks like on my machine.

I trust these people because they told me the unflattering parts before I could find them. ## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Zero canonical-term drift across nine pages — the acronym discipline here is better than most funded documentation sets I audit — but the site's own correction machinery has fallen out of sync with itself, and the paragraph written to reconcile the session numbers contains a wrong example.

### Journey

I audit terminology for a living, so I read this site the way I'd read a docs set going into a release freeze: nav first, then every acronym's first occurrence per page, then cross-page comparison of every expansion string. I had the canonical table in hand. My working expectation was the usual outcome — three or four expansions that drifted apart because different pages were written on different days by different people.

That is not what I found. **Every canonical term checks out on every page.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" in all five places it's expanded; "Lifelong Capability Token" appears nowhere. T3 is character-for-character "Talent / Training / Temperament" on six pages — same spacing, same slashes, same order. V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6 likewise. R6's six elements appear in identical order in all three places they're listed (/projects, /context, /arc-agi-3), which is the kind of thing that normally rots first because nobody re-checks list order. Web4 is called an ontology everywhere, and the words "architecture" and "infrastructure" appear on this site almost exclusively inside explicit negations of themselves. "Production ready" does not appear at all.

So I spent the rest of the pass on the harder question: not *are the terms right*, but *is the site's terminology apparatus internally consistent*. Three answers there, and they're more interesting than the drift check.

**Nav and structure.** Eight links — Projects, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links — same order on every page, no dead ends, no orphans. Nine pages, all reachable. Clean.

**First-use discipline, page by page.** /fleet is the standout and I want to name it, because "this page is correct" is a finding too. Every abbreviation on /fleet is expanded inline at first use: "working memory (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex / dlPFC)", "dopamine / reward prediction error (RPE)", "its identity keyed to its Linked Context Token (LCT)", "that peer's own Markov Relevancy Horizon (MRH)", "LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation)", "SNARC (Surprise / Novelty / Arousal / Reward / Conflict salience-gated memory)". A reader can land on /fleet cold, from a search engine, and never need another page. That is the standard the rest of the site should be held to.

/autonomy passes for a different reason: it uses almost no jargon. LCT, MRH, T3, V3, R6 and SAGE do not appear on it at all, and the two terms it does use are expanded parenthetically on first occurrence — "each scheduled track issues an ATP (Allocation Transfer Packet)", "an ADP (Allocation Discharge Packet) records actual spend". Self-contained by restraint rather than by repetition. Also fine.

The two pages that fail first-use are /arc-agi-3 and /raising, and /arc-agi-3 is the one that matters: it is linked directly from the public ARC Prize scorecard, which makes it the single most likely cold-entry point on the whole site for someone with no Web4 context. It uses **PolicyGate** with no definition (defined only in /context's glossary), and uses MRH and LCT unexpanded. The page that outsiders arrive at first is the page assuming the most prior reading. /raising uses ATP, ADP and SNARC unexpanded while correctly expanding LCT, T3, V3, MRH, LoRA and SAGE — a partial application of a rule the page clearly knows.

**Then the correction machinery.** This site has an unusual and admirable habit: it publishes dated corrections inline. /projects carries "Corrected 2026-07-26: ~80 decades is the spread of critical-density values across physical systems, not the range of any single coherence curve — one curve saturates within roughly one to two decades." That is a precise, well-written correction distinguishing two quantities that had been conflated.

/context, one day later, still reads: "The conjecture spans 80 orders of magnitude — from quantum to cosmic — with experimental validation at specific scales and the full range as the ongoing research target." A decade *is* an order of magnitude. So the corrected page and the uncorrected page are now stating the same figure about two different quantities, and a reader who visits both in either order will not be able to tell which reading is current. A correction applied to one page and not the others is worse than no correction: it converts a single error into a contradiction, and it quietly teaches the reader that dated correction notes on this site are not authoritative.

**And then the finding I did not expect.** /context's "Evidence & limitations" section contains a genuinely excellent paragraph, "Session-count basis," which does something almost nobody does: it defines the unit ("a 'session' is one continuous agent run — a single invocation, cron-triggered or human-started, from start to termination") and then openly states that the site uses at least three different counting bases, not yet reconciled, and instructs the reader to treat each number as scoped to its page. That is exactly right, and it pre-empts the discrepancy Pass 1 hit. Credit where due — and the underlying arithmetic holds up: /fleet's per-machine figures sum to 2,065 across the seven machines that carry counts, and the archived prior-model runs the page lists (Legion's phi4 56, Nomad's gemma3-4b 171, CBP's TinyLlama) plausibly close the gap to the home page's "2,500+" under a cumulative-since-tracking-began basis.

But the paragraph's own examples are wrong. It cites "current-phase per-machine counts (/fleet, e.g. Sprout's '115+')". /fleet's Sprout row reads **488 sessions**. The string "115" *does* appear on /fleet — in a completely different context: "SAGE-Sprout's behavioral patterns — developed over 115 raising sessions on a Jetson running Qwen 0.5B — transferred to TinyLlama 1.1B on CBP... in February 2026." That's the identity-portability transfer figure from a retired model, not a current-phase count. The reconciliation paragraph picks the one number on /fleet that belongs to a *fourth* basis and labels it as an example of the second. The same paragraph attributes "2,500+" to "/projects and the home page"; a literal search of /projects turns up no such string. The page readers are sent to when the numbers confuse them is itself miscounting. This is the highest-value fix on the site, because it's cheap and it protects the site's most credibility-earning paragraph.

**One convention applied unevenly.** /context's glossary handles HUB beautifully: "A proper name, not an acronym (capitalized by convention)." That's precisely the right service to a reader. /raising then sets **BECOMING** in full caps across a six-phase model — Grounding, Sensing, Relating, Questioning, Creating, Acting — whose initials spell nothing, and never says it isn't an acronym. The site invented the fix and didn't apply it to the second instance. Pass 1 lost time to this; so did I.

**The SAGE problem, handled — six times.** SAGE expands to "Situation-Aware Governance Engine," which contains the exact word Principle 10 forbids ("Hardbound isn't 'governance' — it's oversight"). The site is aware and discloses it. But it discloses it on /, /projects, /context, /fleet, /raising and /arc-agi-3, in six different wordings: "Governance in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight correction" (home); "...predates the lab's governance→oversight terminology correction; the name is retained upstream" (/projects); "'Governance' in SAGE's name (kept across code, papers, and the ARC Prize benchmark) and the web4-governance repo slugs" (/context). Six restatements of one fact is where drift is *born*. Today they agree; after two more maintainer cycles editing them independently, they won't. This should be one canonical sentence, authored once in the /context glossary and quoted verbatim or linked from the other five.

**A note on the `/` operator, in the site's favor.** My canonical table says `/` = "verified by". The site says something more careful: `/` means "verified by" in `T3/V3`, but in `ATP/ADP` it's a plain declared→discharged pair — "identical glyph, unrelated semantics," stated explicitly on both / and /context. A strict auditor reading my table alone would flag that as an addition. It isn't drift; it's a glyph collision the canon doesn't yet address, caught and documented by the site before a reader could trip on it. **My recommendation runs upstream:** the canonical terms table should absorb this distinction, not the site should drop it.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Which Synchronism figure is current — "~80 decades of critical-density spread" (/projects, corrected 2026-07-26) or "spans 80 orders of magnitude" (/context)? Given they use the same unit, a reader cannot resolve this from the site.

3. Does the canonical table intend `/` to carry one meaning or two? The site documents two and flags the collision explicitly; the canonical table lists one. The site's reading looks correct and the canon looks incomplete — which gets updated?
4. Is MoltBot a rename of OpenClaw, a fork, or a separate target? /projects and /links name it differently with no relationship stated.

### Verdict

The terminology audit — the thing I was actually sent to do — comes back clean. Nine pages, eleven canonical terms, zero drift, and character-level consistency on expansions that normally diverge within a month; the Web4 equation and R6's six elements are identical everywhere they appear. I'd hold this up as an example of what enforced terminology looks like when it works, and /fleet's inline-expansion discipline is the best single page of documentation on the site.

The failures are all in the *maintenance layer*, not the terminology layer, and they share one shape: mechanisms that work correctly in one place and were not propagated to the others. The dated Synchronism correction went onto /projects and not /context. The "proper name, not an acronym" note exists for HUB and not for BECOMING. The first-use expansion rule is applied perfectly on /fleet and skipped on the one page outsiders actually land on. Six copies of the SAGE disclaimer agree today only because nobody has edited them separately yet.

The one I'd fix first is the smallest: "Session-count basis" on /context is the most credibility-earning paragraph on the site — it defines its unit, admits three unreconciled bases, and tells the reader to distrust cross-page comparison — and its worked example points at the wrong number on /fleet. A paragraph that exists to prevent numeric confusion should not itself be confused. Fix that, propagate the Synchronism correction, and this site's documentation quality matches its terminology discipline, which is already better than it needs to be.

So I went looking at the layer below expansions — the definitions — and that is where I found the two things I'd escalate.

**MRH is defined by causal reach on the two pages that matter most.** The home vocabulary primer and the /context glossary row both carry the same string: *"The boundary of what an entity can know or affect given its position, history, and context"*, with the home version continuing *"which is what determines the scope of relevance for its decisions."* That is the wrong end of the concept. MRH is a **relevancy** horizon — the Markov-blanket-shaped scope of what is *relevant* to a decision. It is not a statement about capability, and the difference is load-bearing: in the fleet's own oversight model, an agent's credentials routinely reach further than its horizon, and that gap is the entire reason the horizon is worth declaring.

The site knows this. /context's MRH section, two paragraphs down from its own glossary row, says: *"Relevance, not raw causal reach: an entity can often touch things outside its horizon and often cannot act on things inside it."* Read those two sentences together and the glossary row is refuted by the section it heads — if an entity can touch things outside the horizon, the horizon is not "the boundary of what an entity can affect." The bridging clause ("which is what determines the scope of relevance") asserts the exact inference the next paragraph denies.

And the correct string already exists on the same page: /context's equation section glosses MRH as *"the scope of what is relevant to an entity's decisions, fractally nested."* That's right. One page carries both the right definition and the wrong one, eleven sections apart, and the wrong one is in the lookup table.

**ADP has the same defect in the same place.** The /context glossary row reads: *"The spent form of ATP — the record of actual outcome."* Full stop. Two states. The ATP/ADP section below it says: *"ADP is not where the accounting ends: discharged packets are charged back to ATP against validated value creation"*, and the home primer describes the recharging cycle correctly. The glossary row states precisely the reading the section exists to prevent.

This is not pedantry about a missing clause. ATP/ADP being a *cycle* is why it sits in the equation as a paired term at all. An ADP that is merely "the record of actual outcome" makes ATP/ADP look like a declare-then-log convention — which is what every reader who has only ever seen a budgeting API will assume by default. The recharge-against-V3-validated-value step is the thing that makes it an economy rather than a logging schema, and it is the thing the canonical lookup entry drops.

**These two are one bug.** The glossary rows are lossy compressions, and both times the compression landed on the misreading the prose was written to refute. That's the highest-leverage text on this site — the /context table is what people paste into their own CLAUDE.md when they start a Web4-adjacent repo. ### Journey

I came in from a conference mention with the usual prior: an independent "AI lab" site with developmental vocabulary is going to be a consciousness claim wearing a lab coat. I was wrong about that, and I want to be precise about how wrong, because the interesting failures here are not the ones I expected.

The home page front-loads the disclaimer before I could form the objection: developmental language — machines that "teach," "raise," and hold "identities" — is *"functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience."* First screen, not a footnote. That's the right placement. The numeric claims then carry an explicit **evidence tier** label — *"internal observation — all three rest on the fleet's own session logs. Documented and dated, but not externally audited, not blinded, and with no published log samples or coding criteria."* I have reviewed papers that would not survive that sentence being applied to them.

**/raising** is where I spent most of my time, and it contains the best and the worst of the site.

The best: a section titled **"The deflationary alternative"** that states the null hypothesis in the site's own words — *"everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more"* — and then does something almost nobody does. It rejects the cheap control (generic-content replacement) on the correct grounds that "task-relevant context beats task-irrelevant context" is already *predicted* by the deflationary hypothesis, so passing it proves nothing. It specifies the right control instead: a **scramble** preserving content but permuting order, measuring *excess* degradation over an ordering baseline, and requiring a **dissociation** — identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence survives. And it pre-commits to the negative result: *"if this control runs and the result is a bare main effect, or no degradation at all, that outcome gets published on this page and the developmental vocabulary gets retired."*

That is a pre-registration in everything but name. Designed by people who understand that the control which can't fail isn't a control. I did not expect to find it.

The worst is the next clause: **"specified but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet."**

Everything on this site routes through that sentence. The developmental vocabulary is not decoration — it is the site's central descriptive claim ("developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do"), and that is an empirical claim about descriptive fit, and the scramble control is exactly the experiment that adjudicates it. So the disclaimers and the unrun control are coupled: the honesty of the framing is *contingent on a test that hasn't been run*. An unscheduled pre-registration is not a weaker pre-registration; it is a different object. It carries the reputational benefit of rigor with none of the exposure. And the incentive gradient runs the wrong way — the control, if it comes back negative, retires the vocabulary the site is built on. Under that gradient, "not scheduled" is a stable state, and the longer it holds, the more the scheduling failure becomes the datum.

I want to be fair about the direction of this criticism: the site put the sentence there. Nobody made them write "no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet." They could have written "planned." My ask is small and it is the highest-leverage thing on the site: **put a date on it.** A date that slips is auditable and creates a record. "Not scheduled" cannot slip, which is precisely the problem.

And there is a dependency the site lists as a separate open item but which is actually the blocker. You cannot run a dissociation test without an operational metric for identity-continuity, and /context admits that **coherence** has *"no single operational definition yet"* across three senses, that **crystallization** is described but not formalized, and that metabolic state is *"an interoceptive proxy value, not yet a formally specified model."* Behavioral identity itself is defined as *"consistent session-to-session interaction patterns"* — consistent by what measure, at what threshold, coded by whom? The missing operational definitions and the unscheduled control are not two gaps. They are one gap, and the definitions are upstream. I'd state that dependency explicitly on /raising; right now the control reads as awaiting attention when it's actually awaiting a metric.

**The observer note.** /raising records *"I notice I want to call it experience"* and labels it *"the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim."* This is the single most sophisticated move on the site. It is a researcher documenting their own degrees of freedom, in-line, as data about the observer rather than the system. I've seen this in maybe two places outside of psychophysics methods sections. Keep it.

**Where /raising loses its own discipline.** The disclaimers cluster — Patterns 1–6 and the "What we're not claiming" section are well-hedged — but **"Identity is not self-concept"** carries none. It asserts a *consistent separation* between stable behavioral identity and drifting self-description across 180+ sessions, and that is the strongest and most scientifically interesting claim on the entire site: a dissociation. Dissociations are exactly the claims that need blinding, because "consistent behavioral pattern" is a judgment made by the party that wants the result. No coding criteria, no inter-rater reliability, no blinded coder, no log samples. The site's global evidence caveat covers this in principle; the specific claim carries no caveat of its own, and it is the one a researcher will quote. Attach the tier locally.

**/principles** reads as an engineering culture document with two entries that function as evidence. Principle 6 ("Document failures") cites the lab's own **p_crit derivation failing by 400x** and concludes that thresholds are empirical rather than derivable from first principles. That is a negative result published on a marketing page, used as support for the principle that produced it. Principle 3 ("R&D, not production") states outright that nothing here suits *safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings*. Principle 7 contains a line I did not expect from a group whose thesis is in-context shaping: *"frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior."* They named the attractor-risk gap in their own method.

Where /principles overreaches is Principle 5. The synthon is positioned as the concept bridging component behavior and system emergence, and its empirical support is **1% coupling → 35% coherence gain, single trial, unreplicated**. /context's own evidence section says neither variable — coupling density or the coherence measure — is *"operationally defined outside the experiment that produced it."* A quantity whose measure exists only inside one procedure is not a measurement; it is an outcome of that procedure. And the same trial does evidentiary duty in three places: Synchronism's empirical grounding (§7), the synthon operational marker (§20), and Principle 5. Each instance is individually caveated — genuinely, they are — but per-instance caveats do not cure the multiplication. One trial cited three times reads as three supports. Label it once as a single observation cited thrice.

**/autonomy** is the page I'd hand to someone arguing that hobbyist autonomy projects don't think about failure. The threat model is real and self-incriminating: the maintainer track is *"the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate"*; the posture is *"detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent"*; automated detection runs on *"roughly 24-hour loop"*; and for content that is *"technically correct but misleading"* the **exposure window is unbounded**. Publisher is worse and they say so: a repo push *"is not fully revertible"* once cloned, and *"that gap is unaddressed, not solved."* Naming an unsolved gap as unsolved on your own site is the behavior the rest of the field claims to value.

And then the gap I'd actually escalate: **there is not one incident on this page.** Thirty-one-plus tracks, roughly 53 sessions a day, running for months, and the safety section contains only hypothetical failure modes. Either nothing has ever gone wrong — in which case that is the most interesting operational datum the lab possesses and should be stated as a measured rate with a denominator — or things have gone wrong and are not published. For a site carrying a principle literally titled "Document failures," operational failures are the one failure class with no log. A dated incident table (what shipped, how it was caught, detection latency, rollback time, whether the visitor loop found it or a human did) would convert /autonomy from a design document into an evidence base, and it would give the detect-and-revert posture an actual measured detection latency instead of an estimated one. This is my highest-value ask after the control date.

Two smaller things on that page. "31+ autonomous tracks, no human triggers them" is unqualified at first use on the home page; **autonomy here means unattended scheduling, not goal-autonomy**, and a safety reader imports the second meaning by default. And /autonomy correctly says *"semantic validation still depends on human review"* while /context says that human review of the logs is *"asynchronous with no committed cadence."* Those two sentences sit on different pages and together they say the semantic backstop is unscheduled. That should be one sentence in one place.

**/context §26** contains the strongest paragraph on the site, and I'll quote the part that earned my attention: *"every check described on this site today is run by the fleet on itself... There is no external, blinded, or third-party check anywhere in this loop yet. For a lab whose research subject is trust and oversight, that is a real gap, not a footnote — named here so it stays visible rather than staying implicit."*

A group whose research subject is oversight, naming its own oversight conflict of interest, in the domain where the conflict is most embarrassing. That paragraph is section **26 of 26** on the longest page on the site. The best credibility asset is buried at the bottom of the deepest page. The home page already demonstrates the fix — it prints an inline "Evidence tier: internal observation" badge next to its numbers. Extend that pattern to every load-bearing claim and link each badge back to §26.

**/arc-agi-3** is the one externally checkable claim and it is handled correctly: 94.85% action score, 24/25 games, linked public ARC Prize scorecard, ~$250 API spend, and the honest verb flagged as honest — *"with the harness-vs-model ablation unrun, 'suggests' is the honest verb."* The conditions gap is stated (Opus 4.6 with network access on the public set, versus the competition's 32GB-VRAM offline sandbox on an unseen set, "not attempted"). **/context §7 (Synchronism)** is the one place the site contradicts itself in a way that matters to me. /projects carries a dated correction that ~80 decades is the **spread of critical-density values across systems**, not any curve's range. §7 still reads *"spans 80 orders of magnitude — from quantum to cosmic — with experimental validation at specific scales and the full range as the ongoing research target."* Beyond the conflation the other passes caught: framing a scatter of thresholds as a *range to validate across* is a category error, not just a stale number. You don't validate across a distribution of critical points. I'd delete that clause independent of the 80-decade fix.

**/context §5–6 (Hardbound)** has the inverse problem of everything else on the site. Present tense throughout — hardware binding *"anchors policy enforcement to physical devices"*, *"nothing executes until PolicyGate clears it"* — while /projects says plainly that *"hardware-anchored enforcement is what this research is building toward, not the current mechanism"* and that enforcement today is process-level credential scoping. Hardbound is private, so it is the single least verifiable project on the site, and it is the one page where the unverifiable project gets the confident tense and the checkable ones get the hedges. That inversion is the shape of a claim drifting, and it is worth fixing *because* the rest of the site is careful — the local exception is what a skeptical reader will find first.

### Unanswered Questions

1. What blocks the scramble control — the metric, the compute, or the willingness? The site says "no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet" but not *why*. If it's the metric, that's a tractable and publishable piece of work on its own.
2. Has anything ever actually gone wrong? Thirty-one-plus tracks over months with zero recorded incidents is either a remarkable operational result or an unpublished log. Both are interesting; only one is stated.
3. What is the measured detection latency for a bad maintainer push? The page estimates ~24 hours from the schedule. Has that been observed, and what's the distribution?
4. Who would count as an external checker here, and what would it take? §26 names the absence of third-party review as "a real gap." Is anyone being asked, or is the gap noted and parked?
5. How is "consistent session-to-session behavioral pattern" actually coded? This is the measurement underlying the site's central internal claim and no criteria, threshold, or coder are described.
6. If the 1%/35% coupling result were retracted tomorrow, what else on the site changes? It supports Synchronism's grounding, the synthon marker, and Principle 5. A dependency map would tell readers how much weight one unreplicated trial is carrying.
7. Is the developmental vocabulary a hypothesis or a house style? The site defends it as better-fitting description, which is testable — but nothing on the site says what would count as it fitting *worse*, apart from the unrun control.

### Verdict

This is the most epistemically careful independent research site I have read this year, and I want that on the record before the criticism: the evidence tiering is explicit, the one externally verifiable claim links to its scorecard *and* names the ablation it hasn't run, the failure log includes a 400x error in the lab's own derivation, and §26 names the self-verification conflict — a lab whose subject is oversight, admitting every check on the site is run by the fleet on itself — as *"a real gap, not a footnote."* Nobody had to write that. The consciousness question I arrived to prosecute is handled better here than in most published work: disclaimed on five pages, with the observer's own interpretive pull recorded as data about the observer.

The failure is singular and structural. The site's honesty rests on a falsification test that is designed correctly and scheduled never, and the vocabulary that test would adjudicate is the vocabulary the whole site is written in. That is not hypocrisy — it's the ordinary incentive gradient, which is exactly why pre-registration exists as an institution. The same shape repeats twice more: a safety section with a rigorous threat model and no incident history, and a strongest-claim dissociation with no local caveat and no blinded coding. In all three cases the design is right and the *execution that would expose it to falsification* is the missing half.

So my recommendation is not "hedge more" — this site hedges well, and hedging further would be the cheap move that changes nothing. It's the opposite: **stop hedging and start exposing.** Put a date on the scramble control. Publish the incident log with detection latencies. Publish the coding criteria for behavioral consistency, redacted if necessary. Each of those risks a result the lab won't like, which is precisely what would make this site's honesty a demonstrated property rather than a well-written one.
