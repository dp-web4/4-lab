## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: An unusually honest research-lab site that a developer can actually parse in ~15 minutes, though I still don't know what languages anything is written in or how I'd integrate any of it.

### Journey
Landed on home from a repo README. Headline "Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop" plus a clear problem statement — "there is no general way to establish whether [an AI agent] will behave appropriately in a given context" — got me oriented fast. Within two minutes I had the shape: a fleet of machines running 31+ daily autonomous jobs, three main projects (Web4 = trust ontology, SAGE = cognition kernel, Synchronism = physics conjecture), and one externally checkable claim (94.85% on ARC-AGI-3).

/projects was the page I came for. Eleven projects, each with a one-liner, and every acronym I checked was expanded in place — LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, R6, even TPM and FIDO2. What I couldn't find: what language anything is written in, what the deployment target is, or a concrete "here's how you'd use this" example. The quick-starts live on /links (SAGE: clone, `python3 -m sage.instances.init`, build a Rust daemon, dashboard on localhost:8760 — so Python + Rust, but I had to infer that from the commands).

/fleet was the most fun page for a hardware-minded dev: eight named machines from a Jetson Orin Nano running a 0.8B model up to a Jetson AGX, each mapped to a brain-region role. /autonomy told me what actually runs on them, with a caveat section that names real risks (the maintainer track ships to the live site with detect-and-revert only; the publisher track's public-repo pushes are called out as the larger tail risk because clones are irreversible).

One thing made me stop and re-read: home says eight machines, but /autonomy says "31+ autonomous tracks across 6 cognition machines." I eventually reconciled it via /fleet's pool breakdown (6 cognition machines + 2 society-host machines = 8), but the reader has to assemble that themselves.

/raising and /principles are where I hit my ceiling. I followed the "shape context, don't update weights" idea, and I genuinely respect a page that says its own headline result "might be competent context engineering and nothing more." But synthons, coherence thresholds, and pheromone-field metaphors are past what I can evaluate. /context is a real glossary — 50+ terms — and is where I'd send another developer first. /arc-agi-3 is the strongest page for an outsider: exact numbers (94.85%, 24/25, ~$250 API cost), a link to the official scorecard, and an upfront admission that the harness-vs-model ablation hasn't been run.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes — specifically to check whether the named-but-unscheduled controls ever run
- **Overall impression**: The most epistemically careful lab site I've audited this year, but its transparency is doing work that only evidence can actually do — the site names every gap and closes almost none of them.

### Journey
I came in from a conference mention expecting the usual: emergence language, consciousness innuendo, cherry-picked benchmarks. The landing page disarmed most of that in the first screen. "Autonomous" is explicitly disambiguated ("unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense of choosing its own goals"), anthropomorphic verbs are quoted and glossed as "functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness," and there's an explicit evidence-tier hierarchy that rates most of the site's own claims as "internal observation." A landing page that demotes its own claims is rare.

/raising is where sites like this usually lose me, and it half did. The strengths are real: the deflationary alternative is stated in the site's own voice ("everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more"), a scramble control is specified, and — this impressed me — the page then critiques its own control, noting that order sensitivity is a documented property of in-context learning, so a bare "behavior degrades when scrambled" result "adjudicates neither" hypothesis. That's a level of methodological self-awareness most preprints don't reach. The weaknesses are equally real: the consciousness disclaimer lives in the opening paragraph while the Pattern sections run on developmental language ("growth," "trust formation," "becoming") with caveats demoted to parentheses; a skimmer absorbs the framing before meeting the deflation. And the control has "no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric" — a named gap is not a closed one.

/principles showed me the same asymmetry at page scale. Principle 5 (synthon) carries three paragraphs of self-critique — single trial, unreplicated, and an acknowledged definitional circle (synthon defined through coherence, coherence partly through the synthon marker). But Principles 1, 2, and 7 make structurally similar analogical claims — pheromone-field engineering, Hill-function kinetics, attractor basins — with far lighter qualification, and the emergence framing ("when conditions are right") doesn't specify what observation would falsify it. The hedging is concentrated where the authors already know they're exposed, not applied uniformly.

/autonomy is the page I'd cite in a review. The zero-rollback figure (79 sessions, 117 commits, zero reverts) comes pre-undermined by its authors: "the only automated detector is the same daily loop being evaluated, so a zero is equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'" — labeled "weak evidence, not a safety result." The correlated-error problem (LLM sessions auditing LLM output — "their misses are correlated with the authors', not independent of them") is named, which almost nobody does. But heterogeneous review is listed as a future target rather than treated as the design requirement it is, and the Publisher track's irreversibility gap is "unaddressed, not solved" with no stated remediation path.

/context and /arc-agi-3 anchor the good end. The glossary flags its own term collisions (ATP/ADP's biochemistry namesake called "a deliberate metaphor," ontology scoped to the Semantic Web sense, the RL "policy" collision), admits "coherence" has "no single operational definition yet," and marks its circular definitions explicitly. The benchmark page links a third-party scorecard, states the missing harness-vs-model ablation twice, discloses cost, and notes the human-leaderboard comparison is "not a like-for-like result." What the glossary lacks entirely: external references. No trust-ontology prior art, no in-context-learning literature, no AI-safety citations — a self-contained vocabulary is exactly the structure that lets a field talk only to itself.

Is "raising not training" defensible? As a mechanistic distinction, yes — context-shaping without weight updates is a real and checkable difference, and the page states it cleanly ("we shape context — we do not update weights"). What's not yet defensible is the developmental connotation the word carries beyond its mechanism: whether accumulated context produces something a control couldn't, which is precisely the unrun experiment. The term is fine; the freight it carries is unearned until the scramble control (or a better one) runs.

### Verdict
This is the rare site where I trust the authors' epistemics more than their evidence: every objection I formed while reading was already stated somewhere on the site, usually more precisely than I'd have put it. But that's also my concern — the site has become excellent at *naming* its gaps (unrun ablation, unscheduled control, unpublished logs, undefined coherence) and shows no visible mechanism for *closing* them, so transparency is functioning as the product rather than the process. I'd cite /autonomy's self-undermined zero-rollback figure as a model of honest reporting, and I'd want the log samples, one pre-registered control, and a related-work section before I'd cite anything else.
