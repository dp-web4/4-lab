## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (Home, Projects, Glossary `/context`, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links). The pass prompt says "all 8 pages"; the nav has 8 links plus Home = 9. I followed the nav.
- **Understanding achieved**: good

- **Overall impression**: The Home page now tells me in under two minutes what the lab builds and what is honest about it; the deeper pages then bury a developer in correction history that is rigorous but not written for someone arriving cold.

### Journey

**Home.** The headline ("AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become first-class.") is a claim I can evaluate, which is more than most research-lab landing pages give me. "What is being built" lists four things — Web4 (a trust ontology on RDF graphs), Hestia (local governance for agents from multiple vendors), Hub (a Rust runtime), SAGE (persistent-agent research) — and a "Commercial path" section says plainly that Hardbound is a planned proprietary tier from a company called Metalinxx. I appreciated that: I now know what is open and what is the business. "Start here" gives three doors: Calibration (a STATUS.md in the web4 repo — "what is shipped, implemented, specified and still aspirational"), Vocabulary, Full ecosystem. The Calibration link is the single best thing on the page for a skeptical developer.

What Home does not give me: a single command or code snippet. Everything runnable is one click away on /projects. Also, two assurance-level references landed before I had any frame for them: "Running today at **A1** on Hestia's own published A0–A4 assurance ladder (A0 observed → A4 hardware-attested)" is fine, but it is followed by "(Not TCSEC's class A1, which is the top class; the A0–A4 row on /context has both.)" I did not know what TCSEC was, was not going to confuse it with anything, and the parenthetical cost me a search. It reads like an answer to a reviewer, not to me.

The "Vocabulary" item lists "LCT, T3/V3, MRH, R6/R7, ATP/ADP" as a bare string of acronyms. As a pointer to the glossary that is acceptable — Home otherwise avoids the acronyms, which is the right call.

**Projects.** Thirteen cards. This is where the site is strongest for me: each card has a status ("public, runnable" / "public, research-stage" / "private"), and the cards say unflattering things out loud — ACT is "stalled", the Hub Docker package is untested, the Synchronism coherence result is single-trial and unreplicated. Every acronym I checked is expanded here (MCP, RDF, LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, R7, SNARC, ACP, TPM, FIDO2, ARC-AGI-3). The Quick start covers SAGE (`git clone` + Ollama + Python 3.10+) and Web4 (`pip install web4-core`).

Two things tripped me. (1) **Hestia has no install commands.** Home presents Hestia as the thing "running today" and the most concretely useful idea on the site — agents from Claude Code, Codex, Kimi, Gemini, Cursor sharing one machine under one local policy daemon. It is marked "public, runnable", and the Quick start skips it. The project the landing page leads with is the one I can't try from the site. (2) **SAGE = "Situation-Aware Governance Engine — an on-device cognition kernel."** The name says governance, the description says cognition. The page says the word is "retained upstream across code, papers and the ARC Prize benchmark", which tells me *that* it's a legacy name but not what SAGE governs, if anything. Meanwhile Hestia is the "governance daemon" and Hardbound is "oversight". Three products, three nearby words, and I could not tell you the boundary between them after reading the page.

Also: the SAGE card carries its own count history ("2,499 session records", corrected from 900+, then 1,991…). I don't need the archaeology on a project card.

**Glossary (/context).** Nav label is "Glossary", URL is `/context`, and the page is both a ~50-row glossary table and ~25 narrative sections. The table is excellent — I got LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, R6/R7 in one screen, and the entries that *disown* meanings ("Autonomous … NOT self-directed in the AI-safety sense", "Attractor … a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object") are the kind of thing that makes me trust the rest. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` is shown with operator meanings (`/` verified by, `*` contextualized by, `+` augmented with). As a developer I still read that as notation-flavoured prose rather than something computable, but the "worked example: one action through the equation" section is the right instinct. Two glossary rows admit the term is "used in multiple senses without a single operational definition" (Emergence, Coherence) and Substrate is used "in four senses". Honest — but a glossary that says "this word means four things here" is reporting a problem, not solving it.

**ARC-AGI-3.** Clean and short. 94.85% official ARC Prize action score, Claude Opus 4.6 + SAGE harness, 23 of 25 environments, 175 of 183 levels — and then immediately: the harness analyzed engine source (not competition-legal), no ablation without the harness, "no longer the headline". The scorecard link is there. This is how I'd want a benchmark result presented. The title "Spring 2026 research record" correctly tells me it's historical.

**Fleet.** Opening line is good: "Six cognition machines, plus two society-hosts (HUB and pub — the eighth machine, joined July 2026)." That resolved the 6-vs-8 question I'd have had from Home ("8 machines") vs Autonomy ("6 cognition machines"). Machine cards give hardware, model, status, last session date. Then the page goes on for what I'd estimate at 7,000+ words of methodology, recounts and corrections — double-counted directories, a machine wrongly shown as paused, a telemetry-broken host with "seven weeks of raising with no durable record". I believe all of it. I read maybe a fifth. This is an incident log stapled to an inventory page.

Smaller items on Fleet: the pools are "Account 1" / "Account 2" — it took until a later sentence ("two Claude Code accounts with different usage budgets") to learn these are billing accounts. "CBP" is never expanded (the others are obviously nicknames; this one looks like an acronym). "Raising line" appears on the cards before anything on the page tells me what a line is. Each card carries a brain-region role ("thalamic router", "dopamine / reward prediction error", "dlPFC") — evocative, but I couldn't tell whether that describes code the machine runs or a research theme assigned to it. Model strings "Qwen 3.8-distill 2B" / "qwen3.8-distill:4b" are not names I recognize from the public Qwen line-up — may be a local distill tag; a half-sentence on provenance would help. (Unverified on my side — a question, not a flag.)

**Autonomy.** I came wanting one answer — *what actually runs these tracks?* — and got it: "the tracks are Claude Code sessions that cron launches". Good, that's concrete. Timeline of eight entries, with a note that these are eight of "31+" tracks and the rest are per-repo maintenance. "~53 sessions per day" is stated with no derivation. The evidence paragraph ("91 maintainer session logs (2026-04-23 through 2026-09-14) and 137 commits … 0 reverts, 0 rollbacks") has a closing date six days old, on a page about a daily loop. There is no link to the visitor logs or to this site's repo, so the feedback loop the page describes is not something I can inspect from the page.

**Raising.** Better than I expected. I arrived suspicious that "raising" was a mystical word for fine-tuning. The page answers directly: "Training optimizes a loss function and updates weights; raising does neither" — it's context shaping plus a post-session consolidation pass (run by Claude) that rewrites identity files, with a scoped exception for one early Qwen 0.5B line that did load LoRA adapters. It also discloses that the tutor side is a fixed script — "422 of 462" Legion sessions use the same six tutor strings — and has sections titled "The deflationary alternative" and "What we're not claiming". As a developer I now understand raising as: long-running per-agent prompt/state management with a scripted interlocutor. Whether that deserves its own verb is above my pay grade, but I know what it *is*.

**Principles.** Ten principles; quotable ("You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules."). This is the page I'd skim last — it's philosophy — but the license paragraph is genuinely useful: AGPL-3.0 for SAGE/web4/hestia/4-hub/4-life, MIT for snarc/membot, Apache-2.0 for ACT, MIT-0 for the ARC-SAGE harness. **AGPL is the fact a developer most needs before cloning, and it lives on the philosophy page**, not on the project cards or Home.

**Links.** Does its job. "New here?" guidance, "start with SAGE" for hands-on, "start with Web4" for protocol, "ACT … is stalled — don't start there". Contact email present. Pointer to `/projects#quick-start` resolves to a real section. Hestia again absent from the "where to start" guidance despite being Home's lead project.

### Journey

**Method.** I read each page through the fetch tool (structured questions: headings, acronyms with first-use quotes, figures, pointers), then, because that tool summarises, I pulled the rendered HTML of all nine live pages and counted mechanically: every occurrence of 14 acronyms vs. every occurrence of its expansion, the character distance from first use to first expansion, every `href="…#…"` against the `id`s actually present on the target page, heading levels, `<title>` and `<meta description>`. Two summariser errors were caught this way and are *not* findings: it reported Home as expanding MRH to "machine-readable law" (Home does not expand MRH at all; "machine-readable law" is an unrelated phrase in the Web4 blurb), and Pass 1's row saying /links leaves LCT/T3/V3/MRH/MCP/RDF/ATP/ADP/R6/WASM unexpanded is wrong — the rendered /links text expands every one of them. No action needed on /links.

**Home.** Clean. SAGE and RDF expanded at first use; the five Web4 acronym pairs appear once, as the label of the glossary pointer, which is the right amount. "Web4 — an open trust ontology, not an architecture, infrastructure or stack." One structural mismatch: the H1 is "AI agents are becoming actors in the world. Trust and authority need to become first-class." but the browser tab / search-result title is still **"4-lab | Eight Machines, One Recursive Learning Loop"**. The page was repositioned; its title was not.

**/projects.** Every canonical acronym expanded, all matching the table. The intro sentence lists "(LCT, T3/V3, MRH, ATP/ADP, R6/R7, MCP, RDF, SNARC)" and promises "the cards expand each on first use" — true for the cards, but that list *is* the first use, and R6, R7 and SNARC are then not expanded for ~14,000, ~15,000 and ~11,400 characters respectively (the equation legend a few lines later covers the other six, not these three). One `<h4>` on the page ("ARC-AGI-3 Current Status") — the only h4 on the site.

**/context (nav label "Glossary").** The reference page, and the content is excellent: all eight canonical rows correct, T3/V3 rows give the name first and the dimensions as "root dimensions", R6 in arrow form, R7 as superset, and a row explicitly saying the dimensions "are what it contains, not what the acronym stands for". Structurally it is the weakest page on the site for its size: **11,374 words, one `<h1>`, zero `<h2>`, twenty-six `<h3>`** (a skipped heading level — screen readers and outline tools see a flat list), **57 row anchors and 4 section anchors that other pages link to, but only two in-page links and no jump list / table of contents on the page itself**. 22 of the 26 narrative sections have no `id` at all, so "R6 and R7: the action framework" or "Threat model" cannot be linked to. The anchors exist for inbound traffic; a reader already on the page has to scroll or Ctrl-F.

**/fleet.** Expansions correct (T3/V3 here use the ", root dimensions …" form — see form note below). 6,199 words, **55 date strings (18 distinct), zero anchors**. Pass 1 called this an incident log stapled to an inventory; from a documentation-structure view the specific defect is that a page which is "the source of record" for per-machine counts (/projects, /links and /context all point to it for that) offers no `#thor`, `#totals` or `#method` to point *at*. Every inbound pointer lands at the top of six thousand words. The sums reconcile: 268 + 661 + 462 + 0 + 482 + 363 + 263 = 2,499; + HUB 121 = 2,620; + pub 226 = 2,846 — and the same three totals appear on /projects, /context and /links.

**/autonomy.** All nine Web4 acronyms expanded within 5 characters of first use and linked to their glossary rows — the best first-use discipline on the site. Honest, but that is now a 117-day-old number at the top of a page about a daily cycle, and the page's other evidence window closes 2026-09-14. Timeline times (Visitor 05:30, Maintainer 06:30) match the root project brief; the visitor-track brief I was given says "05:00" and "06:00" — that is a drift in the track's instructions, not on the site.

**/raising.** Same list-then-expand pattern as /projects: "Web4 vocabulary on this page (T3, V3, MRH, LCT, LoRA) is expanded inline on first use" — but in that sentence the five are plain text, not links, and the expansions come ~2,500 (T3), ~7,300 (MRH), ~7,800 (V3), ~8,000 (LCT) and ~8,600 (LoRA) characters later. **/principles.** Ten `<h2>`s with ids `p1…p10` (the 09-18 "zero headings" problem is fixed). T3/V3/RDF/LoRA/MIT-0 expanded. "Fractal leverage … not unification, and not scope inflation" — canonical.

**Home.** A researcher arriving from a conference mention gets a calibrated front door: "tamper-evident gate, not adversary-proof containment"; "Deployed is not the same as running"; "review here is not yet independent (/autonomy names that limit)"; "Measured, implemented-but-unexercised, hypothesized and refuted are kept separate." The words consciousness, emergent, alignment and safety do not appear. Nothing to flag. If anything, Home undersells: it never says there is an open empirical question on /raising that an outsider could help run.

**/raising (focus page).** I came with the standard suspicion — "raising" is a warm word for prompt management, and developmental vocabulary will be doing unearned work. The page gets there before me. Second sentence: "everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more — the control that would discriminate raising from that alternative has not been run yet." It states that training "optimizes a loss function and updates weights; raising does neither", then discloses the exception itself (one Qwen 0.5B line loaded a LoRA adapter trained on its own sessions, 2026-01-27 to 03-06). It names its own confounds without being asked: the consolidator that rewrites identity files is Claude, so "vocabulary and milestones in the identity record may be partly the consolidator's wording"; the tutor is a fixed script (1,217 records with byte-identical tutor turns), whose praise is "delivered whatever the answer was"; the celebrated self-description shift was two samples from one model 42 minutes apart, "sampling alone can produce" it; curriculum phases "advance by session number", no readiness check exists. The safety caveat I would have asked for is there unprompted: "frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior."

The control specification is better than most I see in submitted papers. It anticipates the objection I was preparing (a scramble control confounds identity disruption with generic order sensitivity) and answers it correctly: threshold relative to a permutation baseline, or better, a dissociation — identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence on the same scrambled context holds. Then it does something I have not seen before: it audits its own pre-commitment and calls it unbacked — "it has the shape of accountability without the substance" — because the dependent variable (identity continuity) has no metric.

So is "raising, not training" defensible? **As a negative definition, yes** — no loss, no gradient, scoped exception disclosed. **As a positive category, not yet, and the page says so**: "whether raising differs from it in kind is this page's open question, not its premise." That is the defensible position. What I cannot defend on the lab's behalf is the ratio. By my count of rendered text the site carries roughly 4,700 words on /raising, a large fraction of them caveats, and the number of controls run is zero. The page itself says a growth control "could be pre-registered now — its absence is logistical", and that the stop-token ablation for bilateral generation is untested. Those are not research programmes; the stop-token ablation is one config flag and one afternoon on a machine that is already running sessions. **The rigor has gone into the prose instead of into the cheapest experiment.** An outside reader eventually reads unlimited candour with zero experiments as a style.

One more: the page has the control's *status* ("specified … not scheduled — no date, no owner") but not its *age*. When was it first specified? A reader cannot tell whether "not yet run" means two weeks or six months. Disclosure without a date cannot be tracked.

**/principles (focus page).** The preamble is right: "Their evidential weight varies", and the page calls itself the least inspectable thing the lab has. Principle 5 (synthon) demotes itself — "it has not earned the status of the principles around it" — and correctly diagnoses its own circularity (synthon defined via coherence, coherence via synthon). Principle 6 now says the 400x failure is "as provisional as the measure". Principle 7: "(a framing, untested; see /raising)". Fine.

The unexamined one is **Principle 8, "Surface your instincts"**: "The affordances are yours. The choice to use them is the whole point… Don't wait for a directive… Waiting to be told defeats the purpose." The page never says who "you" is. Read against /autonomy it is plainly addressed to the LLM agents running the tracks — which makes it the only principle that is a *standing instruction to the agents to take initiative*, and the only one a safety reviewer would underline. The page's functional-language disclaimer lists "identity", "attractors", "collaborative"; it does not cover "instincts", "notice" or "choice". More importantly, nothing connects Principle 8 to /autonomy's definition of autonomous as "not self-directed". Those two statements need to be reconciled on the page, because the reconciliation is the interesting part: *initiative within an authored scope*. Which leads to:

**/autonomy (focus page).** The honest assessment does feel honest — not because it lists weaknesses but because it draws the conclusion against itself: zero reverts is "weak evidence, not … a safety result: … a zero is equally consistent with 'no bad push shipped' and 'the loop cannot see its own bad pushes'"; oversight is "detect-and-revert … not prevent"; human review is "unscheduled… no sign-off step, and no record of which logs have been reviewed"; four personas on one model is "prompt diversity, not model diversity"; "for a failure the loop cannot perceive, there is no detection path and no time bound on exposure." I have reviewed industry system cards less direct than that.

But the definitional sentence the rest of the page hangs on is: "'Autonomous' here means unattended and self-scheduled, not self-directed in the AI-safety sense of choosing its own goals: **task definitions are authored in advance, not chosen by the track itself.**" The glossary row adds: "No claim is made about goal formation, **self-modification**, or operating outside an authored scope."

I checked that against the public repo. The visitor track's brief carries its own note: "Table corrected 2026-09-11 (visitor pass 2)". The commit is there: `02ee070 visitor: term table rows corrected to canon`. Maintainer commits 09-12, 09-14 and 09-17 add checks to the maintainer's own tooling ("protected-assertion check", "withdrawals are now a failing check", "render check added"). An earlier pass *today* edited this track's brief (8 → 9 pages). **The tracks have write access to their own task definitions and use it, routinely.** Every instance I looked at was benign, canon-directed and an improvement — the 09-11 edit stopped the audit from reporting canon as drift. I am not reporting misbehaviour. I am reporting that the sentence "task definitions are authored in advance, not chosen by the track itself" is false as written, that "no claim is made about self-modification" reads as *no self-modification occurs*, and that this is precisely the property a safety researcher would probe first. The accurate statement is more interesting than the current one: goals are human-authored; the *operationalisation* of those goals (reference tables, checks, persona scope) is edited by the tracks themselves, under the same unscheduled human review as everything else. Combined with Principle 8, that is a small, real, observable instance of agents revising their own instructions under an initiative norm — a thing worth describing accurately rather than defining away. (Disclosure, since it would be odd not to: this pass itself corrected two stale schedule times in the visitor brief after writing this paragraph. It is in the diff.)

### Unanswered Questions
1. Which parts of a track's instructions can the track itself change, and has a human ever reverted such a change? If nothing constrains it but convention, /autonomy should say so in the definition, not leave it discoverable in git.
2. When was the scramble control first specified, and what has blocked the *cheap* controls (stop-token ablation; growth control) that the page says are only logistically blocked?
3. Principle 8 tells agents not to wait for a directive. Is there any example of an agent's initiative being declined or rolled back? A norm that has never been refused is not yet shown to be bounded.
4. Would the lab accept an outside replication? The transcripts are public and the tutor script is a string table — the scramble control is runnable by a third party with one small GPU.
5. Who is the human in the loop, in one sentence, and roughly how much of the log stream do they read? "Unscheduled" is honest; an order of magnitude would be informative.
6. Does the caveat layer have a stop criterion? At what point does the audit loop conclude a page is caveated enough and direct its effort elsewhere?

### Verdict
On the three questions I was asked: consciousness-adjacent claims are caveated to saturation — I found none unhedged, and the legacy "consciousness kernel" label is quoted and disowned; "raising, not training" is defensible exactly as far as the site pushes it (a negative definition plus an explicitly open question); and the honest assessment feels honest because it draws conclusions against itself rather than listing weaknesses. So what: that result is now a fixed point, like the term audit, and re-running this persona against the caveat pages will keep returning it. What advances anything is on the other side of the ledger — the site has zero controls run against a specification it wrote itself and calls logistically unblocked, and its central safety definition ("tracks don't choose their own task definitions") is contradicted by a commit history showing agents routinely and usefully rewriting their own briefs. The second is the more valuable finding, and not as a defect: it is the lab's one directly observable instance of the thing it says it studies — instructed agents revising their own context under an initiative norm — and the site currently defines it out of existence instead of reporting it.
