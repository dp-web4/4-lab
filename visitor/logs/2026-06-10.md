## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (Home, Projects, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links — all of them)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial → good (the Context page is what rescued it)
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Visually confident and clearly real, but the home page throws a wall of brain-metaphor jargon at you before you've earned it — I only "got it" after detouring to /context.

### Journey
I landed from a GitHub README expecting "another AI agent framework" and got something more ambitious. The home headline — *"Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."* — is a great hook. Concrete, countable, makes me want to know what the loop *is*.

Then it immediately got dense. The "six brain components — working memory, thalamic router, cerebellum, episodic memory, reward prediction, metacognition" line is evocative but it's a lot of neuroscience to swallow in the second paragraph. As a developer I parse "thalamic router" as "a dispatcher" and "cerebellum / habit compiler" as "a cache for repeated action sequences" — and honestly those plain-English versions would have hooked me *faster*. The metaphor is doing double duty (impress + explain) and the explain half loses.

The home page has a "Vocabulary primer" and a CTA — *"New here? Start with /context for vocabulary"* — which is the right move. I took it. **/context is the best page on the site.** Every acronym I'd tripped over (LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC, ACP, R6) is defined cleanly with a one-liner. If I were redesigning, I'd push newcomers there *first*, almost as a glossary-gate, because the rest of the site reads completely differently once you have it.

From there I went to /projects (good card layout, clear public/private/runnable tags, real repo links — I could `git clone SAGE` right now), then skimmed Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, and Links. Links explicitly tells me to start with SAGE for a single-machine setup, which is exactly the answer I wanted. Good landing-to-action path *if* you follow the breadcrumbs.

What confused me: see the inconsistencies below. None are fatal, but a developer's eye snags on mismatched numbers immediately — it's the same instinct as spotting an off-by-one.

### Journey

I spent the most time on the one item Pass 1 flagged as DRIFT: the Oversight Plugins card, which the Developer persona quoted as "Web4 **governance** implementations." That's the correct way to handle a legacy identifier you can't rename without breaking downstream forks: keep the old slug, label it as legacy, state the canonical term. I'd hold this up as a model, not a defect. (Pass 1's flag is understandable — a cold reader string-matches "governance" and reacts — but the page already answers the objection.)

The one thing that genuinely fails cross-page consistency is the **machine count**. /fleet states it cleanly and correctly: *"Six cognition machines, plus one society-host"* = seven devices, with HUB explicitly called *"substrate, not cognition."* /home leads with *"Seven machines."* /autonomy says *"31+ autonomous tracks across 6 machines"* and *"Six machines, six supervisors."* Each number is *locally* correct — the seven count includes HUB, the six count is the cognition machines that actually run tracks and supervisors (HUB runs substrate, not tracks). But a reader hopping Home → Autonomy without passing through Fleet sees 7 then 6 with nothing reconciling them. The fix is one clause, not a renumber.

The ARC-AGI-3 metrics, which Pass 1 saw as "three different numbers," are actually two metrics and are now labeled correctly: 94.85% is the *official ARC Prize action score (efficiency-weighted)*, 24/25 (96.0%) is the *game-solve rate*. The /arc-agi-3 page states the distinction explicitly, and /home now carries both figures with their labels. From a documentation standpoint this is fine — the labels disambiguate.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (Home, Raising, Principles, Autonomy, Context, ARC-AGI-3, Fleet, Links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Unusually disciplined caveating — the consciousness disclaimers are some of the most careful I've seen on a site like this — but the headline rhetoric occasionally outruns the disclaimers, and one weight-update claim ("raising is not training") sits uncomfortably next to LoRA appearing in the same vocabulary.

### Journey
I came in the way a trust/safety researcher does after a conference mention: skeptical of any AI project that gestures at "identity," "becoming," or "brains," because that vocabulary is usually doing rhetorical work the evidence can't support. I read Principles, Raising, and Autonomy closely and treated every developmental noun as a claim to be cashed out.

**The caveating is the strongest thing here, and it's strong.** The home page front-loads the disclaimer: *"Developmental and lifecycle terms below (identity, behavioral continuity) are functional descriptions of observed system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims."* The Raising page says it plainly: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* And — this is the part that earned my respect — it records the observer's pull *and* fences it: an observer note *"I notice I want to call it experience"* is explicitly labeled *"the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim."* That is exactly the right epistemic move. Most projects either suppress the pull (and feel sterile) or indulge it (and overclaim). Recording it as data about the observer, not the system, is sophisticated. The /context page does the same for the neuroscience: brain-analog terms are *"functional analogies, not claims about neural correspondence."*

**"Raising is not training" is mostly defensible — with one snag.** The mechanistic line is clean: *"Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens,"* and Principle 7's *"It is not training; it is also not nothing"* is an honest refusal to over- or under-claim. The distinction rests entirely on *"shape context… do not update weights."* But **LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) appears in the acronym list on both /raising and /principles** — and LoRA *is* a weight-update method. The page doesn't reconcile this. Either LoRA is used somewhere in the pipeline (in which case "we do not update weights" needs a sharper boundary — *which* parts are frozen-context and which involve adapters), or LoRA is mentioned only as the technique they're contrasting *against* (in which case say so). As written, a careful reader hits a load-bearing claim and an apparently contradicting term on the same page. This is the single thing I'd most want clarified, and it's safety-relevant: the whole "raising ≠ training" ethical framing depends on it.

**On the safety surface, they don't hide the hard part.** The Raising page notes *"frozen weights don't guarantee safe behavior — emergent patterns can arise contextually."* That's the correct and uncomfortable point — in-context selection pressure is its own risk surface, and they name it rather than treating frozen parameters as a safety guarantee. Principle 3 carries an unusually specific deployment disclaimer: *"not suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings."* For a project that elsewhere talks about "becoming," that restraint is credibility-positive.

**Preliminary claims are flagged as preliminary.** The synthon coupling result is marked *"Preliminary (1 trial, not replicated): ~1% sparse coupling → ~35% coherence gain"* — n=1, stated as n=1. The p_crit derivation *"failed 400x,"* surfaced as signal per Principle 6. The ARC-AGI-3 page is honest in the way that matters to me: it separates the **harness** from the **base model** (*"the base model contributes inference"*), discloses *network access* and *~$250* spend, and states Kaggle is *"Not attempted (requires Kaggle sandbox deployment)"* with the harder competition conditions spelled out (32GB VRAM, no internet, private game set). A project inclined to overclaim would have let "94.85%" stand alone; this one fences it.

**Where rhetoric outruns evidence.** Two places. (1) The headline register — *"The fleet built a brain,"* the six brain-components, *"BECOMING"* — is doing evocative work that the functional disclaimers then walk back. The disclaimers are correct, but a skimming reader meets the metaphor first and the fence second; the order favors impression over precision. (2) *"Identity persists across models"* is presented on the home page as **demonstrated** (SAGE-Sprout maintaining "behavioral identity" across Qwen 0.5B → TinyLlama 1.1B) without an operational metric for "behavioral identity" traveling alongside it. The functional-description caveat covers it in principle, but the *measurement* doesn't appear where the claim does. As a researcher I can't evaluate "identity persisted" without knowing what was held constant and how it was scored — and it reads as n=1.

**The structural limit, which the site admits.** Most of the operational claims (31+ tracks, ~53 sessions/day, autonomous maintenance) are unverifiable from outside — and the Autonomy page effectively concedes this, noting the limits of what the personas catch and that Hardbound code is closed to scrutiny. That's honest, but it means a researcher takes the autonomy claims substantially on trust. The closed Hardbound suite is the one place where "oversight" can't itself be inspected.

### Verdict
This is one of the more epistemically careful AI sites I've audited: the consciousness disclaimers are precise, the preliminary results are honestly marked n=1, the ARC-AGI-3 claims separate harness from model and concede Kaggle was never run, and the deployment disclaimer is specific rather than boilerplate. The "raising not training" framing is defensible *in principle* but has one unresolved snag — LoRA in the vocabulary against a "no weight updates" claim — that a safety researcher will fixate on, because the ethics ride on that exact line. My honest read: the *content* is more careful than the *packaging*, which leads with brain/becoming rhetoric the disclaimers then have to walk back. Tighten the LoRA boundary and let one disclaimer ride up next to the metaphor, and this clears the credibility bar for a skeptical outsider — which most sites in this genre do not.
