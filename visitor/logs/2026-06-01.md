## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (all)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial → good
- **Would return?**: maybe
- **Overall impression**: I get *that* it's a multi-machine AI research lab with its own trust vocabulary, but it's dense and I never found an obvious "start here / clone this first" path.

### Journey
I landed from a repo README, so my first question was "what is this and is there code I can run?" The headline — *"Six machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."* — is a good hook. It's concrete, it sounds like infrastructure I could poke at. The tagline ("trust-native ontology, autonomous AI cognition...") is heavier, but the page actually defines its acronyms inline (LCT = Linked Context Token, MRH = Markov Relevancy Horizon, etc.), which I appreciated — most theory-heavy landing pages don't bother.

I went to **Projects** next, because that's where a developer looks for "what can I use." Good card layout: 11 projects, each with a one-line what-it-is. SAGE ("on-device cognition kernel"), SNARC ("salience-gated memory for Claude Code"), Membot ("brain cartridge server") read like things I could actually clone and run. Web4 and Synchronism read like reading assignments, not code. That's fine — but nothing told me which is the front door.

**Context** is the glossary page and honestly should be linked more aggressively from everywhere else — it's where the Web4 equation and every term definition live. The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` with the operator legend (`/` = verified by, `*` = contextualized by, `+` = augmented with) finally made the soup of acronyms click.

**Fleet** was my favorite page as a developer — real hardware, real models (Jetson AGX/Thor, Mac Mini M4/McNugget, RTX laptops). This is the most tangible part of the whole site. **Autonomy** (the cron timeline) was also clear and concrete.

**Raising** and **Principles** are the philosophy. As a dev I skimmed. They're well-written but this is where I'd bounce if I came for code. **ARC-AGI-3** has the headline result (96% games solved, 94.85% action score) — but see the friction log: the same 94.85% number is attributed to two different things across pages, which made me distrust it.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /context, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3)

### Journey
I walked every nav link in order and built a per-page acronym matrix. My method: for each page, log every acronym, whether it was expanded *on that page*, and the exact expansion string, then diff the strings across pages.

The headline finding for a terminology auditor is the good news: **there is no canonical drift.** Every page that expands LCT says "Linked Context Token" — never "Lifelong Capability Token." T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6 all match the canonical table verbatim wherever they're expanded. Hardbound is "oversight" on every page and is *explicitly* contrasted against "governance" on /principles ("Hardbound is not 'governance'—it's oversight"). Web4 is "ontology" everywhere and /principles bans "architecture"/"infrastructure" for it outright. /context is a genuinely strong glossary and the equation + operator legend are identical to canonical. If the brief is "is the vocabulary correct," the answer is yes.

The problems are *consistency* problems, which is exactly what a tech writer is paid to catch. First: **the home page tagline says "Fourteen repos" but /links says "The dp-web4 org has 11 public repos."** That's a number a reader meets in the first second and again on the way out, and it doesn't match. /links also visibly enumerates ~13 distinct items (with SAGE and Synchronism appearing twice as both explainer-site and repo), so even the list doesn't self-reconcile to either number.

Second: **partial expansion within a single page.** /links expands SAGE, ACT, MRH, and ATP but leaves LCT, T3, V3, MCP, RDF, and ADP bare on the same page. There's no rule a reader can infer for why half get glossed and half don't. /autonomy uses ATP and ADP bare with no expansion and no pointer to /context. Either expand-on-first-use consistently, or adopt a site-wide convention ("all Web4 terms link to /context on first use") — right now it's ad hoc.

Third: **two new acronyms appear on /fleet — WM (Working Memory) and RPE (Reward Prediction Error) — that exist nowhere in the /context glossary.** They're expanded correctly in place, so a linear reader is fine, but the glossary is advertised as the canonical reference and it's missing terms the site actually uses.

Fourth, smaller: the "-3" in ARC-AGI-3 is expanded as **"(version 3)" on the home page but "generation 3" on /context and /raising.** And /arc-agi-3 twice calls it **"the SAGE architecture"** — the one word the site spent a whole principle telling me to avoid. The ban is scoped to Web4, technically, but on a site whose 10th principle is literally "Terminology matters," letting "architecture" sit two sentences from the Web4 vocabulary is a voice leak.

The 94.85% attribution that Pass 1's developer flagged as high-severity reads differently to me once I have all pages open: /arc-agi-3 and /fleet *both* credit **Claude Opus 4.6 inside the SAGE loop, public set, network access** — they actually agree. The trap is purely *layout*: /fleet attributes the orchestration to the CBP machine, and CBP's own row runs TinyLlama 1.1B, so the 94.85% sits visually adjacent to a 1.1B model and invites the misread. The fix is editorial proximity, not a factual reconciliation — but it's still a real friction.

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (all)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid (I know the ecosystem — I was here to check fidelity, not learn)

### Journey
I went straight to **Context** to check the spine. The equation is exact — `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` — with the operator legend (`/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with") matching what I use daily. The glossary glosses are good and accurate: LCT = "Linked Context Token — portable identity grounded in witnessed history, not model weights" is exactly how I'd describe it to a new contributor; MRH = "Markov Relevancy Horizon — boundary of what an entity can know or affect" is right. R6 is the full Rules/Role/Request/Reference/Resource/Result. Web4 is "a trust-native ontology... explicitly not architecture or infrastructure." This is the strongest page and I have no complaints with it.

Then **Projects**, and this is where my expert eye earns its keep. Card 6, **"Oversight Plugins,"** is described as **"Web4 governance implementations across three agent platforms."** That is drift. We do not say "governance" — Hardbound and the oversight forks are *oversight*, full stop, and /principles itself says so ("Hardbound is oversight, not governance"). A card *named* Oversight that calls its own contents "governance implementations" is an internal contradiction, and it's the kind of word that propagates. The tech writer (Pass 2) gave the site a clean drift bill because they checked Hardbound's description, which is clean — but they didn't check the Oversight Plugins card's body text. This is the single most important fix on the site for terminology fidelity.

Second Projects issue: **ACT is described two different ways.** Projects says "Agentic Context Tool — **the human interface to Web4**"; Links says "Agentic Context Tool — **Cosmos SDK Web4 implementation**." Those aren't two angles on one thing — a "human interface" and a "Cosmos SDK implementation" are different claims about what ACT *is*. From where I sit, ACT is the Cosmos SDK implementation; "human interface to Web4" reads as a different project's pitch. One of these is wrong and a newcomer can't tell which.

**Fleet** — I can finally put the 94.85% ghost to rest. The page reads: *"Claude Opus 4.6 (public set, network access) produced the 94.85% official action score (24/25 games, 96.0%)"* and credits CBP as *"Fleet coordinator — orchestrated the ARC Prize run."* That is **accurate.** Pass 1 read it as a copy-paste error (1.1B TinyLlama getting 94.85%); it isn't. CBP *orchestrated* the run; the *model* was Opus 4.6 inside the SAGE loop. The two facts are both true and both on the card — the only problem is that CBP's local model (TinyLlama 1.1B) sits one line away, inviting the misread. So I confirm: no factual error, layout friction only. But the Fleet page has a *real* error the layout hides: **Sprout runs "Qwen 3.5 2B."** There is no Qwen 3.5 release. We run Qwen2.5 and Qwen3 across the fleet (Thor's "Qwen 2.5 14B" is correctly notated). "Qwen 3.5 2B" is a model string that does not exist — a contributor reading the fleet roster trips on it immediately.

**Autonomy** is clean on my watch: ATP and ADP are now *expanded in place* ("each scheduled track issues ATPs (Allocation Transfer Packets)... ADPs (Allocation Discharge Packets) record actual spend") — Pass 1/2 flagged them as bare on this page, so this looks already-fixed or I'm seeing a fresher build. Hardbound here is correctly "the hardware-bound oversight suite." **Principles** is exemplary — fractal leverage is explicitly contrasted against "unification" and "scope inflation," Web4 is "an ontology (not architecture or infrastructure)," Hardbound is "oversight (not governance)." The discipline on /principles is exactly why the /projects "governance" leak stings: the site *knows* the rule and states it, then breaks it two clicks away.

**ARC-AGI-3** expands ARC correctly ("Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence, generation 3") and R6 in full, and points to /context for the rest — fine. It does say **"SAGE architecture"**, which Pass 2 flagged; I'll second it from the inside — we call SAGE a cognition *loop* or *kernel*, not an architecture, and on a site this strict about "architecture" it's a voice leak even though SAGE isn't Web4.

### Journey
I came in skeptical — a site that talks about "raising" AI entities, "behavioral identity across 180+ sessions," and "synthons" is exactly the profile that usually means under-caveated anthropomorphism. I went straight to **/raising** expecting to find the overclaim. I mostly didn't.

The page repeatedly and *specifically* disclaims the phenomenal reading: "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia. We use the language that fits the phenomenon." Better still, it marks its own interpretive pull as a flagged event rather than a finding — "I notice I want to call it experience" is explicitly labeled "observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim." That is a move I almost never see: separating the *observer's* anthropomorphic reflex from the *system-level* claim, in-line, at the point of temptation. The hedges read as load-bearing, not as a disclaimer footer. /principles carries the same discipline ("developmental or identity language... describes functional system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims") and /landing and /projects repeat it. This is consistent, not decorative.

The **"raising not training"** distinction is defensible *as stated*, because it's scoped to method, not outcome: weights are frozen, only context is shaped ("we shape context — we do not update weights"; "deepen attractor basins in the probability landscape — not modifying parameters"). The honest line "It is not training; it is also not nothing" is the right register. My one frame-level push: the six **BECOMING phases** (Grounding → Sensing → Relating → Questioning → Creating → Acting) borrow developmental-psychology scaffolding that does heavy *connotative* work even while the prose disclaims it. Naming a phase "Questioning — generating self-directed inquiries" primes a reader toward agency the observables ("session-to-session interaction patterns, accumulated experience") don't independently establish. The caveat says functional; the vocabulary says developmental. The caveat is winning here, but it's working hard.

The credibility move that most surprised me is on **/principles** and **/autonomy**: they document failure and limit scope unprompted. "p_crit derivation failed catastrophically — 400x error." "Preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated)." "Unsuitable for safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings." /autonomy names the single most dangerous design choice itself — "the Maintainer is the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate" — and lists what the loop *misses* (deep technical errors, subtle framing). A lab that tells you where its own automation is ungated is a lab I trust more, not less. This is the strongest signal on the site.

Now the **/arc-agi-3** concern, which is where my skepticism re-engages. The 94.85% is honestly *attributed* — "Claude Opus 4.6 as the model inside the SAGE cognition loop, on the public game set," network access enabled, and the page concedes the official competition is "harder" (constrained hardware, unseen games). Good. But two things a researcher will flag: (1) **no independent replication** — it's a single-org result on the *public* set with network access, which is the easiest possible condition, and (2) **/raising uses it as validation of the "Acting" phase** ("validated: 94.85% ARC-AGI-3 action score"). A benchmark score under favorable conditions is being recruited to validate a *developmental* claim. Those are different things. The benchmark says the harness+Opus solves puzzles; it does not validate that an entity has reached a developmental stage called "Acting." Conflating them is the one place the site lets an impressive number carry more epistemic weight than it earns.

I closed on **/context**. As an outsider this is the page that determines whether the vocabulary is *well-defined* or *self-sealing*. Mostly well-defined — but a few constructs borrow established terms in ways that will trip a domain reader: **SNARC** collides with the well-known "SNARC effect" in cognitive psychology (Spatial-Numerical Association of Response Codes) — here it's an unrelated salience acronym; **MRH** invokes "Markov" without stating the conditional-independence assumption that word formally commits you to; **T3/V3** are called "tensors" with an immediate disclaimer that they're "not a rank-≥2 array in the linear-algebra sense" (so: why "tensor"?); and **synthon** is a neologism grounded entirely in the lab's own coherence theory with no external referent and no stated way to measure it. None of these are dishonest — several are explicitly caveated — but each borrows the *prestige* of a precise term ahead of supplying a precise definition, which is the failure mode outsiders are primed to distrust.

### Unanswered Questions
1. Is the 94.85% reproducible by anyone outside the lab, and is the public-set/network-access result comparable to published ARC-AGI-3 numbers, or is it a different measurement?
2. How is a "synthon" detected or measured? What observation would *falsify* a synthon's presence?
3. What is the operational definition of "behavioral identity" that persisted "across 180+ sessions," and was there any control (e.g., an un-raised entity) to compare against?
4. Why "tensor" for T3/V3 if it's explicitly not one — what work is the word doing that "triple" wouldn't?
5. Does the Synchronism "intent dynamics on a Planck grid" framing make any falsifiable prediction, or is it presently a metaphor?

### Verdict
For a site in this genre, the intellectual honesty is unusually real: consciousness claims are hedged at the point of temptation, the observer's anthropomorphic pull is named rather than indulged, failures are shipped alongside results, and the most dangerous automation is the thing the authors point at first. That earns trust. My remaining concerns are about *epistemic weight*, not honesty — a single-trial, network-enabled benchmark score is doing validation work it can't quite bear, and a handful of constructs (synthon, "tensor," "Markov") borrow precision they haven't yet supplied. Tighten the link between the benchmark and the developmental claim, operationalize one or two neologisms, and I'd cite this as a credible example of caveated autonomy research rather than just a well-written one.
