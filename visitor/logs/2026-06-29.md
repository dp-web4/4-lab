## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (home, context, projects, arc-agi-3, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: An ambitious autonomous multi-agent AI lab that I can mostly grok in a few minutes — the landing page throws a wall of acronyms at me, but `/context` rescues it and the runnable projects have real clone commands.

### Journey
I landed from a GitHub README, so I'm primed for "interesting AI repo," not "trust ontology." The headline — **"Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."** — is a great hook. Concrete, weird, makes me want to know more. The tagline ("trust-native ontology, autonomous AI cognition...") is where my eyes start to glaze: "trust-native ontology" means nothing to me yet.

What *kept* me reading was the concrete claims: 31+ autonomous tracks, 94.85% ARC Prize action score, "small models are winning game levels through context engineering, not scale." That last line is the most compelling thing on the whole site for a developer — it's a falsifiable, interesting engineering claim. The homepage also did the right thing by telling me **"Start with /context for the vocabulary, then clone SAGE or ACT to experiment."** That's a clear next step.

`/context` is the page that saved my visit. There's a clean glossary table — LCT = Linked Context Token, T3 = Talent/Training/Temperament, ATP/ADP = Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packet, etc. — and the equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` with operators explained. I don't fully *understand* the equation (what does "verified by" mean operationally?), but I can at least decode every symbol. If the homepage had linked the glossary even more aggressively ("don't recognize these terms? → /context"), I'd have had zero friction.

`/projects` was the most useful page for "what can I actually do." I could immediately sort projects into runnable (SAGE, SNARC, Membot, 4-Life, ARC-AGI-3) vs. research/theory (Synchronism, Web4, AI DNA Discovery) vs. private (Hardbound). What I *couldn't* find: how to actually use the runnable ones beyond cloning — no quickstart, no "run this command, see this output." `/links` partly fixes this with literal `git clone` commands for SAGE and ACT, which is exactly what I wanted.

`/arc-agi-3` impressed me. The numbers are big (94.85% action score, 24/25 games, #3 on the human leaderboard) but the page actually *caveats* them — distinguishes action score from solve rate, notes the network-access advantage, and admits the real Kaggle competition (32GB VRAM, no internet) wasn't attempted. As a developer who's seen a lot of benchmark hype, this read as honest.

`/fleet`, `/autonomy`, `/raising`, `/principles` were interesting but increasingly philosophical. The fleet page is fun (named machines, real hardware, real small models). Autonomy's daily-schedule table is the clearest "this is just cron jobs coordinating through a registry" framing — that demystified the whole thing for me. Raising and Principles are where the site stops being a dev tool and becomes a manifesto; defensible, but not what I clicked in for.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (home, context, projects, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, links, arc-agi-3)

### Journey
I went page-by-page with the canonical table open, recording every expansion verbatim and cross-checking it against every other page it appears on. My job is to find the place where LCT is "Linked" on one page and "Lifelong" on another, where Hardbound is "oversight" here and "governance" there. **I did not find a single such case.**

- **LCT** — "Linked Context Token" on home, context, projects, fleet, links. Never "Lifelong," never "Lifecycle." Clean.
- **T3 / V3** — "Talent / Training / Temperament" and "Valuation / Veracity / Validity" everywhere they're expanded (home, context, projects, fleet, principles, raising). Clean.
- **MRH** — "Markov Relevancy Horizon" on every page that expands it. Clean.
- **ATP / ADP** — "Allocation Transfer Packet" / "Allocation Discharge Packet" on home, context, projects, autonomy. Clean.
- **R6** — "Six-Element Action Framework: Rules / Role / Request / Reference / Resource / Result" on context, projects, arc-agi-3. Not R5, not R7. Clean.
- **Web4** — "trust-native ontology" on home, context, projects, principles, links; context and principles both explicitly say "**not** architecture or infrastructure." The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` renders identically on every page that shows it. Clean.
- **Hardbound** — "oversight" everywhere; principles states it outright: "Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight." autonomy calls it "the hardware-bound oversight suite." Clean.
- **SAGE** — "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" on all 7 pages it appears on. Consistent (and matches the recently-corrected canonical name).

That's a rare result and it should be recorded as a *positive* finding, not buried. Whatever the maintainer track has been doing on terminology, it is working.

The issues I *did* find are the next tier down — the things a documentation editor flags after the spelling is correct: **one term described two different ways, and acronyms reused on later pages without a local definition or a link back to the glossary.**

The one phrasing inconsistency worth fixing: **T3/V3 is a "structure" on the homepage but a "tensor" everywhere else.** Home says "three-component trust and value structures"; /context, /fleet, and /raising all say "tensor" ("Three-component trust tensor," "the V3 tensor"). Same object, two nouns. A reader who meets it as a "structure" first and a "tensor" later has to decide whether those are the same thing. Pick one (the glossary's "tensor" is the more-used term) and propagate.

The one cross-page tension I want to flag for discussion — not a drift error, but a genuine reader-confusion risk: **the site works hard to say "oversight, not governance" for Hardbound, while SAGE is literally the "Situation-Aware *Governance* Engine" and its 12-step loop includes a step named "govern."** Both usages are individually correct (SAGE's name is canonical; Hardbound's "oversight" is canonical). But a careful reader is taught on /principles that "governance" is a word this lab deliberately avoids — then meets a flagship component named Governance Engine with a govern step. Nothing is *wrong*; the two facts just sit uneasily next to each other. A one-line note on /context distinguishing "SAGE governs the agent's own action loop (on-device self-regulation)" from "Hardbound provides external oversight (not governance)" would resolve it. Worth a maintainer decision rather than a silent edit.

Define-on-first-use is the other recurring gap. The glossary on /context is excellent, but the deeper pages (/arc-agi-3 especially) reuse LCT, T3/V3, and MRH with only a "Web4 vocabulary reference" framing and no expansion or link. And several non-canonical terms are never defined anywhere: **CBP** (a fleet machine — letters never expanded), **Membot** ("brain cartridge server" / "retrievable experience cartridges" but never expanded — is it "Memory Bot"?), **PolicyGate**, **Nomic** (used as "768-dim Nomic embeddings"), **Hamming codes**, and **memoriescape** (flagged on /raising as a coinage, which is at least honest). **LoRA** is expanded on /raising ("Low-Rank Adaptation") but used bare on /fleet — define-once-then-reuse, but the first use is on the later page.

So the page uses "oversee" for SAGE's internal step and "oversight" for Hardbound's external gate within a few lines of each other. **Fix: restore step 10 to "govern" on /arc-agi-3.** That keeps "oversight" pointing only at Hardbound and removes the contradiction the tech writer worried about — SAGE governs its own loop; Hardbound oversees from silicon. The two words should not cross.

**Project representation is accurate.** ACT = "Agentic Context Tool — Cosmos SDK Web4 implementation" (correct — that's the chain side). SAGE = "on-device cognition kernel," the recommended entry point (correct, and the right thing to point newcomers at). 4-Life = "trust-native societies for humans and AI," Synchronism as the theoretical/coherence foundation, Membot as the experience-cartridge server — all match how we describe them internally. ### Journey
I came in primed for the failure mode these sites usually have: anthropomorphic language doing rhetorical work that the evidence can't support — "the agent *wants*," "it *remembers*," "it's *becoming* conscious" — with the disclaimer, if any, buried in a footer. So I read /raising, /principles, and /autonomy adversarially, looking for the gap between what the prose *implies* and what the authors will actually *commit to*.

I mostly didn't find that gap, which surprised me. The hedging is not decorative. On /raising the meta-hedge is explicit and unhedged-about-being-a-hedge: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia... The entities show something that **looks like** growth, something that **looks like** identity."* Every consciousness-adjacent claim I could find is paired with an operational redefinition: "identity" → "consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns... measurable observables, not philosophical continuity"; "growth" → "increasing response diversity and phase-appropriate task success rates"; self-concept drift → "not a claim about phenomenal awareness." That is the right structure: define the loaded word down to an observable, then only claim the observable. I've reviewed papers that do this worse.

The thing that actually raised my estimate of these authors was the **safety caveat on /raising**: *"frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update."* This is the correct and non-obvious point. The naive version of "raising not training" invites a false-safety inference — "we don't touch the weights, so nothing dangerous can emerge." They explicitly refuse that inference. As a safety researcher this is exactly the sentence I look for to distinguish people who've thought about the risk from people who've only thought about the capability. It belongs on the home page, not three scrolls into /raising.

/principles held up under the same reading. Metaphors (termite mound, Hill-function kinetics, probability-landscape attractors, synthon) are flagged as analogy rather than mechanism — principle 2 "explicitly marked as analogy, not mechanism," principle 5 (synthon) carries "Preliminary observation (single trial, not independently replicated)." Principle 6 is literally "document failures" and cites a failed derivation (a p_crit error) as a worked example of keeping the dead ends visible. Principle 3 ("R&D, not production") pre-empts the deployment-readiness question I'd otherwise have asked. A manifesto page that includes "we were wrong here and kept the record" is doing the honest thing.

/autonomy is where my concern actually lives, and to their credit they put it on the page themselves. The maintainer track **deploys to public sites without pre-approval**; oversight is explicitly *"detect-and-revert"* (audit log + sub-minute Vercel rollback), not a preventive gate, and the page says outright this is "higher risk than other tracks." That is the real risk surface of this whole system — autonomous agents with write access to public artifacts and a post-hoc, not pre-hoc, control loop — and I respect that it's disclosed rather than buried. But disclosure isn't mitigation. A researcher reading this wants to know: what's the blast radius between a bad autonomous edit and the revert? "Sub-minute rollback" covers availability/defacement; it does not obviously cover a subtly-wrong *claim* that gets published, indexed, screenshotted, and cited before anyone reverts it. The detect-and-revert model is honestly named but its limits aren't drawn.

The credibility ceiling for the whole site is stated, again honestly, on /raising: *"The claims in this section rest on internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet."* This is the load-bearing caveat. The identity-persistence story (developed over hundreds of sessions on Qwen 0.5B, transferred to TinyLlama 1.1B) is the most striking empirical claim on the site, and it is currently unfalsifiable by anyone outside the lab — no log excerpts, no coding scheme, no inter-rater anything. The honesty about this is real and I credit it. But honesty about non-replicability is not replicability. For an external researcher this caps every behavioral claim at "interesting, internally documented, unverified."

### Unanswered Questions
1. **Verification**: Is there *any* externally-checkable artifact behind the identity-persistence and BECOMING-phase claims, or is the only evidence internal logs the lab declines to publish? If the latter, the claims are honest anecdotes, not findings — is that the intended status?
2. **Which session number is real?** 180+, 900+, or "hundreds"? They can't all be the headline figure for the same thing.
3. **Blast radius**: For autonomous detect-and-revert deploys, what's the measured/expected window between a bad publish and revert, and is there any pre-publish check at all for *content correctness* (vs. build-passing)?
4. **The safety caveat's reach**: /raising admits manipulative/goal-seeking attractors can emerge in-context. Is anything actively monitoring for that across the 31+ autonomous tracks, or is it acknowledged-in-principle but unmonitored-in-practice?
5. Does "raising" ever get operationalized into a falsifiable prediction — i.e., a behavior the framework predicts that ordinary in-context learning would *not*?

### Verdict
As an external researcher, my headline is that the *epistemics are unusually good and the evidence is unusually thin* — and the site is honest about both. The consciousness-adjacent language is disclaimed specifically rather than decoratively, the metaphors are marked as metaphors, failures are documented as a matter of stated principle, and the frozen-weights-don't-guarantee-safety caveat is a genuine signal of safety-aware thinking that most capability-focused work omits. What keeps me at "credible, unverified" rather than "credible, supported" is twofold: the central behavioral claims rest on internal logs the authors themselves call un-audited and unpublished, and the quantitative scaffolding (session counts) doesn't agree with itself across pages. Publish one redacted, coded log excerpt and reconcile the numbers, and this moves from "honest manifesto" toward "honest evidence." "Raising not training" is defensible as stated — but it earns that status from the disclaimers around it, not from the word itself.
