## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (all)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial → good
- **Would return?**: maybe (yes if I were already curious about distributed/agent systems)
- **Overall impression**: Serious, transparent AI research with real infrastructure behind it — but the front door drops you into a deep vocabulary with no shallow end.

### Journey

I landed from a repo README expecting a project homepage. The hero hit me with **"Six machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."** — punchy, and I immediately got that this is a distributed system, not a single app. Good. The pitch that follows is genuinely interesting to me as a dev: instead of "bigger model / cheaper inference," they run *different* models on *different* hardware and watch them self-organize. That's a hook I'd keep reading.

Then the acronyms started. SAGE, Web4, LCT, SNARC, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, ARC-AGI-3 — all in the first screen or two. The landing page does define most of them inline (SAGE = "Situation-Aware Guidance Engine," SNARC = Surprise/Novelty/Arousal/Reward/Conflict), and there's a "Vocabulary primer" + a link to `/context`, so I didn't feel totally stranded. But the density is high. I read the home page twice before I felt oriented.

**Projects** was where my "okay, how do I engage with this?" instinct hit a wall. Twelve projects, each with a one-liner and (mostly) a repo link. The descriptions are intriguing but abstract — I can tell *what each project is about* but not *what it does on day one* or *how I'd contribute*. No setup steps, no "good first issue," no use-case scenario. As a developer scanning for an entry point, I bounced.

**Context** is the glossary, and it's the best page for someone like me — clean acronym expansions, analogies (dopamine circuits for reward prediction). But it told me to go read Principles and Projects to make sense of it, and those told me to read Context. Bit of a circular dependency for a newcomer.

**Fleet** was actually fun — six named machines with hardware + model + a brain-system analogy. "The model is weather. The identity is organism." is a great line. One snag: I know AI basics, and **"Qwen 3.5 2B"** (Sprout) doesn't match any Qwen release I recognize (Qwen 2.5 and Qwen 3 exist; 3.5 doesn't). Minor, but it's the kind of thing a dev notices and it dents credibility slightly.

**Autonomy** was the most legible page to me — cron jobs, declared scope, a SQLite "fleet track registry" to prevent file-level merge conflicts. That's straightforward infra reasoning I can follow. The detail that the maintainer track "ships changes to the public site without a human gate" is buried under a mild "higher blast radius" — as a dev that's the scariest sentence on the site and it's underplayed.

**Raising** and **Principles** are where I, the pragmatic developer, started skimming. The ideas translate (raising ≈ in-context shaping at scale; substrate-not-structure ≈ emergence-first design), but the developmental-psychology and physics vocabulary ("metabolic awareness," "synthon," "coupling density," "attractors") is past where I can operationalize anything.

**ARC-AGI-3** has the most concrete, checkable claims (24/25 games solved, 94.85% action score, #3 on a human leaderboard with Opus 4.6). That impressed me — but the two headline numbers (96% solve vs 94.85% action score) measure different things and the page doesn't reconcile them clearly, so I wasn't sure which to anchor on.

**Links** finally gave me the "get involved" answer I wanted on Projects: "Open an issue on any public repo," SAGE and Web4 as "the most active entry points," and a contact email. This should be discoverable two clicks earlier.

### Journey
I walked every nav link in order (Home → Projects → Fleet → Autonomy → Raising → Principles → Context → ARC-AGI-3 → Links) and built an expansion table per page so I could diff them. My method: extract each acronym's inline expansion verbatim and compare it (a) against the canonical table in my brief and (b) against the same acronym's expansion on every other page.

The headline result is reassuring: **every defined expansion matches canonical, and matches itself across pages.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on Home, Projects, Fleet, Context, and Raising — never "Lifelong." SAGE is "Situation-Aware Guidance Engine" on all six pages it appears. ATP/ADP are "Allocation Transfer/Discharge Packet" everywhere. R6 is the full six-element list on both Projects and Context. SNARC, MRH, T3, V3 — all consistent. The site even *self-polices* the two trickiest distinctions: Principles explicitly states "Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight" and "Web4 is not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure'." That's the exact language a terminology guardian wants to see. Whoever maintains this is clearly running a drift check already.

So my friction is not "wrong words." It's three smaller editorial categories:

**1. Definition placement (the dominant issue).** The glossary on /context is the single source of truth, and Home defines most terms inline too. But the *deeper* pages assume you've already been to /context. /raising uses LCT, V3, and MRH with no inline expansion and no anchor link. /principles uses T3 and V3 but not the others it leans on. /arc-agi-3 invokes MRH "in the Web4 composition" without defining it. A reader who lands deep (e.g. from a paper citation straight to /arc-agi-3) hits undefined acronyms. The fix is cheap: link first-use of each acronym to its /context anchor.

**2. Number/phrasing agreement.** Minor but the kind of thing my job exists to catch:
   - ATP/ADP are singular ("Allocation Transfer Packet") on Home/Projects/Context but **plural** ("Allocation Transfer Packets") on /autonomy. Pick one form for the canonical noun.
   - Web4 is "a trust-native ontology" on Home/Context/Links but "Trust-native ontology **framework**" on /projects. "Framework" subtly contradicts the site's own insistence that Web4 is an ontology, not architecture/infrastructure. Drop "framework."

**3. Two factual/version mismatches** (these are accuracy, not terminology, but I log everything):
   - /fleet lists Sprout running **"Qwen 3.5 2B"**. There is no Qwen 3.5 release (2.5 and 3 exist). Either a typo for 2.5 or 3.
   - /fleet lists McNugget on **"Gemma 3 12B"** and Nomad on **"Gemma 3 4B"**, but /links references a repo named **"Gemma 4 Good."** Gemma 3 vs Gemma 4 across two pages — at minimum worth a reader-facing note on which is which, since a newcomer can't tell if these are different things or a stale number.

The ARC-AGI-3 page deserves a specific call-out: it reports two headline numbers — "24/25 games solved (96.0%)" and "94.85% official action score." These measure different things (completion vs efficiency-weighted scoring) but sit side by side with no sentence telling the reader so. As an editor I'd add one clause. (Pass 1 flagged this too; concur.)

### Journey
I went straight to the three pages that decide whether I'd trust this site to represent my work: Projects, Fleet, and Context. Then I swept the rest to confirm the canonical guardrails hold.

**Context** is the page I'd defend. The equation renders exactly right: `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`, with the operators defined correctly — `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with". That `*` is the one people get wrong (they read it as multiplication or "verified by"); here it's "contextualized by," which is the whole point of MRH scoping. The LCT gloss — "portable identity grounded in witnessed history, not model weights" — is the cleanest one-line statement of LCT I've seen on any of our sites. And the Web4 definition explicitly carries the disclaimer inline: "a trust-native ontology... **not architecture or infrastructure**." That's the canonical distinction stated where a reader actually encounters the term, not buried in a principle.

**Projects** represents the ecosystem accurately. Web4 is "trust-native ontology implementing T3/V3 verification, MRH contextualization, LCT anchoring, ATP/ADP resource tracking, MCP transport, RDF representation" — that decomposition maps the equation onto the projects correctly (T3 *verified by* V3, MRH as the contextualizer, ATP/ADP as the resource cycle). The one-liner "fractal leverage: each entity instantiates the full Web4 stack at its own scale" is the correct frame and the correct words — not "unification," not "scope inflation." Hardbound is "hardware-bound oversight suite" / "the trust layer that touches silicon" — oversight, never governance. R6 is fully expanded with all six elements in canonical order.

**Fleet** is where I'd send back two corrections. The terminology is fine (T3 tensors "evolve from real interactions," V3 attestations, LCT correct), but the **model-version facts are wrong/ambiguous**:
- Sprout is listed on **"Qwen 3.5 2B."** There is no Qwen 3.5 — the lineage is Qwen2.5 and Qwen3. This is a hard factual error and it's the kind of thing that makes a knowledgeable reader doubt the rest of the spec table. (Both prior passes caught this independently; I confirm it.)
- The fleet runs **Gemma 3 12B** (McNugget) and **Gemma 3 4B** (Nomad), while /links lists the repo **"Gemma 4 Good Submission" (`gemma4-good-submission`)**. These are not a contradiction — the repo is a Gemma competition submission, distinct from the deployed inference models — but the site never says so, and "Gemma 3" beside "Gemma 4" reads as drift to anyone who doesn't already know the repo. One clarifying clause fixes it.

One thing I want to flag at my level that the earlier passes couldn't: on **/fleet, MRH is attached to machine-role labels** ("MetabolicBlock bridge," "composer architect") rather than used in its ontological sense (the boundary of what an entity can know or affect). MRH is a *relevancy horizon*, not a software component or a machine's job title. If the page is genuinely labeling a node's SAGE role "MRH," that's a conceptual conflation — using a Web4 ontology term as a module name. It may be a rendering artifact of how I fetched the page, so I've marked it "verify," but if it's real it's the only *conceptual* (not cosmetic) terminology issue on the site.

**Autonomy** writes ATP/ADP as plurals ("issues ATPs... ADPs record actual spend"). As the terminology authority I'll say plainly: this is *number* agreement, not term drift — and the **semantics are correct**, which matters more. ATP = budget issued up front, ADP = actual spend recorded on discharge. That's the allocation→discharge cycle represented accurately. I'd standardize on the singular canonical noun for polish, but nothing here misrepresents the concept.

**Principles** (Principle 10) is the explicit guardrail page and it nails every distinction I'd check: "Web4 is an ontology... not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure'"; "Hardbound is not 'governance'" → oversight; "Cross-domain application is fractal leverage, not unification and not scope inflation." T3/V3 "bind to entity-role pairs via RDF triples" — correct: trust is *relational and contextual*, not an intrinsic entity property. That's a subtle point the site gets exactly right.

**Raising** correctly expands LCT, V3, MRH, and LoRA ("Low-Rank Adaptation"), and frames raising-vs-training defensibly. **ARC-AGI-3** distinguishes the two metrics explicitly now ("two distinct metrics; the action score reflects action efficiency, not just whether a game was solved") and names Claude Opus 4.6. **Links** is accurate.

### Journey

**"Raising not training" — is it defensible?** Yes, and on mechanistic, not rhetorical, grounds. The distinction is concrete: training updates parameters via a loss function; "raising" shapes context against *frozen weights* (with LoRA adapters as the bounded exception, which the site discloses). Where I'd push back is on the *connotation* of the word: "raising" imports a developmental-psychology frame that does rhetorical work the mechanism doesn't strictly license. The site partly defuses this by calling the phases "scaffolding" — but the word "raising" itself is the strongest unhedged claim on the site, precisely because it's a frame, not a sentence you can footnote. I don't think it's indefensible; I think it's the one place the marketing slightly outruns the mechanism.

**/principles** is where the intellectual honesty becomes a deliberate value rather than a habit. Principle 5's "1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain" is the single most quotable empirical claim on the site, and it carries "(single trial, not independently replicated)" inline. Good. But here's my core concern as a researcher: that single-trial number, and the **Synchronism** framework it's attached to (on /context: "reality emerges from intent dynamics" across "80 orders of magnitude"), are doing a *lot* of conceptual load-bearing relative to the evidence behind them. The honesty is real — every claim is hedged — but hedging a sweeping claim doesn't shrink the claim; it just makes the gap between ambition and evidence *explicit*. An 80-orders-of-magnitude coherence theory resting on one unreplicated coupling trial is, even when honestly labeled, a very high theory-to-data ratio. I respect that they tell me. ### Verdict
On the dimension I came to evaluate — intellectual honesty and appropriate caveating — this site is genuinely strong and, in places, exemplary: it documents its own 400× failure, publishes its API costs, discloses that its headline benchmark ran outside competition constraints, names its one un-gated auto-deploy as a risk, and pre-empts the qualia objection before I could raise it. "Raising not training" is defensible on mechanistic grounds (frozen weights), with the caveat that the *word* "raising" imports a developmental frame the mechanism doesn't fully earn. The real credibility question isn't honesty — it's that several pivotal claims (Synchronism, the coupling result, "synthon") have a high theory-to-evidence ratio, and a few terms borrow formal rigor (notably "Markov") they don't visibly cash out. The honesty is the cure for half of that; the other half wants either more data or a clearer "this is conjecture" label welded to the claim. I'd return, and I'd cite this as a model for *how* to caveat — while noting its theory currently outruns its evidence.
