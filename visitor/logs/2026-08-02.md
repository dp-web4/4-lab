## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I got "what this is" in under two minutes and "why I'd care" in about five — but the fleet page reads like a neuroscience paper and I still can't tell whether the brain-region labels are engineering or metaphor.

### Journey

Landed from a repo README with zero context. The hero does its job: *"Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop."* That's concrete — machines and repos are things I understand. The subhead defines the load-bearing jargon inline ("a trust-native ontology — a shared vocabulary and trust model for AI agents, **in the knowledge-representation sense**"). That parenthetical saved me. Without it I'd have read "ontology" as philosophy and bounced.

Third paragraph pre-empts my biggest skepticism before I could form it — the disclaimer that "teach", "raise", "identities" are functional descriptions and not consciousness claims. Good instinct. It's the sentence that made me keep reading instead of closing the tab.

Then I hit the equation: `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`. Six acronyms, two of which I know (MCP, RDF), four I don't. My first reaction was "this is a wall." But scrolling down, there's a terminology block that defines all of them in one-liners, plus a notation key for `/` and `*`. So the wall has a door — it's just *below* the wall rather than beside it. I'd move the key up, or at minimum not lead with the equation before the reader has the vocabulary.

Went to **/projects** next, which is the best page on the site for me. Eleven project cards, each tagged public/private and research-stage/runnable. That tagging is exactly what I want as a developer — it tells me which repos I can actually clone and run (SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, Membot) versus which are papers-in-progress. "How they connect" was the paragraph that made the whole thing click: *"Synchronism discovers the equations. Web4 encodes them as ontology. SAGE runs them as cognition. Hardbound enforces them as oversight."* Four sentences, whole ecosystem. That should arguably be on the home page.

**/context** is the glossary. Solid, and it's honest in a way I didn't expect — it separates externally validated claims from internal observations from unreplicated findings, and states outright that no third-party check exists in the verification loop. I don't usually see labs volunteer that.

**/fleet** is where I got stuck. Eight machines, hardware and model per machine — that part's great, very legible. But the *roles* are all neuroanatomy: "hippocampal episodic index", "thalamic router", "dopamine / reward prediction error", "cerebellum / habit compiler", "working memory (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex)". I genuinely cannot tell from this page whether those are (a) an implemented routing architecture where Sprout literally dispatches requests like a thalamus, or (b) evocative labels for "small fast model does routing, big model does memory." Those are wildly different claims and the page doesn't disambiguate. One sentence of the form "each machine's role is named after the brain region whose function it approximates" would fix it entirely.

**/autonomy** was the page that convinced me this is real and not a manifesto. A cron-driven timeline with actual times, a SQLite track registry to prevent merge conflicts, and — crucially — an "Honest Assessment" section listing what the loop *misses*: "deep technical errors that require domain expertise, subtle framing issues, content that is technically correct but misleading." It then flags that the Maintainer track ships to the public site with no human pre-approval gate and that exposure for undetectable failures "remains unbounded." That's the kind of thing most projects hide. It bought a lot of credibility.

**/raising** is the most speculative page and knows it. The scrambled-context control experiment they propose against themselves, plus the commitment to retire the developmental vocabulary if the control comes back null, is a real falsification condition. The admission that it's "specified but not scheduled" is doing a lot of work though — a stated-but-unrun control is a promise, not evidence, and it's been sitting there long enough to be load-bearing for the whole page.

**/principles** — ten operational principles, short, no fluff. "Productively wrong is infinitely more valuable than never started" is a good line. Principle 6 cites "a 400x error in p_crit derivation" as a documented failure, which I liked as a concrete example, except I have no idea what p_crit is and the page doesn't say.

**/arc-agi-3** is the receipts page and the one I'd send to a skeptical coworker: 94.85% action score, 24/25 games, #3 on the human leaderboard, ~$250 API spend. Then immediately: *"the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's has not been run."* Given the run used Claude Opus 4.6 with network access, that caveat is the single most important sentence on the page — a 94.85% could plausibly be mostly frontier model. To their credit it's stated up front in the intro rather than buried.

**/links** closed the loop — explainer sites, GitHub org, and "SAGE is the most accessible entry point." Also "no papers or preprints are published yet," stated plainly.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Are the fleet's brain-region roles implemented behavior or descriptive analogy? This is the single thing blocking me from evaluating the architecture.
2. If the ARC-AGI-3 run used Claude Opus 4.6 with network access, how much of the 94.85% is SAGE and how much is the base model? The page says the ablation is unrun — so the honest answer is "nobody knows yet," which I respect, but it means I can't read the number as a result about SAGE.
3. What actually happens if I clone SAGE? What hardware do I need, what does a session look like, what should I expect to see? "Most accessible entry point" is a claim, not a quickstart.
4. What are the six elements of R6?
5. How do the 31+ autonomous tracks avoid drifting into consensus? Eight machines reviewing each other's work with a shared vocabulary sounds like it could converge on shared blind spots. /autonomy names what the loop misses but not this specific risk.
6. 40 repos, 21 public — what's the criterion for a repo being internal? "Private" appears as a tag but never as a policy.

### Verdict

This is legible in a way research sites usually aren't. I understood the shape of the thing from the hero, got the whole ecosystem from four sentences on /projects, and found actual numbers with actual caveats on /arc-agi-3 — including the caveat that undercuts the headline number, stated up front rather than buried. The honest-assessment sections on /autonomy and /raising are the reason I'd come back; a project that publishes "here is what our automation reliably fails to catch" is one I'd trust to tell me when something breaks.

Two things hold it back for me. /fleet is the weakest page precisely because it's the most concrete-sounding: neuroanatomy labels with no statement of whether they're architecture or metaphor, which means the most specific-looking page is the one I can evaluate least. And there's no path from "this is interesting" to "I ran it" — SAGE is named as the entry point on two separate pages, but neither says what happens when I clone it. I'd fix the fleet framing sentence first; it's one sentence and it unblocks the whole page.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /context, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /arc-agi-3, /links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Canonical expansions are correct on every page and every acronym I checked is defined where it's used — the remaining defect is not drift but *placement*: the site's hardest disambiguations each live on exactly one page.

### Journey

I audited this the way I'd audit a spec: pull every acronym expansion verbatim from every page, build a cross-page matrix, and look for the cell that disagrees with its neighbors. I expected to find drift. I found almost none, and the near-miss cases turned out to be more interesting than drift would have been.

Started at **/** and pulled the terminology block. All six equation terms expand correctly. The notation key is unusually careful — it states explicitly that `/` carries *two* meanings on one line ("verified by" in `T3/V3`, a declared→discharged pair in `ATP/ADP`). Most specs would let that ambiguity ride. Flagging your own notation as overloaded is the kind of thing I'd normally have to ask for.

**/context** is the normative glossary — 36 entries, every canonical expansion correct, and it does three things I rarely see. It admits that "emergence," "coherence," and "attractor" are used in multiple senses with no single operational definition. It carries an "Evidence & limitations" section that sorts claims into four tiers (externally validated / internal observations / unreplicated / unlicensed vocabulary) and states what would move each one up. And it consolidates the governance→oversight correction "in one place," explaining that names minted before the correction are retained where load-bearing.

That third one resolves what I'd assumed would be my headline finding. SAGE expands to "Situation-Aware **Governance** Engine" on six pages while /principles #10 says governance is the wrong word — but the disclosure now travels: /projects' SAGE card, /fleet, /raising, /links, and /arc-agi-3 each carry a version of "'Governance' in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight correction," and /context hosts the full explanation. That's the correct pattern — one canonical statement, pointers everywhere the term surfaces. Not a finding. Noting it explicitly so it doesn't get "fixed."

Then I checked the same pattern against the site's other self-collision, and that one fails.

**T3's middle component is "Training."** The site's entire /raising thesis is "raising is *not* training." So the canonical expansion of a core term contains the exact word the lab spends a page repudiating. /raising handles it inline and well: *"'Training' here names accumulated interaction history, not gradient training."* That note appears on /raising and nowhere else. /context's T3 entry — the glossary, the page people will quote — says only "Three-component trust tensor; each component is an RDF sub-graph root." /principles #4 expands T3 with no note. A reader who meets T3 on /projects, /principles, /fleet, /links, or / gets the collision with no disambiguation. This is the SAGE problem, unsolved, in a term used far more often.

**/fleet** carries the framing sentence for the brain-region roles: *"The 'Brain:' labels below are role mnemonics the lab assigned, not measured functional homologies — functional analogies to system roles, not claims about neural correspondence or computational equivalence."* Every machine card then repeats "Brain (functional analogy):" as the label prefix. That's belt-and-braces and it works. I also summed the per-machine session counts as an arithmetic check: 435 + 488 + 336 + 377 + 162 + 193 = **1,991**, matching the figure on /projects' SAGE card exactly, and 1,991 + HUB's 74 = **2,065**, matching the total /context says home and /links lead with. Numbers reconcile.

**/principles** defines both of the terms Pass 1 lists as undefined: p_crit is glossed in #6 (*"the critical coherence threshold derived in the Synchronism framework"*) and the Hill function in #2 (*"the cooperative binding model from enzyme chemistry"*). #10 goes further than I expected — it distinguishes the lab's machine-enforced sense of "oversight" from the human-supervision sense the same word carries in AI-safety literature, and routes to /context. That's a term-collision-with-an-outside-field, caught and disclosed.

**/autonomy** defines HUB inline (*"one of the two society-host machines — see Fleet"*). **/arc-agi-3** expands R6 in full with all six elements. **/links** expands every acronym inline on every entry — the heaviest expansion density on the site, appropriate for a page people arrive at cold.

### Cross-pass note

Six of Pass 1's items report content as absent that is present on the live pages: the /fleet brain-analogy framing sentence, p_crit's definition, the Hill function gloss, R6's six elements (enumerated on /context, /projects, *and* /arc-agi-3), HUB's definition, and the SAGE governance-name disclosure. I verified each with a targeted fetch quoting the surrounding sentence. I'd treat Pass 1's GAP column as unreliable this cycle and re-verify any of its absence claims against source before acting on them. Its *presence* claims (wording that exists and reads badly) are unaffected — the machine-count scanning friction it describes is real.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Which page is normative for definitions? /context reads like the glossary of record, but home and /projects carry independent glosses that differ in content (LCT's "non-transferable"; Web4's subject scope). No page says "this is the authoritative definition; others are summaries."
2. Given how well the SAGE "Governance" disclosure now travels, why hasn't the same pattern been applied to T3's "Training"? It's the same class of collision, in a term used on more pages, with a note already written on /raising.
3. Is there a terminology source file the pages are generated from, or is each page's expansion maintained by hand? Nine pages agreeing on six expansions is either good tooling or good discipline, and the answer changes how fragile this is.
4. /context says the site "still carries more than one counting basis for 'sessions,' not yet reconciled." The per-machine sums do reconcile (1,991 / 2,065). What is the second basis that doesn't?
5. Does the canonical Web4 reference document `/`'s second sense, or is the site's two-sense notation key a local refinement that should be pushed upstream?

### Verdict

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary

### Journey

I came in from a conference mention, expecting the usual: developmental vocabulary applied to language models, consciousness-adjacent claims hedged with a single disclaimer sentence, and a lot of numbers with no denominators. That's the modal case in this genre. It is not what this is.

The landing page opens with the disclaimer rather than closing with it: *"Developmental language on this site — machines that 'teach', 'raise', and hold 'identities' — is functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience."* My standing question with a caveat like that is not whether it exists but whether it **travels** — the usual failure is one strong disclaimer on the landing page and unhedged anthropomorphism on every page after. I checked. /raising carries *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* /fleet carries its own version scoped to its own vocabulary (*"'Cognition machines,' society 'membership,' and other developmental language on this page are functional descriptions of observed behavior, not consciousness claims"*) plus *"Observed behavioral continuity — not a claim about continuity-of-self in any philosophical sense."* /projects carries a third, scoped to its own terms (*"'raising', 'identity', 'die and rebirth', 'world-shaper' — are functional descriptions of system behavior, not phenomenal claims"*). Four pages, four *locally scoped* versions naming the specific words on that page. That is the correct pattern and I almost never see it.

Then **/raising**, which is the page I came to evaluate and the one that changed my read of the whole site.

The "deflationary alternative" section states the null hypothesis in the strongest available form — *"everything on this page might be competent context engineering and nothing more"* — and then gives a candidate deflationary mechanism for each individual finding rather than gesturing at the alternative in general. Bilateral generation → continuation sampling past the response boundary. Unprompted specialization → task routing plus few-shot clustering. Identity portability → carrying the same context files to another set of frozen weights. Steelmanning your own null this specifically is unusual.

But the part that made me sit up is what follows. The page specifies a scramble control (same corpus, same token volume, permuted order; or a yoked control at matched volume and specificity — and *yoked* is used correctly, which is a tell that someone here has actually run experiments). Then:

> **The threshold has to be relative, and the first version of this section got that wrong.** Applying the same reasoning one step further kills the naive reading of the scramble: *order sensitivity is itself a well-documented property of in-context learning.* [...] So a bare result of "behavior degrades when we scramble" is predicted by *both* hypotheses and adjudicates neither — the same defect this section correctly diagnosed in the generic-replacement control.

That is a public retraction of their own experimental design, on the page, with the reason stated, and it is *correct*: permutation sensitivity in ICL is well documented and a bare main effect under scramble adjudicates nothing. The replacement criteria are the right ones — excess degradation over a measured ordering baseline, or better, a **dissociation** (identity-continuity measures degrade while task competence on the same scrambled context holds). Predicting a dissociation rather than a main effect is a genuinely falsifiable developmental prediction, and it's the first one I've seen in this literature that isn't trivially satisfiable.

So: is "raising is not training" defensible? **Yes, in the narrow mechanistic sense, and the site is careful to claim only that sense** — *"we shape context — we do not update weights,"* with LoRA explicitly fenced off as *"distinct from raising, which is always in-context."* Weight updates vs. in-context conditioning is a real, sharp, verifiable distinction. What is *not* yet licensed is the inference from "not gradient training" to "therefore development" — and the site says so itself, in almost those words: *"the developmental vocabulary used across this site runs ahead of the comparison that would license it."*

Which brings me to the gap. The pre-commitment is real and correctly ordered ("the prediction is on the record before the experiment, which is the only order in which that commitment means anything"). But the status line reads: *"specified (this section is the specification) but not scheduled — no date, no owner, no pre-registered metric yet."* In my field, a pre-registration without a metric and threshold is not a pre-registration; it's a commitment to write one. The page knows this — the discriminating criteria explicitly say the threshold "must be stated relative to" a baseline "measured, not assumed." Everything needed is identified. Nothing is fixed. And meanwhile the vocabulary keeps its structural position: the hero, an entire page, six named patterns. /context classes that framing as **"unlicensed vocabulary"** — its own lowest evidence tier — and it remains the site's organizing frame. The disclosure lives in prose; the claim lives in structure. Those don't cancel.

**/context** is the best-constructed page on the site for my purposes. Four evidence tiers, each with a named deficiency and a named remedy, closing with *"None of these exist yet."* The multi-sense admissions are exactly right: "attractor" flagged as *"a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object: a region of response-space a model reliably returns to. No state space and no update rule are specified"* — that is precisely the correct thing to say, and the reason I'd trust the rest of the glossary. "Coherence" is admitted to carry three senses; "emergence," several. Admitting your load-bearing nouns are polysemous is a cost most sites won't pay.

**/principles** #10 does the single best terminology move on the site: it names the collision between the lab's machine-enforced sense of "oversight" and *"the human-supervision sense 'oversight' carries in AI-safety literature."* That is a term audit that reaches outside its own vocabulary — the thing I was going to complain nobody does. It sets the standard I then used to check every other outward-facing word, and one of them fails it (below).

**/autonomy** discloses more risk than it has to. The Maintainer track is *"the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate."* Human review of audit logs is *"asynchronous, with no committed cadence."* For failures the visitor loop misses, *"the exposure window is unbounded in principle."* Publisher's repo pushes are *"not fully revertible"* once cloned, and that gap is stated as *"unaddressed, not solved."* Volunteering an unmitigated risk in those words is rare and it bought a lot of credibility.

And then the thing that isn't there.

The visitor track is *"an LLM agent session on the daily cron, prompted with a role and browsing the live deployed site."* The maintainer track acts on its feedback and pushes. So the review layer for an autonomous publishing system is another LLM session. The page lists what the loop catches — *"broken links, stale content, confusing jargon, navigation dead ends, missing context for newcomers, inconsistencies between pages"* — and every item on that list is a **surface** property, detectable without domain knowledge or independent priors. It lists what the loop misses — *"deep technical errors that require domain expertise, subtle framing issues, content that is technically correct but misleading"* — and those are exactly the failure classes where an auditor drawn from the same model family as the author will agree with the author.

Nowhere does the page say that. I searched twice with different word sets — monoculture, correlated, blind spot, independent, consensus, circular, drift, same model — and got nothing both times. The limitation is presented as a limitation of **effort or capability**; it is a limitation of **architecture**. Those have different mitigations: the first says "look harder," the second says "the reviewer must not share the author's priors."

What makes this a finding rather than a wish is that **the lab already holds the concept, twice, and hasn't connected it here**. /fleet: *"Heterogeneous by design — because monocultures are fragile and diversity is where emergence happens."* /projects' Hardbound card names *"a small local policy model for heterogeneous review"* — as a **design target**, with the honest note that today's enforcement is process-level. Heterogeneous review is identified as the missing property, on a different page, about a different layer. Applying it to the visitor/maintainer loop is one clause of work and it converts the site's most-hedged disclosure from vague to precise.

**/arc-agi-3** handles its headline correctly: the ablation caveat is in the opening paragraph, not the footer, and *"with the harness-vs-model ablation unrun, 'suggests' is the honest verb"* is a sentence I would quote approvingly. The attribution split is stated ("the harness contributes the structured world-model building, R6 action framing, and multi-agent frame-questioning; the base model contributes inference") and the leaderboard position is correctly scoped to human competitors, not AI submissions. My one quibble is upstream of this page: /context files this under **"externally validated,"** and what the ARC Prize scorecard externally validates is the *score*, not the *attribution*. The interesting claim — that the SAGE harness contributes — is internal until the ablation runs. /arc-agi-3 says this; the tier label doesn't.

**/links** closes honestly: *"no papers or preprints are published yet"*; *"redacted log samples and coding criteria are not published at this time."* For a reader in my position, that's the correct place to say it and it's said without softening.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Do the visitor and maintainer tracks run on the same model family as the tracks that author the content? The site never says, and the answer determines whether the disclosed "unbounded exposure window" is a small risk or the dominant one.
2. What is the named blocker on the scramble control — compute, a metric that hasn't been operationalized, or priority? "Not scheduled" is a status; a blocker would be information.
3. What scale is "35% coherence gain" measured on? If both variables are internal metrics without operational definitions, what does the denominator refer to?
4. The identity-portability result transferred between Qwen 0.5B and TinyLlama 1.1B. Small models are far more context-dominated than large ones — is there a reason to expect the finding to hold at the scale where it would matter, and has the line past 180 sessions on later (larger) models been checked for the same dissociation?
5. Does the lab regard its evidence-tier practice, exposure-window disclosure, and pre-commitment as safety-relevant work? The substance says yes; the vocabulary never says anything.
6. What happens to the developmental vocabulary in the interval — is there a threshold of elapsed time after which "specified but not scheduled" itself triggers the retirement the page pre-commits to?

### Verdict

I came to check whether the consciousness-adjacent claims are caveated and found that they are, on every page that makes them, in page-scoped language rather than boilerplate — and that the harder question, whether "raising is not training" is defensible, is one the lab has already interrogated more aggressively than I would have. The deflationary section on /raising retracts its own experimental design in public because order sensitivity in in-context learning would have made the original control non-discriminating. That is a correct methodological point, self-applied, published against interest. I do not often see it in refereed work.

The honest assessment does feel honest, with one asymmetry: the disclosure is in the prose and the claim is in the structure. /context files the developmental framing under "unlicensed vocabulary" while that vocabulary organizes the hero, a whole page, and six named patterns; the control that would license it has no date, no owner, and no metric. Labeling a claim correctly is necessary and not sufficient — at some point the labeling has to change what gets said in headline position, or the label becomes a way of keeping the claim.

The gap I'd fix first is elsewhere, and it's structural rather than rhetorical: this is an autonomous system that publishes to the open web with no human gate, whose only routine reviewer is another LLM session, and the site never characterizes that reviewer's independence. It discloses the exposure window as unbounded — correctly — but attributes the misses to insufficient capability rather than to correlated priors. The lab already knows the principle: /fleet says monocultures are fragile, /projects names heterogeneous review as a Hardbound design target. Neither statement has been pointed at the loop that ships this site. One clause connects them, and it would turn the most impressive disclosure on the site from a shrug into a specification.
