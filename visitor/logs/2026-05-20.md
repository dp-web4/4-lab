## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Journey
I clicked through from a dp-web4 repo README expecting a project I could clone and poke at. The landing page hit me with "Six machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." — good hook, I get the scale instantly. The tagline ("trust-native ontology, autonomous AI cognition, and the theoretical frameworks that connect them") is where I first slowed down. "Trust-native ontology" is three abstract words in a row; I parsed it as "identity/trust system for agents" and moved on, but I wasn't sure I was right.

The stat strip (6 machines / 14 repos / 31+ tracks / 180+ raising sessions) is the most developer-friendly thing on the page — concrete, scannable, makes the place feel alive. SAGE's one-liner ("on-device AI cognition kernel running a continuous 12-step loop: sense, deliberate, act") was the first thing that *clicked* for me as software. Projects page is where I spent the most time — it reads like an ecosystem map and that's exactly what I wanted. SAGE, Web4, Synchronism, ACT, SNARC, Membot, Oversight Plugins all have crisp one-liners and tech stacks (Cosmos SDK, TypeScript/WASM/Python, embedding-based memory). This is the page that made me think "OK, these are real codebases." The acronym soup (T3, V3, MRH, LCT, ATP, ADP) is dense, but most of it is spelled out, so I didn't bounce. The Web4 equation I skipped — too theory-heavy for a first pass, which is fine, I knew where to find it (Context page).

Context page is genuinely good for a curious skimmer: the glossary defines everything I'd hand-waved past. LCT-as-"a passport that travels with you across systems" is the single best line on the whole site — that's the kind of analogy that makes me actually understand a concept. Fleet page was fun: each machine mapped to a brain region (hippocampus, thalamus, cerebellum, dlPFC) with a model and a host. Very concrete, very legible.

Where I stalled: I never found the thing a developer clicking from a README is looking for — *how do I run this / contribute / try the smallest piece?* Links page has GitHub repos and explainer sites, but no "start here," no quickstart, no "good first issue" pointer. The site explains the philosophy beautifully and the "how to get hands on it" not at all.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (Home, Projects, Context, Fleet, Autonomy, Raising, Principles, Links, ARC-AGI-3)

### Journey
I audited methodically, page by page, with the canonical terms table open, checking every acronym expansion against it and comparing wording across pages. My headline finding is reassuring: **the Web4 ontology vocabulary is consistent and correct everywhere it appears.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on Home, Projects, Context, Fleet, Raising — never "Lifelong." T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, SNARC, SAGE, MCP, RDF all hold their canonical expansions across pages. Web4 is consistently "an ontology, not architecture or infrastructure"; Hardbound is consistently "oversight," and the Principles page even calls out the distinction explicitly ("Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight"). That kind of self-documenting terminology discipline is rare and worth preserving.

The Principles page is the terminology backbone of the site: Principle 10 ("Terminology matters"), Principle 2 ("Fractal leverage … Not unification for its own sake … not scope inflation"), and the Web4/Hardbound clarifications turn the canonical rules into reader-facing content. This is exactly what a doc set should do — state its own naming conventions.

Where my methodical eye caught friction, it was in the *small* consistencies, the kind that erode polish rather than meaning:

1. **R6 expansion punctuation differs by page.** Projects renders it `Rules/Role/Request/Reference/Resource/Result` (slashes, no spaces); Context and ARC-AGI-3 render it `Rules / Role / Request / Reference / Resource / Result` (spaced). A recent commit ("terminology punctuation") suggests the site is standardizing on spaced slashes — Projects looks like the straggler.

2. **LCT is singular on most pages but plural ("Linked Context Tokens") on Fleet.** Trivial, but a consistency auditor flags it.

3. **ARC-AGI-3 is expanded inconsistently and with two different forms.** Raising expands it "Abstraction and Reasoning Corpus for Artificial General Intelligence"; the dedicated ARC-AGI-3 page adds ", version 3"; Home, Projects, and Principles use it unexpanded. The acronym should be expanded on first use per page (or at least on the high-traffic Home/Projects), and the expansion string should be identical wherever it appears.

4. **The ARC-AGI-3 score is labeled three different ways, and one is wrong.** The dedicated page and Raising now agree on two clean metrics: "24/25 games solved (96.0% game rate); 94.85% official ARC Prize action score." But **Home says "94.85% on the public set,"** which mislabels the *action score* as a *pass rate on the public set* — they are different things (the pass rate is 96.0%). This is the most substantive issue I found: it's not just inconsistent phrasing, it's a semantically incorrect label on the most-visited page. (Pass 1 flagged the disagreement; I'm confirming the dedicated pages are now reconciled and isolating Home as the remaining offender.)

5. **"12-step loop: sense, deliberate, act"** (Home) still names only 3 of 12 steps — a reader can't reconcile the count. Either name the 12 or call it a 3-phase loop.

The good news for the maintainer: items 1–3 are find-and-replace fixes, item 4 is a one-line edit on Home, and none of them touch the canonical Web4 vocabulary, which is clean.

## Pass 3: Web4 Contributor

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (Home, Projects, Context, Fleet, Autonomy, Principles, Raising, ARC-AGI-3, Links)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid (I already know the ecosystem; I was checking fidelity)
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: The canonical Web4 vocabulary is rendered correctly *and* the equation's semantics are translated faithfully — the part outsiders get wrong, this site gets right; what's left is metric mislabeling and a couple of model-version errors that only someone who runs the fleet would catch.

### Journey
I went straight to Projects, then Context, then Fleet — the three pages where a contributor can tell whether the site is written *by* the ecosystem or merely *about* it. My headline finding is the one I most wanted to confirm and was prepared to be disappointed by: **the Web4 equation is not just displayed correctly, its operator semantics are translated correctly in prose.** The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` carries meaning in its operators (`/` = verified by, `*` = contextualized by, `+` = augmented with), and the Projects page renders it as "T3 … *verified by* V3 …, *contextualized by* MRH, anchored to LCT … *augmented with* MCP and RDF." That is the correct parse. Most secondhand descriptions of Web4 flatten the operators into a bag of plus-signs; this site preserved the grammar. That alone tells me the author works in the ontology, not adjacent to it.

Context page held up to expert scrutiny. LCT-as-passport is the canonical analogy, not a paraphrase. ATP defined as "declares what will be spent before the action runs" and ADP as "the spent form of the ATP" is the charge/discharge cycle stated precisely — ADP being "the spent form" is exactly the biological-ATP framing the term is built on. MRH as "boundary of what an entity can know or affect," Synthon as "emergent coherence entity formed when components interact recursively" — all correct, all in-house phrasing.

Web4-as-ontology and Hardbound-as-oversight are not just used correctly but *defended* on Principles 10 ("Web4 is not 'architecture' or 'infrastructure' — it's an ontology. Hardbound is not 'governance' — it's oversight"), and fractal-leverage is distinguished from unification/scope-inflation. From the authority seat: no canonical drift anywhere. None. I went looking for "Lifelong Capability Token," "governance," "architecture," "Trust-Tracing-Tensor" — found zero.

Where my expert eye caught things the first two passes couldn't fully adjudicate, all of it was in *accuracy of facts*, not *fidelity of terms*:

1. **The 94.85% is an action score, not a public-set solve rate.** This is the one that matters. On ARC-AGI-3, the official ARC Prize "action score" (efficiency-weighted) and the game-solve rate are two distinct numbers: 94.85% is the action score; 96.0% (24/25) is the games-solved rate. The Raising and dedicated pages now state both cleanly and label 94.85% as "official ARC Prize action score" — correct. But **Home still says "94.85% on the public set,"** which relabels the action score as a solve rate, and the ARC-AGI-3 page's prose also drifts toward "the 94.85% represents performance on the public game set." As someone who reports these numbers, conflating the two is a real error, not a phrasing nit — they measure different things.

2. **"Gemma 4 E4B" (ARC-AGI-3, Phase 2) is a suspect model label.** "E4B" is the suffix of the Gemma 3n edge family (E2B/E4B). Pairing it with "Gemma 4" looks like a mislabel of **Gemma 3n E4B** — unless a genuine Gemma 4 line has shipped that I should be told about. Complicating it: the Links page lists a repo literally named `gemma4-good-submission`, so "Gemma 4" may be the team's own shorthand. Either way the on-device model string should be verified against what Sprout/Nomad actually run.

3. **"Qwen 3.5 2B" (Fleet/Sprout) is not a real release.** Authoritatively: there is no Qwen 3.5 line — it's Qwen2.5 or Qwen3. Adjacent entries correctly say "Qwen 2.5 14B." Sprout's string should be corrected to whatever it actually runs (likely a Qwen2.5 or Qwen3 small variant).

4. **T3/V3 called a "three-dimensional tensor" (Home) is imprecise.** T3 and V3 are 3-*element* quantities (rank-1, three components) — "three-dimensional tensor" reads as rank-3 to anyone who works with tensors. Minor, but it's the kind of thing a contributor squints at. "Three-component vector" or just "three dimensions" would be cleaner.

5. **SAGE "12-step loop: sense, deliberate, act"** — confirming from the SAGE side: the loop has more structure than the three named phases; the line conflates the 12-step loop with its 3-phase summary. Name the steps or call it a 3-phase loop.

Everything else — fleet brain-region mappings, SNARC five dimensions / four-tier storage, ACT as the Cosmos SDK implementation, Hardbound's silicon-trust framing — checks out against what I know.

### Summary

- **Overall impression**: Unusually disciplined epistemic hygiene — every consciousness-adjacent claim is explicitly disclaimed at the point of use, and the autonomy/benchmark sections caveat themselves better than most published work — so the one place the rigor lapses (a benchmark number mislabeled on the home page) stands out precisely *because* the rest is so careful.

### Journey
I came in skeptical. A site that pairs "the fleet built a brain," brain-region mappings, and the language of "raising," "becoming," "identity," and "growth" is exactly the kind of thing that, in AI-safety circles, usually signals an author who has talked themselves into believing their system is more than it is. I went to Raising first specifically to see whether the consciousness framing was load-bearing or decorative — and whether it was hedged.

It is hedged, and hedged well. The Raising page states plainly: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia. We're claiming that developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do."* And the vocabulary discipline is the part I didn't expect: *"We record entity-generated vocabulary as observational data — not as an ontological claim about inner states,"* and *"The entities show something that **looks like** growth, something that **looks like** identity… We use the language that fits the phenomenon."* That "looks like" construction is doing real epistemic work — it marks the gap between observable behavior and inner state at every turn, rather than once in a footnote. The home page carries the same disclaimer inline: *"Developmental and lifecycle terms below (identity, behavioral continuity) are functional descriptions of observed system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims."* The Fleet page does it again next to its most poetic line: *"The model is weather. The identity is organism"* is immediately followed by *"Observed behavioral continuity — not a claim about continuity-of-self in any philosophical sense."* This is the right pattern — caveat co-located with claim, not quarantined.

**Is "raising not training" defensible?** Yes, as stated. The operational definition is mechanistic and falsifiable-adjacent: *"Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens. We shape context; we do not update weights."* The claim is narrow — that in-context behavioral attractors emerge with frozen parameters — and the site does not overreach from it. What earned my respect as a safety researcher is that they draw the *safety-relevant* corollary themselves rather than burying it: *"frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior."* That is the correct and uncomfortable implication, and most groups selling a "we don't touch the weights, so it's safe" story omit exactly that sentence. The Principles page reinforces it without inflation: *"It is not training; it is also not nothing,"* and the Hill-function trust-kinetics framing is explicitly labeled *"an analogy, not a fitted mechanism."* Labeling your own analogy as an analogy is the single cheapest credibility signal an author can send, and it's almost never sent. Here it is.

**Autonomy** was where I expected overclaiming about "no human intervention," and it mostly held up. The page is candid that the maintainer track *"autonomously commits to the public site without human review,"* names it the *"higher blast radius"* component, and offsets it with a concrete reversibility argument (Vercel deploy history, one-minute rollback, dated audit logs). It even has an explicit *"Honest assessment"* section listing what the loop misses (deep technical errors, subtle framing). Naming your own failure modes on the marketing site is the behavior of people who expect to be checked.

So where does the rigor lapse? The **ARC-AGI-3 benchmark number** — the one quantitative claim a researcher would actually cite. The dedicated ARC-AGI-3 page and Projects page get it exactly right: they separate *"24/25 games solved (96.0% game rate)"* from *"94.85% official ARC Prize action score,"* and — impressively — they caveat that this is Claude Opus 4.6 *inside the SAGE harness, with network access, on the public set*, and that the real competition is harder (*"32GB VRAM, no internet access,"* unseen private games), explicitly separating model contribution from architectural contribution. That is textbook honest benchmarking. But the **home page** flattens all of it to *"94.85% on the public set (Claude Opus 4.6),"* and the **Links** page repeats *"94.85% public set."* For a researcher, this is the worst place to be sloppy: the action score (efficiency-weighted) and a pass-rate-on-the-public-set are different quantities, and "X% on the public set" reads unambiguously as a solve rate. A reader who only sees the home page leaves with a wrong number in their notes. The fix is trivial and the dedicated page already contains the correct wording — which makes the home-page version look like un-propagated drift, not intent.

A secondary credibility note, from the outside: several glossary terms are defended ontologically rather than operationally, and a skeptic will notice. *"Synthon: an emergent coherence entity formed when components interact recursively under the right substrate conditions"* tells me nothing measurable — what counts as "recursive," what makes conditions "right"? The honest "Preliminary observation: 1% coupling → 35% coherence gain" on Principles is *more* convincing than the definition, because it's a number with a hedge. And **MRH ("Markov Relevancy Horizon")** borrows the Markov property as branding without stating what makes the horizon Markovian — to a researcher who uses the term technically, that's an unearned word. Neither is dishonest; both are places where mechanism would beat metaphor.

### Unanswered Questions
1. Is "94.85% on the public set" ever a correct phrasing, or is the number strictly the action score with 96.0% as the solve rate? The home and dedicated pages currently disagree on what the number *measures* — a researcher can't tell which to cite.
2. What is the operational, measurable definition of a Synthon? "Recursive interaction under the right substrate conditions" isn't testable as written — what's the observable that distinguishes a synthon from a non-synthon?
3. Where is the data behind "1% coupling → 35% coherence gain"? It's the most credible empirical claim on the site and the most hidden (one line on Principles, no link).
4. Does "raising" make any *prediction* that training would not? The framing is defensible as description, but a researcher wants the discriminating prediction — what would we observe under "raising" that we wouldn't under "in-context prompting"?
5. The site discloses that frozen weights don't guarantee safe in-context behavior — so what *is* the safety story for the autonomously-committing maintainer track beyond fast rollback?

### Verdict
As an outside AI-safety researcher, this site clears the bar that most "emergent AI identity" projects fail: the consciousness-adjacent language is consistently and locally disclaimed, the "raising not training" claim is narrow and mechanistic rather than mystical, and — the detail that flipped me from skeptical to credulous — the authors volunteer their own uncomfortable corollaries ("frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior") and label their analogies as analogies. The intellectual honesty reads as genuine, not performed. The serious blemish is not in the philosophy but in the one number: "94.85% on the public set" on the home and Links pages mislabels an efficiency-weighted action score as a solve rate, and that's exactly the figure a researcher carries away. Correct that to match the (already-correct) dedicated page, give Synthon and MRH one operational anchor each, and I'd cite this site without a caveat about its rigor.
