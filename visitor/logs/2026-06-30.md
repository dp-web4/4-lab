## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home + all 8 nav pages)

### Journey
I landed from a repo README, so my first question was the dev-instinct one: "what does this thing actually do, and can I run it?" The home headline — **"Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop."** — is a good hook. Concrete numbers, clear that it's a fleet of machines doing something cooperative. The termite-mound analogy ("You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules.") landed for me — that's emergent-systems thinking I recognize from distributed systems.

Then I hit the equation: **`Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP`**. As a developer I read `+`, `*`, `/` as math operators, but here they apparently mean "augmented with," "contextualized by," and "verified by." That stopped me cold — it *looks* like a formula but isn't one, and the home page only half-defines the symbols. I recognize MCP (Model Context Protocol) and RDF, but LCT/T3/V3/MRH/ATP/ADP were all new and only loosely glossed inline.

**Projects** was the most useful page for me. Twelve project cards, and I could immediately sort them into "I could clone and run this" (SAGE, SNARC, Membot, ACT — they have GitHub links) versus "I'd need a PhD" (Synchronism, AI DNA Discovery, 4-Life). That triage is exactly what a developer wants. SNARC ("salience-gated memory for Claude Code") and Membot ("semantic memory server") were the two I'd actually try first because they're scoped, named like real tools, and solve problems I understand.

**Context** is where the page finally explained itself. It's the glossary I needed on page one. Once I read it, the equation made sense and the acronyms clicked. The "what's tested vs. claimed" evidence grading (external scorecard vs. internal-only vs. single-trial) genuinely raised my trust — most projects hide that.

**ARC-AGI-3** impressed me: a real external benchmark, **94.85% action score**, **24/25 games**, ~$250 API cost, and a clear admission that "the result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness" — i.e. they're not pretending the small local models did it. The insight "context engineering dominates model size" is one I'd actually quote to a colleague.

**Fleet**, **Autonomy**, **Raising**, **Principles** are the philosophy half of the site. Fleet (named machines, models, roles) reads like cool homelab infrastructure. Autonomy (the cron timeline) is concrete but assumes I know what "Hardbound" and "ATP" are. Raising and Principles are where the density spikes — evocative, carefully caveated, but I bounced off the "raising not training" framing without the Context glossary already in my head.

**Links** confirmed it's a hub for an existing community, not an onboarding funnel — it says "New here?" but immediately assumes Web4 fluency.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home + all 8 nav pages)
- **Understanding achieved**: good (I have the canonical table, so I can verify expansions, not just spot gaps)
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Every acronym expansion I could check is canonically *correct* — there is no drift in the expansions themselves; the consistency problems are **where** terms get defined (first-use is inconsistent across pages) and one **structural naming tension** the site half-acknowledges but doesn't resolve in the reader's view.

### Journey
I audited methodically, page by page, building a matrix of every acronym × every page it appears on, comparing each expansion against the canonical table. My job is not "is this interesting" but "does page A say the same thing page B says, and do both match the canon."

The headline finding is reassuring: **the expansions are right.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on every page that expands it. T3 is "Talent / Training / Temperament," V3 is "Valuation / Veracity / Validity," MRH is "Markov Relevancy Horizon," ATP is "Allocation Transfer Packet," ADP is "Allocation Discharge Packet," R6 is the six-element Rules/Role/Request/Reference/Resource/Result framework, MCP is "Model Context Protocol," RDF is "Resource Description Framework." No "Lifelong Capability Token," no "governance" where "oversight" belongs in the prose, no "architecture/infrastructure" for Web4. For a site whose own Principle #10 is "Terminology matters," the canon is being respected. That's the good news, and it's substantial.

The problems are second-order — exactly the ones a tech-writer audit exists to catch:

**1. First-use definitions are inconsistent.** The canonical expansions live almost entirely on `/context`. Other pages use the same acronyms but define them unevenly:
- `/autonomy` uses **SAGE**, **MCP**, **LCT** with no expansion, but *does* fully expand ATP/ADP.
- `/raising` expands **V3** but leaves **T3** undefined on the page — an odd split, since they're always a pair.
- `/arc-agi-3` uses **ATP/ADP** and **MRH** without expansion ("full expansion not provided").
- `/fleet` doesn't expand **MCP** — it calls it "tool surface" instead.
A reader who lands on any deep page from a search result, not from `/context`, hits undefined vocabulary. The fix is not "define everything everywhere" (that bloats the prose) — it's a consistent convention: link every acronym's first in-page use to `/context#term`. The `/links` page already models the right instinct ("See /context for full definitions"); that pattern should be global.

**2. One term is defined two different ways.** **MCP** is "the transport layer for tool calls between AI agents" (home), "tool-call transport… Web4's interaction surface" (context), and just "tool surface" (fleet). Same concept, three phrasings. The expansion never drifts, but the gloss does. Pick one one-liner and reuse it.

**3. The governance/oversight tension — my single most important flag.** The lab forbids "governance" (Hardbound = *oversight*), and the prose honors that. But the flagship project's canonical name is **SAGE = "Situation-Aware Governance Engine"** — the forbidden word is baked into the acronym itself, and it appears on home, projects, fleet, raising, principles, and arc-agi-3. The `/projects` page reportedly carries a note that the naming "predates the terminology change," which is the honest move — but that note lives on one page while "Governance Engine" propagates across six. A careful reader *will* notice the lab saying "we don't say governance" beside a project literally named a Governance Engine. This isn't drift (the expansion is the project's real name), but it is the highest-friction terminology inconsistency on the site.

**4. Acronym collision: ACP vs ACT vs MCP.** `/projects` introduces **ACP** = "Agentic Context Protocol" and **ACT** = "Agentic Context Tool" — two near-identical acronyms, both "Agentic Context ___," one letter apart, on the same page, alongside MCP ("Model Context Protocol"). ACP appears nowhere else and isn't in the canonical table. A reader will conflate them.

Navigation itself is clean: all 8 nav links resolve, the equation and operator legend are byte-identical on home and `/context`, and the machine/repo counts are internally consistent (7 machines = 6 cognition + HUB; 14 repos, 11 public) — so Pass 1's "six vs seven" double-take is actually a reconcilable framing, not a contradiction.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home + all 8 nav pages)

- **Would return?**: yes

### Journey
I came in from a conference mention, so my evaluation question is narrow: **can I trust what this lab says about itself, and does it know the difference between what it has shown and what it merely believes?** I read Raising, Principles, and Autonomy closely, then triangulated against Context and ARC-AGI-3.

The first thing that surprised me — pleasantly — is that the consciousness disclaimers are not buried in a footnote. They're inline, at the point of use. The home page tags its developmental vocabulary with *"functional descriptions of observed system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims,"* and the brain-component analogy with *"not a claim about neural correspondence."* Raising goes further and operationalizes the loaded words before using them: *"by 'identity' we mean consistent session-to-session behavioral patterns"* and *"by 'growth' we mean increasing response diversity,"* capped with the flat statement *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* Most strikingly, when an internal observer wrote *"I notice I want to call it experience,"* the site records that as *"the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim."* That is the lab catching its own anthropomorphic reflex in writing and labeling it as such. I do not see that often. It is the single strongest honesty signal on the site.

**Is "raising not training" defensible?** Yes — and not as a rhetorical dodge. The claim has a concrete, falsifiable backbone: *"in raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights."* Weights are frozen; only context changes between sessions. That is a real, checkable distinction, not a metaphor doing covert work. And the lab does not let the framing flatter itself: it concedes *"frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior."* A weaker site would have used "raising" to imply moral growth and stopped there. This one defines the term mechanically and then warns that the mechanism it relies on is not a safety guarantee. Defensible.

Where I'd push back is at the rhetorical layer, not the technical one. "Raising," "BECOMING," "metabolize," "Synthon," "the model is weather, the identity is organism" — the developmental/biological metaphor is load-bearing across the whole site, and disclaimers neutralize the *claims* but not the *connotations*. A reader who skims (and most do) absorbs the frame, not the footnote. The disclaimers are honest; the vocabulary still pre-loads the conclusion the disclaimers walk back. That's a tension worth naming, even though the lab handles it better than most.

**Principles** is where the epistemic posture is stated as policy, and it largely holds. Principle 3 (*"R&D, not production… explicitly unsuitable for safety-critical applications"*), Principle 6 (document failures), and Principle 9 (*"productively wrong is infinitely more valuable than never started"*) are exactly the norms I'd want a research collective to commit to in writing. Principle 4 — *"Trust is not a property of an entity. It's a property of the relationship between entities, in a specific context, at a specific time"* — is a genuinely sound relational/contextual framing of trust, and one I'd cite approvingly; it resists the common error of treating trust as a scalar attribute. My one critique: Principle 2 ("fractal leverage" — the same stack self-instantiating at every scale) is asserted as a working pattern but carries no empirical grounding on the page; it reads as an aesthetic commitment dressed as a finding. The lab grades its *experimental* claims rigorously but exempts its *architectural* claims from the same scrutiny.

**Autonomy** is the page a safety researcher should read most carefully, and to the lab's credit it does not soften the part that should worry me. The oversight posture is *"detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent (pre-approval),"* and the Maintainer track *"autonomously commits and pushes to Vercel — the only track that ships changes to the public site without a human gate."* This is stated, not hidden — full marks for disclosure. But as an evaluator I have to register the substance, not just the candor: detect-and-revert is only as safe as detection is reliable and the exposure window is short, and *"the public site is the first thing newcomers see"* (the lab's own framing elsewhere). Content that is reverted in sub-a-minute still reached every reader inside that window, and reversion does not un-read it. For an internal experiment this posture is reasonable and even elegant; for a *public-facing, newcomer-targeted* surface it is a real residual risk that the honesty about it does not eliminate. I'd rank this the most important thing on the site for my persona — and I credit them for letting me find it in plain text rather than reverse-engineering it.

**ARC-AGI-3** is the credibility anchor and it survives scrutiny. The attribution is unusually clean: *"The result is Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness. The harness contributes the structured world-model building, R6 action framing, and multi-agent frame-questioning; the base model contributes inference."* They do not let the 94.85% rub off on the small local models — Phase 2 (the local Gemma 3n target) is explicitly separated as the unproven case. And Context volunteers the exact ablation a skeptic would demand: *"the harness-vs-model split is stated but not quantified: no ablation (same model, no harness) has been run."* A lab that names the experiment it *hasn't* run, next to the result it's proudest of, is a lab telling me where its own evidence stops. That is the behavior I'm here to evaluate, and it passes.

**Context's three-tier evidence grading** — externally validated / internal observations / unreplicated — is the structural feature that earns the most trust. The unreplicated coupling-coherence result (1% coupling → 35% coherence) is labeled *"a single trial with no independent replication… Treat it as a preliminary observation, not a finding."* Self-applied evidence tiers, with the weakest claim explicitly demoted from "finding," is rare and it is the right move.

**Where credibility is capped** (Links / Fleet): for an outside researcher deciding whether to engage, provenance matters and it's thin. One email (`dp@metalinxx.io`) for research inquiries, no author bios, no institutional affiliation, no publication history, and — by the lab's own statement — the internal claims "rest on internal session logs… documented and dated, but not externally audited." That's an honest disclosure of an unaudited evidence base, but it means everything below the ARC-AGI-3 tier is "trust us, here are our notes." The honesty is real; the verifiability is not yet there. That's a fair description, not a criticism of intent.
