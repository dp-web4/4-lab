## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: I understood the pitch in under two minutes and found something I could clone — but the single most interesting thing on the site (agents running themselves on cron) is the one thing with no code behind it.

### Journey

I came from a repo README, so my first question was "what is this org actually building, and is any of it runnable?"

The home page answers the first question faster than I expected. The headline is a stat line — "Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop." — but the paragraph under it does the real work by naming a problem I already recognize: agents are out there browsing and transacting, and there's no general way to know whether a given one will behave in a given context. That's a real problem statement, not a mission statement. I kept reading because of it.

The "New here? This site is acronym-dense" banner was the right call. I'd have bounced without it. It sends you to the glossary or a vocabulary primer, then tells you to clone SAGE. Being told which repo to clone, unprompted, on the landing page, is better onboarding than most orgs manage.

**/projects** — Eleven cards, each tagged with a state. Four say `runnable` (SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, Membot), the rest are `research-stage` or `private`. I want to say clearly how much I appreciated that: the honest labels meant I skipped seven cards without resentment instead of clicking through seven repos to discover they're documents. And "Want to run something rather than read? Start with SAGE" repeats the home page's advice, which is fine — repetition of the entry point is not redundancy.

Where I stalled: SNARC is described as a salience-gated memory plugin for Claude Code. I use Claude Code. That's the card I actually wanted to install, and it's the one with a repo link but no setup detail, while SAGE (the bigger commitment) has the Python/conda instructions. The easiest on-ramp for a developer who's already in this ecosystem is under-served relative to the harder one.

**/fleet** — Eight machines with hardware, role, and a session count each. The brain-analog role assignments (Sprout = thalamic router, McNugget = cerebellum, Nomad = metacognition) are memorable and map onto the SAGE components. This page also does something I noticed nowhere else: it lists its own vocabulary ("T3/V3, MRH, SNARC, LoRA, MCP, crystallization, chapter ledger, chapter law") and points at the glossary. That's a pattern the other pages should steal.

I checked the arithmetic because I couldn't help it. The six cognition machines sum to 1,991 sessions; add HUB's 74 and you get 2,065, which is what the home page claims. The counts reconcile. That did more for my trust than any of the prose did.

One stale-looking thing: the page notes a shared fleet model manifest "has not been updated since 2026-03-08," and the fleet refresh is dated 2026-07-24. That's five months on one and a month on the other. I don't know if that matters, but the site tells me it's stale rather than hiding it, which I'd rather have.

**/autonomy** — This is the page that made me want to try things, and also the page that frustrated me most.

It defines a track concretely: "a scheduled autonomous job — a cron entry with a declared scope and a set of repos it writes to." Good. That's an answer in software terms, which is exactly what I needed. The daily cycle is laid out by clock time — supervisors at 03:00, archivist 04:00, publisher 04:30, visitor 05:30, maintainer 06:30, outreach 07:00, explorer 08:00. It even tells me a visitor persona is "an LLM agent session on the daily cron, prompted with a role." So: cron + agent session + a role prompt + write scope. I get it.

Then I went looking for the cron file, or the prompt, or the script, and there is nothing. No repo, no config, no example. Thirty-one-plus tracks and I can't see one of them. Every *other* claim on this site comes with a repo — the theory does, the kernel does, the benchmark does — and the one claim that's operationally novel and that I could actually copy tomorrow is the one with no artifact. That's backwards. I'd argue this is the highest-value missing link on the whole site: a single published example track (cron entry + role prompt + declared write scope) would convert this page from an interesting description into something a developer forks.

Credit where due: the page is unusually frank about failure modes. It admits the visitor loop's "misses are correlated with the authors', not independent of them," and that for content that is technically correct but misleading "the exposure window is unbounded in principle." It also notes that a publisher rewriting history "doesn't unpublish what was already pulled." Those are the things I'd have asked about in a code review.

**/context** — The glossary is the best-built thing here. Every term I'd collected got a real definition, and several got definitions I didn't expect to be honest. "Coherence" says outright it's used in three related senses "with no single operational definition yet." "Attractor" says it's used "as a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object." A glossary that flags its own soft terms is doing something most glossaries don't.

The equation `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` finally clicked once I found the notation key: `/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with", and `/` means something different in ATP/ADP ("declared→discharged"). Without that key the equation is decoration. With it, it parses. The key should be adjacent to every place the equation appears, not just here.

**/raising** — Six BECOMING phases, and the raising-vs-training distinction is mechanical enough for me to evaluate: "we shape context — we do not update weights." That's a claim about code, not about souls, and I can check it in principle. The hedging is heavier than I'm used to — explicitly not claiming consciousness, sentience, or qualia; "identity" defined operationally as session-to-session behavioral consistency; and the striking admission that "everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more."

My developer reaction: the caveats are so thorough that the absence of data underneath them stands out more, not less. The page says the claims "rest on internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet." So there are 2,065 sessions and zero published excerpts. One redacted session log would move this page further than another paragraph of hedging.

**/principles** — Ten of them. Several are ordinary good engineering values stated well ("Document failures", "R&D, not production"). Principle 5 (Synthon) carries a number — "1% coupling gave 35% coherence gain" — and the page itself says it's a "single trial, not independently replicated," that the number "quantifies the experiment's internal metric," and that the principle "has not earned the status of the principles around it." It's rare to see a site rank its own principles by evidential strength. I believe them more because of it.

**/arc-agi-3** — The most credible page on the site, because it's the only one where I can check the claim myself. 24/25 games, 94.85% action score, 5,845 actions, ~$250 in API cost, with a link to an ARC Prize scorecard and an MIT-0 harness repo. And it puts the disqualifying caveat first: the ablation separating harness from base model "has not been run," and the result is "Claude Opus 4.6 operating within the SAGE harness" — not a locally-raised model. Being told up front that the interesting number might be the model and not the thing they built is the opposite of how benchmark pages usually read.

**/links** — Sixteen links, a contact email (dp@metalinxx.io), and that's the end of the funnel. Which is where the site loses me as a *contributor* rather than a reader.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Can I see one autonomous track? Not a description — the actual cron line and role prompt. Everything else here ships code; why not this?
2. If I fix a typo or find a bug, where does it go? There's an email and sixteen repos, but no stated contribution path.
3. Is any locally-raised model doing anything measurable yet, or is every headline result a frontier model inside a harness? The ARC page is honest that its number is Claude Opus 4.6 — is there a result anywhere that isn't?
4. What's actually running on the eight machines minute to minute — the tracks, or SAGE, or both? I can see the schedule and the hardware but not the relationship between them.
5. Why does SNARC — a plugin for the tool I already use — get the least install guidance of the four runnable projects?

### Verdict

This site passes the two-minute test, which is rarer than it should be: the problem statement on the home page is concrete, the glossary is genuinely reference-grade, and the runnable/research-stage labels let me triage eleven projects without opening eleven tabs. The honesty is not decorative either — the ARC page leads with the ablation it didn't run, and /principles ranks its own principles by how much evidence backs them.

What it doesn't do is convert. The most fork-worthy idea here — agents on cron, with declared write scopes, auditing and fixing a site daily — is described well and shipped nowhere, and there's no contribution path beyond an email address. I'd come back, and I'd probably clone SAGE. But I'd have cloned the autonomy setup today if it existed, and that's the gap that costs the most.

## Pass 2: Technical Writer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (/, /projects, /fleet, /autonomy, /raising, /principles, /context, /links, /arc-agi-3)
- **Understanding achieved**: solid
- **Would return?**: yes
- **Overall impression**: Every acronym expansion on this site is canon-clean and identical across all nine pages — and that clean result is measuring the wrong layer, because the actual terminology defects are all in glosses, coverage, and a promise the glossary doesn't keep.

### Journey

I audit for three things: are terms defined, are they defined *once* and *the same way*, and does the reference the site points at actually contain what the site says it contains. I checked all nine pages against the canonical table.

**The expansion layer is finished work.** LCT is "Linked Context Token" on /, /context, /projects, /fleet, /raising, /links, /arc-agi-3 — seven pages, character-identical, zero instances of "Lifelong Capability". T3 is "Talent / Training / Temperament" on six pages. V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, SNARC, SAGE, LoRA: same story. SAGE expands to "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" on eight pages without variation. I could not find a single expansion that contradicted another expansion, or a single one that departed from the canonical table. That is a genuinely unusual result for a nine-page site with this much vocabulary, and it means the drift-hunting part of my job is done and should stop being the headline of this pass.

So I went one layer down, to the sentence *after* the expansion. That's where I found things.

**/context** is a 25-section thematic glossary with a 44-term "Glossary at a glance" table at the top: Web4, Ontology, Trust-native, Trust, Autonomous, Emergence, MCP, RDF, LCT, Witness, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, R6, R7, SAGE, SNARC, Hardbound, PolicyGate, Policy model, ACP, ACT, LoRA, Synthon, Attractor, Raising, Experience buffer, Fractal leverage, Synchronism, Crystallization, Metabolic state, Coherence, Identity continuity, Society, Sovereign, HUB, Chapter ledger, Chapter law, ARC-AGI-3, p_crit, Cartridge, Membot. It links the source of truth with a real URL: "Source of truth: the canonical terms reference in the public web4 repo — CANONICAL_TERMS_v1.md". It marks its own out-of-canon entries: "Society-layer vocabulary; not yet in CANONICAL_TERMS_v1" on Society, Sovereign, HUB, Chapter ledger, Chapter law. As a reference document this is better than most shipped product glossaries I've reviewed.

Then I checked its coverage against what the rest of the site actually says, and found the gap has a shape.

**The glossary is complete on inherited vocabulary and gapped on the lab's own.** Every Web4 term, every SAGE term, every Synchronism term has an entry. The terms with no entry are, without exception, the ones this lab invented for its own operations:

- **"interactive selection"** — the title of Principle 7. Does not appear anywhere on /context, in any form.
- **"BECOMING"** — the organizing heading of the entire /raising page. Does not appear on /context.
- **"substrate"** — the title of Principle 1 ("Substrate, not structure"). Used four times on /context itself, never defined there.
- **"track"** — the unit of the home page's headline statistic ("31+ Autonomous tracks"). Used repeatedly on /context ("a track picks when it runs and what it writes within a declared scope", "No human gate currently sits on the Maintainer track"), never defined there. Its only definition lives on /autonomy.
- **"society-host"** — appears in the home page's stat line ("6 cognition + 2 society-hosts") and on /context ("one of the fleet's two society-host machines (HUB and pub)"). No entry.

That inverts the usual failure. Sites normally define their own coinages and hand-wave the inherited jargon; this one is rigorous about the vocabulary it borrowed and casual about the vocabulary it minted. And the borrowed vocabulary has an external authority to check against, while the minted vocabulary has none — so the gap sits exactly where nothing else can catch it.

**This makes the home page banner false.** The banner reads: *"This site is acronym-dense. Start with the /context glossary — every term on every page is defined there — or the vocabulary primer below ↓"*. That is a coverage promise, and five terms above falsify it, three of them section or principle titles. This is the single most fixable defect I found: either qualify the sentence ("every Web4 term") or add the five entries. A reference that overstates its own scope trains readers to stop trusting the pointer, and this site's whole information architecture is built on that pointer.

**The attractor contradiction.** /context's entry is the most intellectually careful thing on the site:

> "Used on this site as a METAPHOR, not a formal dynamical-systems object: a region of response-space a model reliably returns to under a given context. No state space and no update rule are specified, and none is implied — we have not defined the dynamics that would make 'attractor' a technical claim. Flagged explicitly because the word borrows the connotation of mathematical precision from a formalism this site does not cash out. **Read it as 'stable behavioral tendency'.**"

Now here is every use of the word on the pages where it does actual work:

- /raising: "we probe what the model responds to, observe which attractors **(stable response basins in the probability landscape)** surface"
- /raising: "Behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns, not in parameter changes."
- /raising: "Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics"
- /principles: "observing which attractors **(stable response basins in the probability landscape)** surface"
- /principles: "shaping context to **deepen attractor basins in the probability landscape** — not modifying parameters"

The glossary says: do not read this as a landscape object, read it as a stable behavioral tendency. Both pages that use the term gloss it, parenthetically and at first use, as a basin in a landscape. "Basin" and "landscape" are precisely the formalism the glossary says the site does not cash out. The two spoke pages are perfectly consistent with each other and squarely contradict the hub.

The direction matters. The glossary is section 21 of 25 on a page a reader reaches deliberately; /raising and /principles are top-level nav. The version that reaches readers is the one the glossary disowns. A caveat that only exists where nobody is looking is not a caveat, and this one is good enough that it deserves to be the version people actually see — the fix is to move the glossary's four-word instruction ("stable behavioral tendency") into the two parentheticals, not to weaken it.

**The coherence caveat has the same problem, and worse placement.** /context flags three senses with no operational definition. /principles carries the caveat in full, including the circularity admission ("'Synthon' is defined through coherence, and coherence is in turn partly defined through the synthon marker — the two terms lean on each other, and neither has an operational definition yet"). /raising links to it. But the home page — the first page every reader sees — states: *"Observable: coherence holds above a coupling threshold; below it, components drift independently."* Labelled **Observable**, no hedge, no link. Three pages hedge the term carefully and the entry page presents it as a measured finding. The caveat exists; it just doesn't reach the front door.

**/autonomy** is the one page with no vocabulary pointer in its body text — only the nav "Glossary" link. It's also the page carrying the most undefined nouns: "HUB (one of the two society-host machines — see Fleet), runs substrate, not tracks" uses two terms that have no glossary entry, in a sentence whose whole meaning is the contrast between them. I could not determine from the site what "runs substrate" means. Meanwhile the page's own definition of a track — "a scheduled autonomous job — a cron entry with a declared scope and a set of repos it writes to" — is the best operational definition on the site and is stranded here, unreferenced by the glossary that uses the word twenty times.

**Two corrections to Pass 1**, since the maintainer track reads both passes and one of these would cause a wrong edit:

1. Pass 1 reports that on /links "every acronym on the page appears bare — LCT, T3/V3, MRH, MCP, RDF, R6, WASM, MIT-0." My fetch of the same page returns inline expansions for all of them: "LCT (Linked Context Token)", "T3 (Talent / Training / Temperament)", "MRH (Markov Relevancy Horizon)", "MCP (Model Context Protocol)", "RDF (Resource Description Framework)", "ATP (Allocation Transfer Packet) / ADP (Allocation Discharge Packet)", "WASM (WebAssembly)", "MIT-0 (MIT No Attribution)", "R6 = the Six-Element Action Framework: Rules / Role / Request / Reference / Resource / Result", plus **two** separate "New here?" pointers to /context. Specific quoted parentheticals are stronger evidence than a general absence claim, so I believe /links is already expansion-complete — but this needs a source check before anyone edits, because we cannot both be right. The only genuinely bare acronym I found there is SNARC.

2. Pass 1 describes "Six BECOMING phases" on /raising. The page says **"BECOMING: six observed patterns"**, labels them "Pattern 1:" through "Pattern 6:", and explains the word choice explicitly: "These are observed descriptive categories — patterns noticed across hundreds of sessions — not mandatory sequential stages with defined transition criteria." The word "phases" was deliberately avoided. Nobody should reintroduce it.

**One editorial disagreement with Pass 1.** Pass 1 rates the repeated governance→oversight disclaimer as medium friction and proposes stating it once on /context with the other pages linking the word. I'd push back. The disclaimer appears on six pages (/, /fleet, /raising, /principles, /links, /arc-agi-3) and every one of those is a landing page for a search engine or a shared link. A reader who arrives at /raising from a search result and reads "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" needs the disambiguation *there*, not one click away. Repeating a disambiguation at each point of use is correct practice, not redundancy — the thing to fix is its length, not its count. Compress it to a clause; don't centralize it.

With a threshold: "excess degradation over an ordering baseline," pre-registered. /autonomy is the most epistemically mature page here and it is not close. /arc-agi-3 is the one externally scored result and it leads with what it doesn't show: the same-model-no-harness ablation "has not been run," said twice; the #3 human-leaderboard placement disclaimed as "'placed on that board,' not... a like-for-like result"; and the sentence I'd most like to see travel — "what it buys is auditability, not a demonstrated safety property."

/context turned out to hold the site's best epistemic asset, and I nearly missed it. There is a four-tier evidence scheme: **externally validated**, **internal observation**, **unreplicated**, and **unlicensed vocabulary — developmental framing awaiting deflationary control**. That fourth tier is a lab saying, in its own reference document, that it is currently spending words it hasn't earned. I have not seen that category named anywhere else.

From outside, a named-but-unscheduled test is not distinguishable from a well-phrased hedge. This lab runs 31+ daily autonomous tracks and, as far as this site shows, not one of them is an experiment. That is the gap between a site that reasons honestly and a lab that produces evidence.

### Unanswered Questions

1. Who runs the scramble control, and by when? It is designed to a threshold. Nothing says it is scheduled, owned, or funded.
2. If the scramble control comes back "raising is a redescription of prompt engineering" — what on this site changes? Naming the consequence in advance is what separates a pre-registration from a hedge.
3. Does the p_crit refutation bear on the 1%-coupling / 35%-coherence result? Same framework, same page, no stated relation.
4. What would "deflationary control" of the developmental vocabulary actually consist of? The tier names the debt but not its terms.
5. Is there any plan for an auditor structurally independent of the model families that author the content? /autonomy states the problem more precisely than most papers would and then stops.
6. Why is not one of 31+ daily autonomous tracks an experiment? The infrastructure to run the named tests on a schedule visibly already exists.

### Verdict

Judged on intellectual honesty, this is the most careful site of its kind I have read: it states its own null hypothesis at the top and the bottom of the page it threatens, names its reviewer-independence failure in terms most published work avoids, reads its own best number as weak evidence, publishes a 400x refutation of its own framework, and — new, and the thing that changed my assessment — specifies the experiment that would falsify its central claim, with a threshold. "Raising not training" is defensible as stated, because as stated it is a claim about a procedure (context, not weights), not about a mind.

What the site cannot do is convert that honesty into evidence, and it has now reached the point where further caveats subtract rather than add. Three falsification tests are named and none is owned; a four-tier evidence taxonomy is written and none of its labels is used; and outside the ARC scorecard there is not one inspectable artifact. The remaining terminology risk runs the same direction — not overclaiming about minds, which is handled well, but borrowing "trust", "attractor", and "society" from fields whose formal apparatus is not on offer here.

Run one of the three tests. Publish one redacted log with its coding criteria. Either would do more for this lab's credibility with my field than everything else on the priority list combined.
