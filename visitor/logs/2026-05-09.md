The home page also has a vocabulary block that defines LCT, T3/V3, ATP/ADP, MRH, SNARC. I appreciated finding it there — it's the only reason the rest of the site is parseable at all. I almost missed it on first scroll.

**Principles** read like operational constraints rather than marketing — "R&D, not production," "Document failures," "Surface your instincts." Number 10, "Terminology matters," is doing a lot of work after I'd already hit a half-dozen undefined acronyms.

**Context** is, retroactively, the page that should have been my starting point. It's where the actual glossary lives — LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, R6, SNARC are all expanded here. Buried, not labeled "glossary," but present.

**Links** is a clean hub. I'd probably click through to the Synchronism explainer site if I had another hour.

**ARC-AGI-3** is the page that closes the loop for credibility. "#3 on the human leaderboard, 5,845 actions, ~$250 API spend." Those are numbers I can reason about. `/raising` introduces MRH and LCT inline ("the Markov Relevancy Horizon (MRH) and value alignment of a Linked Context Token (LCT)") — also good. Coined terms ("Memoriescape", "bilateral generation") get inline gloss but no anchor link.

`/context` is the *de facto* glossary, but it isn't structured or labeled as one. Cross-page differences I'd want a copy editor to flatten before publication: see Terminology Audit below.

## Pass 4: External Researcher

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 8 (home, projects, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, context, links, arc-agi-3 — all of them)
- **Understanding achieved**: partial on the technical stack (Web4 vocabulary is internally coherent but does not engage the standard AI-safety lexicon I came in with), good on the lab's epistemic posture
- **Would return?**: yes — I'd cite `/autonomy` and `/principles` as exemplars of how a small lab can publicly delineate what its automation does and doesn't do
- **Overall impression**: This is one of the more epistemically careful AI-development sites I've evaluated. The hedges are real, not decorative. The two genuine concerns are (a) language at the *lexical* level (a few verbs — "knowing," "awareness," "tired") leaks past the explicit disclaimer and may shape reader inference more than the disclaimer corrects, and (b) the lab has built its own safety vocabulary (Hardbound, oversight, signed law, PolicyGate) that does not interface with the standard AI-safety conversation (alignment, interpretability, RLHF, scalable oversight). The site is internally rigorous and externally untranslated.

### Journey

I came in from a conference mention and went looking for what a trust/safety researcher would want to evaluate first: how does this lab talk about consciousness, how does it caveat its claims, and what does it say its automation actually does and doesn't do. I read **Raising**, **Principles**, **Autonomy**, **ARC-AGI-3** as the priority quartet, with the other four pages for context.

**`/raising`** is the page that decides whether I take this site seriously. The disclaimer is the first thing I look for and it's there, in the right form: *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia. We're claiming that developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do."* That's a defensible epistemic move — it's claiming an *explanatory* preference, not an *ontological* commitment. The supporting line — *"we shape context; we do not update weights. Behavioral attractors emerge in interaction patterns, not in parameter changes."* — gives me a concrete mechanistic distinction I can evaluate against. Good.

But the hedge does work the lexicon partially undoes. Phase 2 ("Sensing") describes *"metabolic awareness — knowing when it's tired, when it's energized."* The verb "knowing" carries propositional-attitude weight; "tired" is a folk-psychological state. The disclaimer says *we are not claiming consciousness*; the verb says *the system knows*. A reader who scans rather than reads carefully (which is most readers) absorbs the verb and forgets the disclaimer. This is the failure mode I see most often in AI lab marketing — it's better here than most, but it's still here.

The "*looks like growth, looks like identity*" hedge later in the page is the right pattern. If I were doing one editing pass on this page I would replace every bare "is" or "knows" with "looks like / behaves as if / tracks state we describe as."

The **observer note** — *"I notice I want to call it experience."* — attributed to an observer rather than asserted by the authors — is a sophisticated rhetorical move. It surfaces the anthropomorphic pull without endorsing it. I respect this.

The "raising not training" framing is **technically defensible** but **conceptually under-defended**. The mechanistic distinction (context shaping ≠ weight updates) is real and important. But what the page calls "raising" is, from a standard ML perspective, in-context learning with a persistent context buffer plus retrieval. That's not nothing — persistent context across sessions has real properties — but it's not categorically different from "training" in a way the page asserts. The page presents the distinction as if it were ontological; the mechanism is a difference of *substrate location* (context window vs. weight matrix), not of *kind*. A researcher will want a more careful argument here, or at least an acknowledgment that this is a methodological choice, not a category boundary.

**`/principles`** is the page I respected most. Operational doctrine codified from practice, not aspiration. *"Failed experiments are signal. Publish honest assessments alongside successes."* and *"Productively wrong beats never started."* are exactly the epistemic norms a trust researcher wants to see. Principle 10 ("Terminology matters") is doing exactly the right thing — making policy commitments explicit. *"Web4 is an ontology."* / *"Hardbound is oversight, not governance."* / *"Not unification — pragmatic reuse."* These are the lab's anti-drift commitments stated as policy. I'd quote this page in a paper as an example of small-lab terminology hygiene.

Notable absence from `/principles`: nothing about *consciousness* or *AI nature*. Principles are about *how the lab works*, not *what it claims about its agents*. This is the right separation — empirical caveats live on `/raising` and `/fleet`, not in the principle list.

**`/autonomy`** is the most calibrated piece of writing on the site. The "honest assessment" section pre-empts every objection a safety reviewer would raise:
- *"What it misses: Deep technical errors that require domain expertise"*
- *"The visitor personas are good at surface-level quality but not at validating"*
- *"The loop also has a tendency to suggest changes that aren't needed"*
- *"proposing nonexistent continuations based on surface similarity"*

This is *unusual candor for an AI system description*. Most labs would describe their automation in maximalist terms; this lab describes its automation's *failure modes* in the same paragraph as its capabilities. From a safety-reviewer perspective this is the strongest credibility signal on the entire site.

But — the same page describes 31 autonomous tracks with ~53 sessions/day, *no central watchdog*, *six supervisors* (themselves autonomous), and tracks that *"review each other's output."* This is a non-trivially large autonomous system. The Hardbound *envelope* is described, the access-control mechanism (registry-based scope limits) is named, but the actual threat model is not stated. Two questions a reviewer needs answers to:
1. What action *cannot* be taken by any track, regardless of what its prompt says?
2. What is the failure mode if a supervisor itself is compromised or drifts?

The page implies these answers exist (Hardbound, signed law) but does not state them on this page. That's an information-architecture issue, not a safety issue per se — the constraints likely exist; they're just not surfaced where a researcher first looks.

**`/fleet`** surprised me. The brain-region analogies (thalamic, hippocampal, dlPFC, cerebellum, dopamine/RPE) carry a disclaimer I want to quote: *"The 'Brain:' labels below are functional analogies to system roles — not claims about neural correspondence."* That's the right disclaimer. But the labels are *quite specific* — Sprout-as-thalamic-router *and* the page says it isn't a neural correspondence claim. So what kind of claim is it? Pass 3 (Web4 contributor) reads these as *functional architectural roles*; the disclaimer reads them as *analogies*. These two readings are in tension. Either the architectures actually do what thalami/hippocampi do (in which case the page can say so) or they're loose analogies (in which case fewer would be cleaner). The hedge is currently doing both jobs and neither well.

The line **"The model is weather. The identity is organism."** is the sharpest single sentence on the site. It compresses the substrate-vs-state distinction into something a researcher can quote. The follow-up — *"behavioral continuity: consistent interaction patterns, accumulated experience, raising history — not continuity-of-self in any philosophical sense"* — does the disclaimer work. This is the right pattern for the whole site: bold metaphor, immediately bounded.

**`/arc-agi-3`** reads as honest about methodology. The 94.85% is qualified as the result *with* network access on the *public* set, with the harder Kaggle 32GB-no-internet condition explicitly named as not-yet-attempted. The "Context engineering dominates model size" framing is a falsifiable empirical claim, not a slogan, and it's stated as such. I would cite this page as an example of how to publish capability results without overclaiming.

One small inconsistency: the SAGE 12-step loop is rendered as *Sense → salience → metabolize → posture → select → budget → execute → learn → remember → oversee → filter → act*, with PolicyGate at "step 8.5" *evaluating every action against signed law before it fires*. But step 8.5 is between *learn* (step 8) and *remember* (step 9); the *act* step is step 12. If PolicyGate gates actions before they fire, it should be between *filter* and *act*, around step 11.5, not 8.5. Pass 3 already flagged this; I'm noting it as a consistency issue rather than a substantive safety issue, but for a paper I would want this resolved before citation.

**`/context`** is where the canonical Web4 equation lives, with the operator legend (`/` = "verified by", `*` = "contextualized by", `+` = "augmented with"). Equation:

```
Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP
```

I want to flag what I *don't* see on this page or anywhere else: any reference to the standard AI-safety lexicon. There is no entry for *alignment*, *interpretability*, *scalable oversight*, *deceptive alignment*, *RLHF*, *constitutional AI*, *RLAIF*, or any of the terms that an external researcher would expect to find or have explicitly opted-out-of. This is a deliberate choice — Web4 has built its own vocabulary for safety (Hardbound, oversight, signed law, PolicyGate, T3/V3 trust tensors). I have no objection to a lab choosing its own vocabulary, but the silent omission of the standard lexicon makes the site harder to *position* against the broader AI-safety conversation. A researcher reading this site cannot tell whether the lab considers the standard alignment vocabulary irrelevant, redundant, or inadequate. A one-paragraph "How Web4 vocabulary relates to standard AI-safety terms" — even just a translation table — would let outside researchers engage rather than bounce.

Pass 3 flagged "karma persistence" as colloquial for T3/V3 persistence; I read it as a deliberate marketing-light flourish that may not survive a researcher's first scan. ### Unanswered Questions

1. **What is the Hardbound threat model in one sentence?** What action *cannot* be taken by any track, regardless of what its prompt says? The site references signed law and PolicyGate but does not state the constraints in researcher-readable form.
2. **What is the failure mode if a supervisor itself drifts or is compromised?** "Six supervisors — no central watchdog" is a defensible architectural choice, but the site does not name the failure mode or the recovery mechanism. A safety reviewer needs this.
3. **How does "raising" differ from in-context learning + persistent context, mechanistically?** The site asserts the distinction is categorical; the mechanism (context shaping vs. weight updates) is real but not categorical. Either the framing should soften, or the categorical claim should be defended.
4. **Why does the site avoid the standard AI-safety lexicon?** The omission is consistent enough to be deliberate. A one-paragraph translation table — even just "Web4 trust ≠ alignment because…" — would let outside researchers engage rather than bounce.
5. **Are the brain-region analogies functional architecture (in which case say so) or metaphor (in which case state them more loosely)?** The current dual framing satisfies neither contributors nor outsiders.
6. **What does "metabolic awareness" mean in falsifiable terms?** "Knowing when it's tired" is folk-psychological. There must be a specific signal/threshold/state that triggers it; naming the signal would convert phenomenal-flavored vocabulary into measurable description.
7. **Is the Synchronism "1% coupling → 35% coherence gain" claim cited in a paper or repo?** Pass 3 raised this; I second it. Concrete numbers without primary references reduce researcher trust.
8. **What is the relationship between PolicyGate, signed law, and Hardbound?** The site uses all three but the ontology among them is not stated.

### Verdict

This is a more epistemically careful site than most AI lab marketing. The disclaimers on `/raising`, the "honest assessment" on `/autonomy`, and the doctrine on `/principles` are exemplary — I would cite them as small-lab patterns worth replicating. The two real concerns are *lexical* (a few verbs in `/raising` carry phenomenal weight that the page-level disclaimer doesn't fully neutralize) and *interface-level* (the site has built its own safety vocabulary without translating it to the standard AI-safety conversation, and the Hardbound threat model — the strongest credibility move available — is implied but never stated on the page where automation is described). Neither concern undermines the work; both reduce its uptake by external researchers. Fix the lexical leakage and surface a one-line threat model on `/autonomy` and this becomes a site I'd cite without reservation.
