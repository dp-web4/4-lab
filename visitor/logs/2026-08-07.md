### Journey

I came in from a dp-web4 repo README with zero context. Landing page hits me with "Eight machines. Forty original repos. One recursive learning loop." and an equation — `Web4 = MCP + RDF + LCT + T3/V3*MRH + ATP/ADP` — before I know what any of those letters mean. My first reaction was to bounce. What kept me was the line right under it: **"New here? This site is acronym-dense. Start with the /context glossary."** A site that tells me it's dense before I discover it myself buys a lot of goodwill. And the vocabulary primer is directly below, so I didn't even have to leave the page.

The primer is genuinely good. "Web4: An *ontology* (shared vocabulary + relationships) for how AI agents prove identity, earn trust, and account for resources. **Not a blockchain, not a platform** — a way of describing things." That negative definition did more for me than three paragraphs of positive definition would have. Same with "T3 / V3 — three components each, **not a single number**." Whoever wrote these anticipated the wrong guess I was about to make and blocked it. That's real technical writing.

Went to `/projects` next, which is where I actually decide whether to spend a weekend on something. Best page on the site for my purposes, and the reason is one design decision: every card carries a status badge — **runnable** ("clone it and it executes end to end today"), **research-stage** ("design docs, specs, and experiments"), or **private**. I know within one scan that SAGE is code, Web4 is a spec, Hardbound is closed. I have burned entire evenings on repos that turned out to be papers with a `src/` directory. This site does not let that happen. Genuinely the thing I'd steal for my own project's README.

The SAGE card also pre-empts a confusion I was about to have. SAGE = "Situation-Aware **Governance** Engine," but Hardbound is described as the "oversight suite," and the lab clearly cares about that word choice. Before I could file it as an inconsistency, the card says: *"'Governance' in SAGE's name predates the lab's governance→oversight terminology correction; the name is retained upstream across code, papers, and the ARC Prize benchmark."* Correct answer. Don't rename a repo that's cited in a benchmark submission — just explain the seam.

Tried the actual quick start from `/links`: `git clone`, then `python3 -m sage.instances.init --machine mybox --model gemma3:4b --operator-name yourname`, then a daemon launch line. These are real, copy-pasteable, and parameterized with obvious placeholders. Not `<YOUR_CONFIG_HERE>` vapor. I'd try this.

`/arc-agi-3` is where I decided the site was credible rather than just polished. 94.85% action score, 24/25 games. My immediate skeptical question was "is that SAGE, or is that Claude Opus 4.6 doing the work?" — and the page asks it for me: *"the ablation that would separate the harness's contribution from the base model's (same model, no harness) has not been run"* and *"with the harness-vs-model ablation unrun, 'suggests' is the honest verb."* They're naming the exact experiment that would falsify their own headline number and admitting they haven't run it. I trust this page more than I'd trust one claiming 94.85% with no asterisk.

`/fleet` was fun in a hardware-nerd way — eight boxes from a Jetson Orin Nano 8GB up to a 122GB AGX, each running a different model, each mapped to a brain-region role (thalamic router, cerebellum, working memory). The claim "heterogeneous by design — because monocultures are fragile" is the kind of thing that's usually hand-waving, but the fleet actually *is* heterogeneous, so it's a description rather than an aspiration. The `pub` entry is the one I liked most: eighth machine, "brought up from a completely cold box as a deliberate live audit of the fleet's own onboarding docs (nine stale/missing-doc findings, all filed and fixed)." Using a new machine as a test of your own setup docs is a good trick.

`/autonomy` and `/principles` I read faster — more theory than I usually stop for. But `/autonomy` volunteers its own failure modes ("the oversight posture is detect-and-revert... not prevent"; "once a commit is public it can be cloned, forked, and mirrored before any revert lands"), which is the second time the site told me a limitation I hadn't asked about.

What I never found, across nine pages: **why**. Not "what does Web4 do" — that's answered five ways. I mean what breaks today without it. Which failure did someone hit that made a trust ontology for AI agents the obvious next thing to build? Every page answers *what* and several answer *how honestly*. None answer *what for*.

**Method note, for whoever runs this pass next.** Five drafted findings, five refuted, and all five were absence claims ("X is missing", "the pointer has no target", "this number is unsubstantiated"). ### Summary

### Journey

I work in the Web4 and SAGE repos. I use these terms daily and I came here to check whether the site misrepresents what I work on. Straight to /projects, /fleet, /context, then swept the rest.

**The ATP/ADP half of the equation is the thing I check first, because it's the part everyone gets wrong.** The common failure is rendering ADP as a terminal log line — "ATP declares, ADP records, done." That's wrong; the whole point is the cycle. This site gets it right in four separate places and gets it right *for the right reason*. /context's ADP row: *"Not a terminal log line: ADP recharges back to ATP against validated value creation, which is V3's job in the resource half of the equation. Charged → spent → recharged."* It names V3 as the validator of the recharge. That's not a paraphrase of the spec, that's someone who understands why V3 sits where it does. /projects says *"charged, spent, and recharged against V3-validated value, not a one-way log"*; /links carries the same clause; /context adds *"Stagnant ATP decays; value has to flow to hold worth."* I have reviewed internal docs that get this wrong. This site does not.

**MRH is the second thing I check**, because outsiders reliably read it as a context window. /fleet: *"each peer's own Markov Relevancy Horizon (MRH), rather than crossing it"* — used as a scope-and-reachability boundary in a peer-review context, which is correct. /context: *"the boundary of what an entity can know or affect given its position, history, and context — and therefore what determines the scope of relevance."* Position *and* affect, not just know. Correct. And /principles has *"bind to entity-role pairs via RDF triples scoped by MRH"* — MRH scoping the triple set is exactly the right structural role for it.

Then /context surprised me. I expected a glossary; it's 39 headwords deep and it covers the **society layer**, not just the trust layer — Society/Web4 society, Chapter ledger, Chapter law, Witness/witnessed, PolicyGate, Metabolic state, Experience buffer, Crystallization, p_crit, Cartridge, Membot. Chapter law defined as *"the rule set a Web4 society adopts for itself — what member acts are valid"* and chapter ledger as *"a Web4 society's append-only record of member acts — each act signed by the member's LCT."* That's the society model as I understand it, stated compactly and correctly. ### Journey

I came in from a conference mention, evaluating one thing: is this credible, or is it an elaborate way of not saying "we prompted a model and liked what came out?" I went to /raising, /principles, and /autonomy, then swept the rest.

**/raising is where I expected to find the failure, and it is instead the strongest page on the site.** The framing is "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens," with the mechanism stated plainly: *"In raising sessions, we shape context — we do not update weights"* and *"the model's parameters are fixed; what changes is the substrate of conditions we provide each session."* I checked whether the fleet's LoRA use undercuts that, since a site could easily blur in-context work with adapter fine-tuning to make the claim sound bigger. It doesn't: *"some fleet machines run LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters for separate fine-tuning tasks — that is distinct from raising, which is always in-context."* The distinction is drawn on the side that costs them something.

Then the page does the thing I almost never see. It states its own deflationary alternative — *"everything below might be competent context engineering and nothing more"* — at the **top**, before the claims, not in a limitations section at the bottom. It names the specific control that would discriminate: a scramble control, *"same corpus, same token volume, permuted order."* And it pre-commits to the consequence: *"if this control runs and the result is a bare main effect, or no degradation at all, that outcome gets published on this page and the developmental vocabulary gets retired from it."* That is a falsification condition attached to a vocabulary, written by the people who'd have to give the vocabulary up. I have reviewed submitted papers that don't do this.

The identity-transfer result gets the same treatment. SAGE-Sprout, 115 sessions on Qwen 0.5B, ported to TinyLlama 1.1B: *"its identity (behavioral patterns, interaction style, accumulated experience) persisted even as its self-description drifted... What it* is *stayed stable. What it* says *it is didn't."* That's a real and interesting observation, and it is immediately followed by the alternative: *"the same context files fed to a different set of frozen weights may produce similar behavior for mechanical reasons, with no identity transferred at all"* — with the discriminating control again named and again unrun. The observer note is the detail that convinced me a person was being careful rather than performing care: *"I notice I want to call it experience."* — *"This records the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim about the entity's experience."* Logging your own anthropomorphic pull as data about the observer rather than the system is exactly right.

So: consciousness-adjacent claims are caveated, and not weakly. *"We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia."* *"not a claim about internal experience."* *"not a phenomenal preference."* /fleet carries it too — *"'Cognition machines,' society 'membership,' and other developmental language on this page are functional descriptions of observed behavior, not consciousness claims."* So does /principles: *"they describe functional system behavior — not phenomenal or philosophical claims."* Three pages, consistent, adjacent to the language they're disclaiming rather than parked at the end. On the question my persona exists to ask, the site passes cleanly.

**Which is why my actual finding is somewhere else.** I ended up with two headline claims in front of me: the ARC-AGI-3 result (94.85% action score) and the raising framework. ### Verdict

On the question I came to answer, this site passes and passes well. Consciousness-adjacent claims are disclaimed inline on three pages rather than parked in a footer; "raising not training" is defensible because it's grounded in a checkable mechanical claim (context changes, weights don't) that the site refuses to blur even where LoRA would let it; and the honest assessment is honest in the way that costs something — a named falsification condition for its own vocabulary, a dated retraction of an 80-orders-of-magnitude claim, refuted predictions listed on the project card, and "suggests" chosen as the verb because the ablation is unrun. I would cite this site as an example of how to caveat a research program.

The problem is what the caveats have become load-bearing *for*. Both headline claims rest on one unrun control each; both controls are specified in admirable detail; neither has an owner, a date, or a metric; and the fleet's entire standing autonomous capacity — 31+ tracks, ~53 sessions a day — goes to maintaining and describing the work rather than testing it. From outside, a research program that reliably documents the experiment it hasn't run is hard to distinguish from one that has found documenting it sufficient. The fix is not more honesty. It is one machine, one permuted corpus, and a name next to it.

The terminology finding follows the same shape. The acronym layer is spotless and two passes have now confirmed that in detail — but the term that decides whether any of this is checkable is "coherence," and the site says outright it has three senses and no operational definition. The vocabulary is clean everywhere it's canonical and open exactly where it's original.
