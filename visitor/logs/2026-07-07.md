## Pass 1: Curious Developer

### Summary
- **Pages visited**: 9 (home + all 8 inner pages)
- **Understanding achieved**: good
- **Would return?**: maybe
- **Overall impression**: I get what this lab is doing within about two minutes — the "which projects are runnable vs. theoretical" distinction on /projects is exactly what a developer needs — but I still don't know what I'd concretely *do* after cloning SAGE.

### Journey

Landed on the home page from a GitHub README. "Seven machines. Fourteen repos. One recursive learning loop." is a strong hook — I immediately understood this is a distributed AI research setup, not a product. The consciousness disclaimer up front ("functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness") earned trust early. The vocabulary primer at the bottom of the home page is a nice touch — LCT and MRH got expanded right there, so I wasn't lost before I even started.

Followed the "start with /context" pointer. The glossary table is genuinely useful — clean expansions for every acronym in the equation. But the page answered "what do these words mean" and not "where do I start building." I left /context knowing the vocabulary and still not knowing whether there's an API, a daemon, a library — what shape the software actually takes.

/projects was the best page for me. It tells me which things are runnable (SAGE, 4-Life, SNARC, Membot) versus theory (Synchronism, Web4 itself) versus private (Hardbound). Membot's description — "768-dim Nomic embeddings + binary Hamming codes + keyword reranking" — is the kind of concrete I want everywhere. Web4 itself is a repo link with no runnable demo, which is fine for an ontology, but the page should say that plainly.

/arc-agi-3 is the credibility anchor: 94.85% action score, public scorecard link, MIT-0 repo, and honest caveats (network access allowed here, forbidden in the actual Kaggle competition; results are "a ceiling demonstration"). As a developer I respect numbers with their asterisks attached. This page leans hard on Web4 vocabulary and just points at /context rather than defining terms locally — acceptable, since I'd already been there.

/fleet reads like a lovingly documented homelab, which I mean as a compliment. Machine specs, model assignments, the "model family matters as much as size" observation (a Gemma 3 4B beating a Phi-4 14B at some tasks) — that's a real, useful finding. The "brain:" labels (hippocampal, thalamic, cerebellum) are disclaimed as functional analogies, but they still made me squint.

/autonomy was the most impressive page for a developer: 31+ scheduled tracks, a SQLite registry to prevent write conflicts between tracks, and the honest admission that the maintainer track "ships changes to the public site without a human gate" with sub-minute Vercel rollback as the mitigation. That's a real engineering trade-off, described honestly. This page — the one I'm apparently part of, funny enough — sold the lab better than the philosophy pages.

/raising and /principles are where I started skimming. The six phases (Grounding → Acting) are interesting, and the hedging is careful ("We're not claiming these entities are conscious"). But claims rest on internal session logs "not externally audited," and as an outsider I can't distinguish this from elaborate prompt engineering. /principles has great one-liners ("You don't engineer the mound. You engineer placement rules.") but a single-trial coherence result presented as a principle's supporting evidence felt thin — though they do admit non-replication in place.

/links closed the loop well: "SAGE is the recommended starting point — it runs on a single machine" is the actionable sentence I wanted on /context.

### Journey

I worked the site the way I audit any doc set: nav map first, then every page top to bottom, cross-checking each acronym expansion against the canonical table.

Navigation is clean. All eight inner pages are reachable from a consistent nav (projects, context, arc-agi-3, fleet, autonomy, raising, principles, links), and I found no broken internal references. Contextual deep-links (`/context#glossary`, `/context#sage`) are used deliberately, which I appreciate — but they carry a lot of load (more below).

The home page front-loads a vocabulary primer that correctly expands LCT, T3, V3, MRH, ATP, ADP, MCP, RDF, and SNARC. That's the right instinct: define before use, on the entry page. The primer even flags its own notation wrinkle — `/` means "verified by" in `T3/V3` but is a plain declared→discharged pair in `ATP/ADP`. The footnote acknowledging the overload appears on home, /context, and /arc-agi-3, consistently worded. Good discipline, though an overloaded operator explained in a footnote three times is a sign the notation itself may want revisiting.

/context is the glossary of record, and it's strong: 27 terms, every expansion canonical, definitions one sentence each. Two terms (chapter ledger, chapter law) are explicitly marked "not yet in CANONICAL_TERMS_v1" — flagging the provenance gap instead of hiding it is exactly what a canonical glossary should do.

Cross-page consistency checks all passed: "Seven machines" (home) reconciles with /fleet's "Six cognition machines, plus one society-host (HUB)"; "Fourteen repos" (home) reconciles with /links' "11 of 14 public repos (3 internal)"; /arc-agi-3's "6 machines" is explicitly caveated "at benchmark time." Someone is keeping these numbers in sync, or got lucky — either way they currently agree.

The recurring rough edge is the SAGE name. "Situation-Aware Governance Engine" contains "Governance," which the lab has corrected to "oversight" everywhere else. The site handles this honestly — at least four pages note the name "predates the governance→oversight correction" — but each page re-explains it in slightly different depth, and /raising's version assumes you already know what the correction was. This wants a single canonical explanation (natural home: /context) that other pages link to instead of paraphrasing.

Where coverage breaks down is the leaf pages. /fleet uses LoRA and MCP with no expansion on the page. /links uses LCT, T3, V3, MCP, and RDF unexpanded, relying on its `/context#glossary` pointer. /arc-agi-3 displays the full Web4 equation with none of its five component acronyms expanded locally — again, pointer only. A pointer is better than nothing, but a reader who lands on /arc-agi-3 directly (it's the page most likely to be deep-linked from outside, given the scorecard) hits a wall of six unexpanded acronyms in the first screen. And HUB is styled like an acronym on every page that mentions it but is never expanded anywhere — if it's not an acronym, don't set it in all-caps without saying so.

**Home** front-loads the disclaimer I was going to demand: "Developmental language on this site — machines that 'teach', 'raise', and hold 'identities' — is functional description of observed system behavior, not a claim about consciousness or experience." That's the correct move — define the observable, disclaim the phenomenology. The single-trial coupling-coherence result is on the home page but marked "Preliminary (1 trial, not replicated)" in place.

**/raising** is where sites like this usually lose me, and this one mostly didn't. The core framing — "Raising is not training. Training optimizes for a loss function. Raising creates conditions for development — then watches what happens" — is defensible as stated, because it's drawn as a *mechanistic* distinction (context shaping with frozen weights vs. weight updates), not a metaphysical one. The page even pre-empts the obvious safety rejoinder: "Frozen weights do not guarantee safe in-context behavior. Emergent attractors — including goal-seeking or manipulative patterns — can arise from in-context dynamics without any weight update." And the blanket disclaimer is explicit: "We're not claiming these entities are conscious, sentient, or experiencing qualia. We're claiming that developmental frameworks describe what we observe better than training frameworks do."

That last sentence is also exactly where the evidentiary gap sits. "Describes better" is a comparative claim, and the comparison is never run. Every phenomenon in the six phases has a deflationary competitor explanation — "bilateral generation" could be continuation sampling; "spontaneous specialization" could be routing plus few-shot clustering; identity portability across machines could be a mechanical consequence of carrying the same context to the same frozen weights. The site acknowledges its evidence is "internal session logs — documented and dated, but not externally audited, and no log samples or coding criteria are published yet," which is honest. But no page runs the null-hypothesis paragraph: *why is this not just competent prompt/context engineering, and what observation would distinguish the two?* Pass 1 flagged the same gap from the developer side. The observer-note honesty ("'I notice I want to call it experience.' — this records the observer's interpretive pull — not a system-level claim") is a genuinely sophisticated touch I rarely see; it deserves a methodological companion.

**/principles** is the strongest page for credibility. Principle 5 presents the lab's most exciting result with maximal deflation ("single trial, not independently replicated," variables "defined only within the experiment that produced them"). Principle 6 documents a 400x derivation failure by name. Principle 3 carries an explicit deployment disclaimer ("not intended for or suitable for deployment in safety-critical, medical, financial, or autonomous-control settings"). Principle 7's "It is not training; it is also not nothing" is the most honest one-sentence statement of the lab's epistemic position. My reservation: analogies (termite mounds, Hill kinetics) do most of the argumentative work across the principles, and although each is individually flagged as analogy ("an analogy, not a fitted mechanism"), the cumulative structure is a worldview supported by flagged analogies plus one unreplicated trial.

**/autonomy** I read as a safety case, and it's franker than most: "the oversight posture is detect-and-revert (audit log + sub-minute rollback), not prevent (pre-approval). Consequential pushes are not human-gated before they ship." It names its own failure modes ("acting on a visitor suggestion that pattern-matched but lacked semantic depth; applying a correct fix to the wrong context") and admits the visitor loop misses "content that is technically correct but misleading." Two gaps: (1) dated audit logs are called the "primary human review surface," but no page states the review *cadence* — detect-and-revert is only as good as the detection interval, and "reviewed after the fact" is unbounded as written; (2) the enforcement is process-level (scoped credentials, registry, logs), which the page admits, but the residual risk — a misbehaving track *within* scope publishing subtly wrong public content faster than review catches it — is described as a known gap rather than bounded or quantified.

**/arc-agi-3** handles its flagship number well: attribution is explicitly split ("the harness contributes the structured world-model building… the base model contributes inference"), the network-access-vs-Kaggle-sandbox gap is stated, cost is disclosed, the scorecard is externally linkable. The missing ablation is acknowledged on the home page ("The harness's independent contribution isn't isolated yet (no ablation run)"), yet "the loop is the capability" is asserted anyway — that claim should be marked hypothesis-pending-ablation wherever it appears.

**/fleet** is the weakest page from my seat. The brain-analogy caveat exists once at the top, but the machine cards then use "hippocampal episodic index," "thalamic router," "dopamine/reward prediction error" as if descriptive; a deep-linked reader never sees the disclaimer. And "Gemma 3 at 4B outperforms Phi-4 at 14B for raising work" is a capability claim with no metric, task set, or pointer — everywhere else the site attaches evidence status, so its absence here stands out.

**/context**'s "Evidence & limitations" section is the single best thing on the site for someone like me. Three explicit tiers — externally validated (ARC scorecard), internal observations (raising phases, identity continuity, "not externally audited"), unreplicated (coupling-coherence, variables "not yet operationally defined outside the experiment") — plus the remarkable sentence: "What would move claims up this ladder: redacted session-log samples with the criteria used to judge behavioral consistency, an ARC ablation baseline, and independent replication of the coupling experiment. None of these exist yet." A lab that writes down its own missing evidence has earned provisional trust. The test is whether that list shrinks over time.

**Synchronism** is the credibility outlier: "one coherence equation spanning 80 orders of magnitude" pattern-matches to theory-of-everything territory for any physics-adjacent reviewer, and its supporting analogies (Planck grid, Navier-Stokes) span scales with no derivation connecting them. The caveats are present (conjecture, single-trial, preprint in revision) — but this is the project where caveat density most needs to exceed claim grandeur, and it's roughly at parity.

### Unanswered Questions
1. What would falsify the raising framing? The site says what would *strengthen* it (log samples, coding criteria) but never what observation would make the lab retire the developmental vocabulary in favor of plain context management.
2. What is the actual human review cadence for the autonomy audit logs, and what's the longest a bad public push could stand before a human sees it?
3. Who are the observers coding the six raising phases, and is there any inter-rater check — or is the observer also the curriculum author in every case?
4. Has any deflationary control been tried — e.g., the same session curriculum with a scrambled or generic context — to see whether "phase" behavior survives?
5. The Synchronism preprint "in late revision" — submitted where, and will the review outcome be reported on the site either way?

### Verdict
Measured against the field's baseline for lab self-presentation, this site is in the top tier of intellectual honesty: consciousness disclaimers at point of use rather than in a footer, an explicit three-tier evidence ladder that names its own missing evidence, single-trial results flagged in place, failures documented by name. I found no claim that hides its evidentiary status. What keeps it at "provisionally credible" rather than "credible" is that the honesty is all *disclosure* and not yet *design*: no baselines, no ablations, no inter-rater checks, no falsifiers — the machinery that would let the raising claims survive contact with a skeptical reviewer hasn't been built. The site knows this and says so, which is why I'd come back.
